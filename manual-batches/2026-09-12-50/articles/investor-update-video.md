A startup investor update video should explain a meaningful change since the previous conversation, show the evidence needed to understand it, and identify the next milestone or request. Pair it with a concise written update so recipients can scan the facts, while using video for a product change that benefits from demonstration.

## Decide whether this update needs a video

Write the update in plain text first. Identify the change, why it matters, what supports it, and what you need from the recipient. If those points are already easy to understand, a video may add little.

Video becomes useful when the change has a sequence: a new review step, a clearer handoff between users, or a product behavior that was previously difficult to explain. Choose one of those changes and let the written update cover the remaining information.

Techstars' investor-update advice includes progress, challenges, and specific asks. A recording can illustrate part of that communication without replacing its written structure. [Techstars' guidance on investor updates](https://www.techstars.com/blog/founder-advice/dont-waste-the-slow-days)

Separate recurring updates from answers to a single investor's question. A recurring update needs shared context about the period. A targeted reply can begin with the specific question and move directly to the evidence. Do not produce both versions unless the audience needs them.

## Establish a comparable starting point

An update describes a change, so the previous state must be clear. Write down what you told the recipient last time, including the product version and any commitment you made. This prevents a general progress story from drifting away from the actual conversation.

For a fictional equipment-inspection startup, the previous update might have shown inspectors submitting reports but leaving managers to assign follow-up work elsewhere. The new recording could demonstrate an assignment step inside the product. That is a concrete change in behavior; it is not automatically evidence of improved operational results.

If you show a metric, use the same definition and reporting period as the comparison, or explain why they differ. When a definition changes, acknowledge it in the written update rather than letting the visual imply a like-for-like comparison.

Avoid recycling an old product demonstration with a new date. If the relevant workflow has not changed, link the earlier recording and explain the new information in text. That keeps the recipient's attention on what is actually different.

## Build a change-and-evidence worksheet

Use this original worksheet before recording. Its purpose is to keep the update specific enough that a recipient can act on it.

| Field | Prompt | Fictional inspection-product example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Previous state | What did the recipient understand before? | Reports were submitted; assignment happened elsewhere |
| Current change | What is different now? | A manager can assign follow-up work from the report |
| Evidence shown | What can the recording establish? | The task is created with an owner and visible status |
| Remaining limit | What does this not establish? | Adoption and operational impact still need evaluation |
| Next milestone | What will the team examine next? | Whether managers complete the assignment workflow |
| Recipient request | What concrete help is relevant? | An introduction to someone who manages inspection teams |

Keep a separate row for each proposed change. Choose the one whose evidence benefits most from motion or narration. Combine changes only when they are part of the same task and the resulting sequence remains easy to follow.

A useful worksheet also records the evidence owner and the date checked. This gives the person recording the update a clear place to resolve uncertainty before making a claim on camera.

## Show the change without overclaiming its importance

Open with the difference: “Last time we showed report submission. This update shows how a manager assigns the follow-up work.” Then show the relevant screen and explain the user's decision.

Spend more time on the new behavior than on familiar setup. You can briefly identify a prepared sample report without repeating the full login and navigation sequence. Preserve any setup condition that affects what viewers should infer.

After the demonstration, say what remains to be learned. A working feature may still need usability evaluation, adoption evidence, or a clearer buyer. Naming that next question makes the update useful for discussion.

If progress is slower than expected, state the changed plan plainly. Explain the obstacle, the decision you made, and the next piece of evidence you intend to gather. A video is optional here; a concise written explanation may make a complicated change easier to inspect.

End the recording when the change and its boundary are clear. Avoid attaching a full fundraising pitch to every product update.

## Make the request easy to answer or forward

A broad request for “feedback” leaves the recipient to invent the task. Ask for a particular kind of response: whether the workflow matches a known buyer process, an introduction to an appropriate operator, or a conversation about a defined uncertainty.

Give an introduction request enough context to forward. State the role you want to meet, the question you hope to explore, and a short description of the company. Avoid implying that the recipient has agreed to make an introduction.

Keep confidential details in a version intended for that audience. Before encouraging forwarding, check whether the update includes customer identities, internal operating figures, or unreleased information. A concise public product explanation may be suitable for forwarding even when the full investor update is not.

Set a sensible response path. The recipient should be able to reply directly or use a clearly identified contact route. A video that ends with a request but provides no usable way to answer creates unnecessary work.

## Package the update for reading and watching

Include a short written summary above the recording link: reporting period, main change, remaining challenge, and request. Use a descriptive link label so recipients know what watching will answer.

W3C recommends planning captions and transcripts alongside other media accessibility needs. For an investor update, a written explanation of the product change can also preserve context outside the player. [W3C's planning guidance for audio and video](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/planning/)

Check the recording against the written update. Product status, dates, metric definitions, and the next milestone should agree. Make the version date visible in the surrounding message or asset description so an old forwarded link does not appear current.

Maintain a small update log with the date sent, audience, asset version, request, and responses received. Use replies to identify follow-up work. Do not interpret a view count as investor conviction or claim that the recording caused a financing outcome.

For the next update, start from the commitments in this one. Close the loop on what changed, what did not, and what you learned. That continuity gives the communication a purpose beyond staying visible.
