A customer onboarding video should help a new user achieve one useful outcome in the product. State the starting conditions, demonstrate the essential actions, and show how to recognize success. Give viewers a recovery path when their screen differs. Build the video around their first task rather than a complete feature tour.

## Choose an outcome the customer recognizes

Define the first useful outcome in the customer's language. “Create a workspace” describes a product action. “Prepare a shared place where your team can review its first request” describes why the action matters. Either may be appropriate, but the video should make the connection explicit.

Talk to the people handling onboarding and support. Ask which task new customers attempt, where they hesitate, and what they expect to see afterward. Use actual task observations to select the lesson. A frequently discussed feature may still be irrelevant to the user's immediate goal.

Google's technical-writing guidance recommends identifying the audience's role, existing knowledge, and learning needs. [Google's audience guidance](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience). Apply that distinction to onboarding: an administrator setting up an account and an invited teammate completing a request may need different starting points.

My suggested scope test is simple: can a viewer finish the video and perform one meaningful task without needing an unrelated lesson? If not, narrow the outcome or make the prerequisite visible before playback.

## Write the starting conditions explicitly

Document the account state used in the recording. Include the role, access level, example data, and any preparation the viewer must complete. These details belong near the beginning when they determine whether the instructions work.

Avoid silently performing setup before the recording. If the demonstration starts with an imported file, explain where that file comes from. If only an administrator can complete the action, identify the intended role rather than letting other users search for a control they cannot access.

Use a safe example that resembles the task without exposing private customer information. Give the example a clear purpose: a sample request, sample project, or demonstration document. Keep its identity consistent throughout the video so the viewer can follow the change.

Write a short “before you start” line and test it with someone new to the workflow. If the line needs a long explanation, consider a separate setup guide. The main onboarding lesson should not hide its real requirements behind a cheerful welcome.

## Build an outcome storyboard

The following original storyboard is a fictional example for an invented request-management product. It shows an editorial structure, not VideoClaw functionality or a customer success story.

| Scene | Viewer question | What to show | Confirmation or help |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | What will I accomplish? | One request with an assigned owner | State the outcome in plain language |
| Preparation | Am I in the right place? | Sample workspace and intended role | Name required access |
| Action | What do I do first? | Open the sample request | Identify the request by name |
| Decision | Which owner should I choose? | Available team members | Explain the choice criterion |
| Completion | Did it work? | Saved owner and updated request | Point out the confirmation |
| Recovery | What if my screen differs? | Location of relevant help | Explain how to continue or ask for access |

Write narration only after the scene sequence is clear. This makes it easier to remove a feature explanation that does not help the outcome. Keep the demonstration visible while naming the action; switching to a presenter for every instruction may obscure the step the viewer needs to imitate.

End with a concrete task for the customer to try. “Assign an owner to your sample request” is more useful here than a broad invitation to explore everything.

## Show the action and its consequence

Microsoft's instruction-writing guidance recommends separate steps for instructions, locating the action before describing it, and including actions that complete the procedure. [Microsoft's step-by-step instruction guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/procedures-instructions/writing-step-by-step-instructions). For video, use that as a basis for keeping narration aligned with the actual screen sequence.

Make the consequence visible after an action. A selected menu item is not necessarily a saved change. Show the resulting state and explain how it confirms success. If the workflow has a delay, tell the viewer what they are waiting for and how to recognize completion.

For the fictional request lesson, keep the request name on screen while the owner changes. That helps a new viewer connect the starting object with the result. Do not jump to a different request merely because it provides a cleaner picture.

Use spoken labels that match the visible interface. An internal nickname may be familiar to the presenter and confusing to the customer. When a label has changed, update the recording or clearly identify the version the lesson covers.

## Design a useful recovery path

List the likely reasons a viewer cannot follow the happy path. They may lack access, have no example data, encounter a different account state, or see a changed interface. Choose the most relevant issues for the lesson rather than trying to include every possible exception.

Place a brief recovery instruction at the point of confusion. If the owner list is empty, explain who manages membership or where the relevant help lives. Do not wait until the closing frame to acknowledge a barrier encountered halfway through.

Keep a written companion with the starting conditions, main steps, confirmation, and help route. It gives the viewer a place to resume without scrubbing through the recording repeatedly. Review this companion with the video so the instructions do not drift apart.

A good recovery path also helps the team diagnose feedback. Ask users to identify the step, visible state, and expected outcome. That information is more actionable than “the onboarding video didn't work,” and it can reveal whether the issue is content, access, or the product itself.

## Test completion and plan maintenance

Invite someone who resembles the intended user to try the task in a safe environment. Provide the video and the normal supporting material, then observe without narrating over the gaps. Record where they pause, backtrack, seek help, or misinterpret the confirmation.

Use an original test sheet with five fields: starting conditions, step reached, difficulty observed, assistance required, and final outcome. Keep observed behavior separate from your interpretation. “Opened the wrong menu twice” is an observation; “the label is confusing” is a hypothesis to investigate.

Revise the most consequential barrier first. A missing prerequisite can prevent the entire lesson from working, while a less polished transition may have no effect on task completion. Repeat the affected part of the task after the revision.

Assign a maintenance owner and refresh triggers. Relevant triggers include a changed navigation path, renamed control, new permission model, or repeated support confusion at the same step. Record the product version or verification date internally so future reviewers know what was checked.

Measure the lesson by its assigned job: whether the intended customer can reach the stated outcome with appropriate help. Playback counts can show exposure, but they cannot by themselves establish that a user completed the task.
