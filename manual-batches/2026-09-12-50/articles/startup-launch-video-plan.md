A startup launch video should help a specific viewer understand the problem, see one current product workflow, and choose a clear next step. Plan the demonstration before polishing the script, show the conditions that matter, and test whether someone unfamiliar with the product can explain what they just watched.

## Choose the launch promise you can actually show

Write a plain statement of what is becoming available and to whom. A new product, a limited pilot, and an additional feature are different announcements. The video should make that distinction before inviting someone to act.

Next, name one task the viewer wants to finish. “Coordinate a supplier review” is still broad. “See which document is blocking a supplier’s approval” gives you a specific opening screen, action, and result. The narrower task becomes the backbone of the demonstration.

In an account published by YC, David Mack describes using early internal demonstrations to gather specific feedback before work becomes elaborate. Apply that idea to the launch video by reviewing a rough screen sequence before investing in visual polish. This is an adaptation of his process, not evidence that a particular launch style will succeed. [YC’s account of shipping early and often](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/tips-ship-early-and-often).

If you cannot show the promised task in the available product, change the promise or clearly present the material as a concept. Animation can explain an idea, but it should not quietly stand in for working functionality.

## Work backward from the demonstration’s final frame

Identify the last useful state the audience needs to see. It might be a completed request, a clearly assigned owner, or a comparison ready for review. Then determine the smallest sequence that explains how the product reached it.

List the required starting conditions. Does the user need imported data, a configured workspace, or a particular permission? You do not need to film every setup step, but the narration or nearby explanation should acknowledge the prerequisites that affect the viewer’s decision.

Now choose the opening frame. If it shows a crowded dashboard, the viewer may spend the first moments figuring out where to look. Start closer to the task and provide only enough context to orient them.

Write the supporting sentence after you have the screen sequence. That order prevents a persuasive script from committing the editor to a demonstration the team cannot record. It also exposes missing transitions while they are inexpensive to fix.

## Build a storyboard that separates speech from evidence

Use this fictional storyboard for a startup launching a document-review workflow. Rehearse the sequence to decide how long viewers need to understand each beat.

| Beat | Spoken purpose | What the viewer sees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | Identify a blocked supplier review | Sample supplier record with an incomplete item |
| Product introduction | Explain where the review information lives | Current product screen and clear product name |
| Action | Show the reviewer locating the missing evidence | A deliberate navigation to the relevant document |
| Outcome | Explain who handles the next step | Owner and status visible together |
| Boundary | State who can access the release today | Accurate availability label |
| Invitation | Direct interested viewers to a relevant walkthrough | One destination with a clear description |

Give each beat a pass condition. For example, the action passes when a new viewer can identify which document is missing. A beautiful screen recording that fails that test still needs revision.

Keep a separate shot list for capture logistics: cursor position, sample record, screen size, and the take to use. The storyboard explains the argument. The shot list makes it possible to film that argument consistently.

## Match the presentation style to the explanation

A founder introduction helps when the viewer needs context about the problem or the release. A screen recording helps when the behavior itself carries the explanation. A simple diagram helps when relationships between people, data, or systems are difficult to see in the interface.

Use only the styles the story requires. Each transition between face, diagram, and product asks the audience to reorient. If the founder repeats what the screen already makes obvious, shorten the spoken section. If the interface hides an important relationship, add a diagram with the same labels used in the product.

Choose a provisional duration by reading the script at a natural pace while performing the actions. Leave room for someone to inspect the result. Speeding up the whole recording to meet an arbitrary duration can make the most important evidence unreadable.

Keep music secondary to speech. Before adding visual effects, check whether the core explanation works as a simple sequence of narration and screens. That version gives the team a useful baseline for judging later additions.

## Plan accessible explanation during production

W3C’s media-planning guidance treats captions, description of meaningful visuals, transcripts, and the media player as parts of production planning. It recommends assigning these responsibilities while the video is being developed. [W3C’s guide to planning audio and video](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/planning/).

For a software launch, write narration that identifies meaningful changes: the record moves to a review state, a particular owner appears, or the user receives a confirmation. “As you can see” does not explain the event to someone who cannot inspect the screen.

Prepare captions from the final spoken version and check technical vocabulary manually. Leave visual space around the important controls so captions do not cover the evidence. A larger crop is often a more useful editorial choice than a long explanation of tiny interface labels.

Provide a text explanation near the video that includes prerequisites and availability. Treat a silent viewing pass and an audio-only pass as diagnostic checks; neither alone establishes that all accessibility needs have been met. Fix the specific missing information each pass reveals.

## Run a comprehension review before announcing

Show the rough cut to someone who understands the customer’s work but has not helped make the video. Ask them to describe the user, the task, the product action, and the next step. Let them answer before supplying explanations.

Record misunderstandings in terms of their cause. If they confuse a supplier with the reviewer, the opening needs clearer roles. If they assume the product performs an action handled by a person, the transition needs a more precise explanation.

Then review the claims against the recording. Does the screen show the outcome named in the narration? Does an edit skip a step that changes the apparent effort? Are current and planned features visually distinguishable?

Finish with a destination check. The linked page should match the offer in the closing line. Someone invited to explore a limited trial should not arrive at an unrelated generic contact form with no explanation. Make the next step understandable even if they never replay the video.

## Release a useful version and prepare its successor

Keep the approved script, capture date, product version, and final asset together. Assign a review trigger such as a changed interface, revised availability, or altered onboarding process. A launch video needs maintenance whenever its explanation stops matching the product.

Plan one follow-up based on the question the main film deliberately leaves open. A detailed setup tutorial or a walkthrough of an exception can serve interested viewers without making the launch piece carry every use case.
