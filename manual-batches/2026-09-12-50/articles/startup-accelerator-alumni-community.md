A startup accelerator alumni community becomes useful when founders can find relevant experience, ask a bounded question, and apply what another founder learned. Use video to preserve explanations worth revisiting, then connect each recording to discussion and follow-up. Organize the library around member problems rather than an endless stream of announcements.

## Start with the exchange members need

Ask alumni what they are trying to do now. Their needs may have changed since the cohort: hiring a first manager, navigating a customer implementation, preparing a product explanation, or rebuilding a sales process. Select one recurring problem for an initial community exchange.

An alumni program can extend beyond graduation. YC describes ongoing office hours for past cohorts and founder communities organized around shared interests. [What Happens at YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/about). That is one program's description, not a promise about what any alumni network will provide.

For your community, state the exchange plainly. A founder brings a question; another shares relevant experience; the group discusses how the situations differ; the question owner tries a next step. A video can preserve part of that exchange, but it needs a reason to exist beyond filling the resource library.

Interview members who rarely attend as well as the regular participants. Someone may need a written answer, a different session time, or a more narrowly defined topic. Those needs should influence the format before the team assumes that another live event will help.

## Match experience to a specific question

Create a request form with the situation, the decision, what the founder has already tried, and the kind of experience they want. Ask for a question that can be discussed without exposing unnecessary company detail.

A request for “growth advice” is difficult to route. A request to understand how another founder introduced an implementation handoff gives the coordinator a better starting point. Match the question to someone who has worked through a comparable situation, then ask whether they are willing and available to contribute.

Do not frame the contributor as a universal expert. Introduce the relevant experience and its context. A founder who built a process for a small services team may have something useful to share with a software company, but the differences deserve attention.

Offer a bounded contribution: a short conversation, a recorded walkthrough of an approved example, or written comments on a specific draft. Tell contributors how much preparation is expected and whether the material will be kept inside the community. A manageable request respects the fact that alumni are running their own companies.

## Use a request-to-lesson worksheet

This original worksheet helps a coordinator turn one member need into a reusable exchange. The example is fictional and includes no reported performance results.

| Step | Record this decision | Fictional example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Member question | The immediate choice | How should a small team hand a signed customer to implementation? |
| Relevant experience | Why this contributor can help | An alumnus recently documented the same transition |
| Shared material | What can be shown | A blank handoff checklist with no customer details |
| Conversation boundary | What remains outside the session | Individual customer terms and internal staffing issues |
| Reusable lesson | What another founder can learn | Which questions a handoff owner should answer |
| Practice | What the question owner will try | Draft a handoff note for an invented customer |
| Follow-up | What the coordinator will ask | Which fields were useful and which were missing? |

Before recording, read the member question to the contributor. Ask which part they can answer from experience and which part would require a different specialist. Narrow the session accordingly.

The worksheet also gives the coordinator a reason to decline recording. Some conversations are useful precisely because participants can explore uncertainty privately. A brief, separately approved summary may be the appropriate reusable output.

## Record an explanation that preserves the context

Ask the contributor to explain the situation before presenting the tool. What team was involved? What problem prompted the change? Which assumptions shaped the approach? Use approved or fictional material where a real example cannot be shared.

Then walk through the reasoning. For the fictional handoff lesson, the founder might explain why the checklist includes an owner, a promised next step, and an unresolved question. Invite the question owner to say where their own circumstances differ.

The work of Wenger-Trayner describes communities of practice through a shared domain, regular interaction, and a developing shared practice. [Introduction to Communities of Practice](https://www.wenger-trayner.com/introduction-to-communities-of-practice/). A practical implication for this program is to pair the recorded explanation with an opportunity to discuss and adapt it.

Preserve caveats when editing. An excerpt should not turn “this helped us with our first implementations” into a rule for all companies. Ask the contributor to review the title as well as the recording.

Make the lesson accessible through reviewed captions and a written explanation of essential visuals. For a distributed community, include an asynchronous way to ask a follow-up so the conversation does not end when the live session does.

## Make the library searchable by problem

Give every lesson a clear entry: problem addressed, contributor's relevant experience, company stage at the time, recording date, supporting template, intended audience, and review date. Use descriptive titles that a founder might search for when the need arises.

A title such as “Preparing the first implementation handoff” is more useful for retrieval than “Alumni spotlight number twelve.” Keep the founder's name visible in the entry, but let the problem explain why someone should open it.

Group material into a few categories based on actual member requests. Avoid creating dozens of empty categories in anticipation of future content. When two lessons cover similar ground, explain their different contexts rather than forcing them into one definitive answer.

Assign a resource owner who can receive corrections and ask the contributor whether the advice remains current. When a tool or assumption changes, add an update, replace the lesson, or retire it. An old recording should not silently become permanent guidance.

Be clear about access. A lesson approved for community members should remain within that intended setting unless a new use is agreed. Keep private discussion notes outside the public-facing library entry.

## Evaluate contribution through useful follow-up

After an exchange, ask the question owner what they tried and what remained unclear. Ask the contributor whether the scope and preparation felt reasonable. These answers help improve the next match.

Record requests received, matches made, exchanges completed, and follow-ups returned with the relevant time period. Keep missing follow-up visible. Silence does not establish that a lesson worked or failed.

Look for recurring requests that justify a workshop or a written resource. If several founders struggle to prepare a customer explanation, organize a practice session. If one lesson repeatedly prompts the same clarification, revise its introduction or worksheet.

Rotate contribution opportunities across stages, sectors, locations, and participation styles. The community should make room for a thoughtful remote contributor as well as a familiar event speaker. Invite people because their experience fits the question.
