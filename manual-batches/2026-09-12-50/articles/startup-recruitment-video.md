To make a recruitment video, show candidates the work they would do, the people they would work with, and the conditions that shape the role. Choose one hiring need, record concrete examples from the team, explain the difficult parts honestly, and direct viewers to an accurate job description and application route.

## Start with one real role

A company-wide culture film can introduce a startup, but a role-specific video can answer a more immediate question: “Would I want to do this work with this team?” Choose an open role with a confirmed manager, responsibilities, and application route.

Describe the role through a recurring task. For an early customer operations hire, that might be investigating incomplete requests and coordinating the next response. For an engineer, it might be turning an ambiguous user problem into a tested product change.

The US Office of Personnel Management describes realistic job previews as tools that explain favorable and difficult aspects of a job so candidates can assess their own fit. Its guidance concerns government hiring, but the communication principle is useful for a startup video. It does not establish a hiring outcome for your company. [OPM’s realistic job preview guidance](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/realistic-job-previews/).

Write down what a candidate is likely to misunderstand. Perhaps they expect a mature process, while the role includes creating one. Make that difference visible early.

## Translate the job description into scenes

Review the written role with the hiring manager and someone who does adjacent work. Identify three scenes that show responsibilities, collaboration, and a meaningful constraint.

Responsibilities should appear as actions. Instead of saying the employee will have impact, show the kind of decision they will own. Instead of claiming a collaborative culture, show how a proposal is reviewed and who can challenge it.

Be precise about conditions that matter to the role. Explain the work model, relevant coordination requirements, and any known operational responsibilities through the approved role details. Keep those details on the job page so candidates can inspect them without replaying the film.

Avoid filming an exceptional social event as if it represents a normal workday. Perks can be included when accurate and relevant, but they should not displace the information someone needs to judge the job.

If the team has not agreed on a material responsibility, resolve that before scripting. A recruitment video can expose an unclear role; it cannot fix that uncertainty through confident narration.

## Use this role-to-scene worksheet

The following fictional example concerns an early customer operations role at an unnamed software startup. It illustrates a planning method, not an open job or a hiring promise.

| Candidate question | Scene to record | Detail to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What would I work on? | A teammate explains a sample unresolved request | The task belongs to this role |
| What would I own? | The manager identifies the next decision and its boundary | Authority matches the job description |
| How do people collaborate? | A staged walkthrough of a normal handoff | Participants explain their actual responsibilities |
| What is difficult? | A teammate describes incomplete information and changing priorities | The example is representative |
| What support exists? | The manager shows where questions and reviews happen | Support is available today |
| How do I apply? | A clear closing card and spoken direction | Job page and application route work |

Label any reconstructed workflow as illustrative when viewers might otherwise think it is a live customer event. Use sample data to explain the work without exposing a customer or employee record.

After completing the worksheet, ask the hiring manager which scene would most influence a candidate’s decision. Give that scene enough time. It should not become a blink-and-you-miss-it moment beneath music.

## Interview the team about actual work

Ask the founder why the role exists now. Ask the manager what the person will need to learn and what decisions they will make. Ask a teammate what a new hire would be surprised to discover.

Useful prompts include: “Describe a task you handled this week,” “Where did you need another person’s judgment?” and “What is still being built in our process?” These prompts invite explanations that candidates can evaluate.

Give participants the option to contribute in a format they are comfortable with. Someone can explain a screen or help prepare an accurate example without appearing in the final film. Do not treat on-camera enthusiasm as a test of commitment.

Record the hard part with the same care as the positive part. If the role involves uncertainty, explain where that uncertainty comes from and what support exists. Avoid turning an unresolved operational problem into a glamorous slogan.

Ask the manager to distinguish intended improvements from current conditions. A plan to add mentorship later should not sound like an established program. Candidates need a clear picture of what joining now would mean.

## Keep the video accessible and easy to inspect

W3C recommends planning media accessibility during production, including captions, meaningful visual description, transcripts, and player support. Assign those tasks before the edit is considered finished. [W3C’s audio and video planning guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/planning/).

For a recruitment film, important information often appears in name cards, role labels, or screenshots. Make sure the spoken explanation or accompanying text carries the information a candidate needs. A silent card listing a work location should not be the only place that condition appears.

Check captions against people’s names, job titles, and team vocabulary. Keep the soundtrack low enough that speech remains understandable. Preview the film on a small screen to see whether the task examples can actually be read.

Place a written summary and the job link near the video. Candidates should be able to inspect responsibilities and the application process at their own pace. The film should help them understand the role, not become a mandatory obstacle to finding its details.

## Review for expectation gaps before release

Ask a colleague outside the hiring team to watch and describe what the person would do in a typical week. Compare that account with the approved role. If they imagine a different level of authority or a more mature process, identify the sentence or scene that created the expectation.

Then ask the hiring manager to confirm current conditions and the employee participants to check their own representations. Review any company-specific employment wording through the organization’s normal process. Keep the exercise focused on accurate information rather than making legal conclusions from a video checklist.

Test the application route from outside the company account. Confirm the role is still open, the title matches, and candidates can reach the complete description. Assign a person to update the film’s placement when the role closes.

Do not use viewing behavior as a proxy for candidate quality. The purpose of this asset is to supply useful information. Someone who reads the written description instead may be making an equally informed decision.

## Learn from candidate questions

During the hiring process, note which questions remain after people have seen the material. Repeated uncertainty about responsibilities suggests the examples need more detail. Questions about the work model may belong in a clearer written section as well as the video.

Ask new teammates, when appropriate, whether the preview matched the work they encountered. Treat their answers as input for revision, not as proof of a retention effect. The most valuable correction may be a small condition that the original team took for granted.
