Outsource video editing with a complete source package, a clear brief, one feedback owner, and an agreed final handoff. Confirm that the editor can open the materials before scheduling the first draft. Keep product accuracy and release approval assigned internally, and test that another operator can use the delivered project materials.

## Define the handoff before transferring footage

Begin by separating the work you are delegating from the decisions you still own. An outside editor may assemble the story, improve pacing, and prepare requested versions. Your team still needs someone who can explain product behavior, approve source use, and decide whether the result is ready.

Write a short task statement: audience, viewer question, expected output, sources supplied, and open questions. Include a realistic first delivery rather than an entire future content program. A narrow first assignment makes missing context easier to identify.

Ask the editor which inputs are required to begin. They may need original footage, separate audio, a transcript, a script, or selected moments, depending on the agreed scope. Do not assume a folder link means the source package is complete.

Set an intake checkpoint. The editor confirms that the materials are accessible and identifies anything missing or unsuitable. The production schedule should reflect source readiness, not only the day you first contacted the editor.

## Package sources so another person can understand them

Organize the package by purpose and status. A simple structure might include brief, source video, audio, graphics, references, and decisions. Use names that make sense outside your laptop and avoid mixing obsolete and approved assets without labels.

Create a source manifest. Each row should identify the file, its role in the project, relevant notes, and the internal owner for questions. If a transcript contains corrected spellings or product terms, identify the approved version.

Use this original intake checklist before the first transfer:

| Item | What the editor should receive | Acceptance check |
|---|---|---|
| Brief | Audience, message, output, exclusions | Editor can restate the assignment |
| Footage | Intended source files and relevant takes | Files open and play |
| Audio | Voice tracks and sync notes where needed | Speech can be matched to footage |
| Product reference | Current workflow and terminology | Factual questions have an owner |
| Brand assets | Approved identity and usage examples | Current assets are distinguishable |
| Source-use notes | Allowed material and restrictions | Reference-only files are labeled |
| Review plan | Version naming, approver, feedback window | First review appointment is feasible |

Include a short orientation note for complex material. Explain what the folders contain and which recording should be used first. Do not bury the only explanation in a long message history the editor cannot access.

## Share only the material needed for this assignment

Review the package for customer details, private conversations, and unreleased screens that are irrelevant to the job. Replace or remove unnecessary sensitive material before transfer. FTC guidance recommends limiting service-provider access to information required for the work and checking that agreed safeguards are followed. [FTC access and provider guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/start-security-guide-business).

Assign access to named people through the company's approved sharing process. Confirm who else will work on the files and whether additional services will receive them. Bring the answer to the person responsible for your company's data handling when the material needs special treatment.

Keep source-use decisions explicit. “Useful background” does not necessarily mean “may appear in the final video.” Mark those distinctions at the asset level so an editor does not have to infer them from a general confidentiality statement.

Agree on where working files and final materials will live and what happens at the end of the assignment. Use the company's actual retention needs and policies. A handoff should not rely on an assumption that either party will keep every file forever.

## Turn the first draft into an onboarding test

Choose a representative first assignment. Supply the same kind of recordings and decisions the editor will receive later. A specially prepared showcase job can hide the intake problems that recur during ordinary production.

Ask the editor to return questions with the rough cut. Distinguish questions caused by missing company context from decisions you expected the editor to make. Use the answers to improve the handoff rather than treating every question as a failure.

Consolidate feedback under one owner. Include the current version, timecodes, required outcomes, and the reason for each correction. Give the editor a single decision when reviewers disagree. The first correction round is part of learning how the relationship will work.

In a fictional example, startup CaseHarbor outsources a short product lesson. Its recording shows two similar status labels, but the source package never explains which is current. The editor flags the ambiguity. The product owner supplies the answer and adds a terminology sheet to future packages.

That fictional correction improves the input boundary. Hiring another editor without changing the package would leave the same ambiguity unresolved.

## Require a usable project delivery, not just a file transfer

Agree on the final package before work starts. Depending on the scope, it may include finished videos, captions, editable project files, linked media, graphics, and a note explaining outstanding dependencies. Ask the recipient to confirm what they can use.

Adobe documents project consolidation that retains used portions of source footage, with options affecting unused clips and handles. A consolidated project should therefore not be assumed to contain the entire original recording archive. [Adobe project consolidation guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/organize-media/create-projects/consolidate-and-archive-projects.html).

Discuss which material your company needs for future changes. A trimmed delivery may support reproducing the current edit while leaving too little surrounding footage for a new story. Decide whether that is sufficient or whether original sources must also be handed over or retained separately.

Record the software version and dependencies needed to reopen the work. Identify fonts, graphics, effects, and linked compositions that require separate arrangements. If the handoff consists only of finished exports, make that boundary explicit in the accepted scope.

Have another authorized operator open the delivered project materials and attempt one small, agreed correction. Record missing dependencies and resolve them while the original editor still has context. Do not declare the handoff complete merely because an upload finished.

## Maintain continuity as the relationship grows

Keep a company-owned index of current projects, approved versions, source locations, open decisions, and responsible people. Update it when each assignment closes. The index should help someone who did not attend the review meetings find the right material.

Create a simple request routine for later changes. Identify the approved version, describe the new requirement, and ask for its effect on time and scope. Avoid asking the editor to reconstruct the request from a published link with no project context.

At a partner change, transfer current materials and decisions before removing access that is still needed for an agreed handoff. Then update permissions through the company owner and confirm the new operator can continue. Keep the sequence consistent with your actual company policy.

Use the first several jobs to improve the source pack. Add recurring questions to the brief, clarify ambiguous asset labels, and remove materials nobody needs. The outcome should be a handoff that becomes easier to repeat because the inputs are clearer.
