A demo day pitch should make one company easy to understand: who has the problem, what the product changes, what evidence exists, and why the opportunity merits another conversation. Build those connections before designing slides, then rehearse against the actual event limit and remove anything that interrupts the story.

## Decide what the audience should understand

Start with a sentence an investor could repeat to a colleague without your help. Name the user, the work, and the product's role. “We help maintenance managers turn inspection notes into assigned repair work” creates a clearer picture than a category label followed by several adjectives.

YC frames Demo Day as an opportunity to earn a subsequent meeting. That is a useful boundary for deciding how much to explain in the room. [YC's guide to Demo Day presentations](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/)

Write the question you want that meeting to explore. It might concern whether a workflow can expand across locations, how a buyer adopts the product, or what the team has learned from early use. This gives the pitch a destination. Without it, you can end up with an introduction that describes the product accurately but gives nobody a reason to continue.

Keep the event's required content and time limit beside the outline. They determine what must fit; another accelerator's example does not set your constraints.

## Connect the problem to an observable change

Use a small incident to introduce the problem, then show how the product changes the work. The incident should belong to your customer research or be explicitly labeled as an illustration. Avoid presenting an invented customer story as a testimonial.

For a fictional maintenance startup, the incident might be a supervisor receiving inspection notes but having no clear repair owner. The product sequence could show a note becoming a task with an assignee and a due date. The audience can now connect the problem to a specific action.

Next explain why this action matters to the business. A completed demonstration establishes that the depicted workflow functions under the shown conditions. It does not establish customer willingness to pay, broad adoption, or measured savings. Those require their own evidence.

Put a short transition between these ideas: “This is the workflow; here is what we have learned about its use.” That sentence prevents a product screen from carrying a business claim it cannot support.

## Build the story on a worksheet before making slides

Use the following original worksheet to find missing connections. Complete the evidence column before deciding which visual to make.

| Story element | Founder prompt | Evidence or material to prepare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| User and situation | Who is trying to complete which job? | Approved interview observation or clearly labeled illustration |
| Existing obstacle | Where does the work stop or become difficult? | Specific current process, without exaggerated frequency |
| Product action | What does the user do differently? | Current screen sequence or physical demonstration |
| Business relevance | Who buys, and what decision makes adoption possible? | Known buying process; assumptions marked separately |
| Progress | What has changed since the previous milestone? | Dated evidence with a clear definition |
| Next conversation | What should the interested listener explore with us? | One concrete follow-up topic and contact route |

Read only the middle column aloud after filling it in. If the answers sound like unrelated facts, repair the transitions. If the evidence column is mostly empty, narrow the statements instead of adding visual decoration.

Then give every proposed slide a job. A slide should either clarify an unfamiliar idea, display evidence, or make the next step easy to remember. Material that only repeats the spoken sentence may work better as a speaker note.

## Explain the opportunity without disguising assumptions

The company story needs more than a useful feature. Explain who might buy it, which alternatives they use, and why your approach could become a business. Sequoia's business-plan guidance includes customer problems, market potential, alternatives, and the business model among its prompts. [Sequoia's guide to writing a business plan](https://sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan)

Make your own argument concrete by separating three statements: what you know, what you currently believe, and what you intend to test next. For example, you may know which role approved a pilot while still testing whether that role owns the recurring budget. Saying so makes the buying-path discussion more precise.

If you include market calculations, keep the inputs visible and label estimates. Do not let an attractive total conceal an uncertain definition of the customer. A broad industry category may include organizations you cannot serve with the current product.

Use the team section to explain a relevant advantage in doing this work. A specific responsibility, experience, or technical contribution is more informative than an uninterrupted list of impressive affiliations.

## Make the visuals carry a clear part of the argument

Kevin Hale's deck-design guidance emphasizes readable slides and a single idea per slide. Use that as a starting point for testing the visuals, rather than copying another company's slide count. [YC's guide to designing a better pitch deck](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck/)

For the maintenance example, a screenshot full of menus asks the audience to discover the important action. A tightly framed task record, paired with a plain headline about assigning ownership, directs attention to the point being discussed. Keep enough surrounding information to make the action understandable.

Check every chart title against what the underlying data measures. “Active locations” and “locations invited” describe different things. Include the period and definition needed to interpret a number, even if the full calculation belongs in follow-up material.

Plan the handoff between your voice and the screen. Pause when a viewer needs to inspect evidence. Resume with the implication, rather than reading every label aloud. If a visual needs a lengthy tutorial, simplify it or reserve it for the meeting.

## Rehearse the connections and prepare the handoff

Record one complete attempt using the actual deck and speaking position. Ask a listener to identify the customer, product action, business opportunity, and next step without looking at the slides again. Their answers show which connections survived the presentation.

When an answer is wrong, find the earliest point where the listener could have formed that interpretation. Fix that sentence or transition first. Adding a late explanation often leaves the confusing opening intact.

Next rehearse a shorter route through the same argument. Mark a supporting example you can omit if time is reduced, while preserving the company description, central evidence, and ending. Do not make the fallback version depend on speaking faster.

Finally, prepare a follow-up asset that continues the story. Label it with a date and make sure its product description and metrics match the pitch. The live presentation should lead naturally to this material, with a clear invitation to discuss the question you chose at the start.
