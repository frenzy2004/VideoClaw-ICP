A startup demo video should show one valuable workflow from a recognizable starting point to a visible result. Choose a specific user task, show the actions that cause the outcome, and explain any limits. Finish when the viewer understands the change and knows what to explore next with your team.

## Choose the question the video will answer

Before selecting screens, write the viewer's question at the top of the brief. “How does a reviewer resolve an invoice exception?” is a workable question. “What does our platform do?” may contain several separate demonstrations and needs narrowing.

Identify what the viewer already knows. An investor who heard your pitch may understand the market but have no picture of the product. A prospective user may understand the task but need to see how it fits their existing work. These viewers could watch the same workflow with different opening explanations.

Techstars encourages founders to demonstrate what they sell and distinguish today's product from future plans. That distinction is especially useful when choosing a sequence to record. [Techstars' Master Your Pitch toolkit](https://toolkit.techstars.com/master-your-pitch)

Define the scope in one line: “This video shows a reviewer identifying a missing field, requesting a correction, and seeing the corrected record.” Everything outside that task needs a reason to remain.

## Select a workflow that has a visible finish

A useful demonstration has a starting condition, a decision, and an observable end. For a fictional invoice-review product, the starting condition is an incomplete record. The decision is to request information from the responsible person. The finish is a corrected record that the reviewer can inspect.

Choose a finish that the video can establish directly. An updated status is visible. A promise that every invoice will now be paid faster is a different claim and would require evidence beyond the screen sequence.

Compare candidate workflows before filming:

| Candidate | What the viewer could learn | Reason to choose or defer it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Browse the dashboard | Which categories exist | Useful orientation, but may lack a completed task |
| Resolve one exception | How work moves from incomplete to reviewable | Strong candidate when the transition is visible |
| Configure every rule | How an administrator controls the system | Better as a separate technical walkthrough |
| Show a future interface | What the team intends to build | Use only as an explicitly labeled concept |

Prefer the task that answers the original question with the fewest unexplained dependencies. This is an editorial selection method, not a claim that shorter demonstrations always perform better.

## Write a storyboard around cause and effect

Create the narration alongside the screen plan. This prevents a polished voiceover from describing an action the footage never shows.

Use this original five-scene worksheet:

| Scene | Screen or action | Narration prompt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting state | Open the incomplete invoice record | What is missing, and why does the reviewer care? |
| Decision | Select the field that needs correction | What judgment is the user making? |
| Action | Send the correction request | What does the product do after that decision? |
| Result | Open the corrected version | What changed, and where can the viewer verify it? |
| Boundary and next step | Hold the result on screen | What remains outside this demonstration? |

Give each scene a visible connection to the next. If you jump between accounts, say whose view is now on screen. If a second person supplies information, identify that handoff. A cut should not make a human action look like an automatic product feature.

Write the closing line now. It might invite a deeper discussion of the reviewer workflow or point to an approved trial route. Knowing the ending keeps additional features from accumulating after the central task is complete.

## Preserve the truth of the product sequence

Use prepared example data and label it as sample data. Make its relationships plausible enough for the viewer to follow: the same invoice identifier, the same missing field, and the same request should remain recognizable across scenes.

Record the product version and the conditions required for the action. Perhaps an administrator had to configure a rule beforehand. Explain that dependency briefly when it affects the meaning of the demonstration. You do not need to film every setup step, but you should not imply that setup never exists.

If you remove waiting time, show a simple indication that time was omitted. Do not describe the edited clip's length as the product's processing time. Similarly, do not use a prepared successful outcome as evidence of reliability across all cases.

When the actual product cannot complete the chosen task, change the task or label the unfinished portion. An honest prototype walkthrough can still answer a useful question about the proposed interaction. Its narration should identify what viewers are seeing before they draw conclusions from it.

## Make important details understandable in more than one way

W3C's media-planning guidance addresses captions, descriptions of important visual information, and transcripts according to the content. Plan these alongside the storyboard. [W3C guidance on planning audio and video media](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/planning/)

For a software workflow, name the action and the result in the narration. “The corrected amount appears in the review panel” gives more information than “Now you can see it here.” The accompanying text summary can describe the same transition for someone who needs to read the explanation.

Keep the important record large enough to inspect in the viewing context. Test the full video at the size it will actually appear, with captions enabled. A readable editing preview does not tell you whether a small embedded player will preserve the critical labels.

Use emphasis sparingly. If a highlight follows every pointer movement, it no longer tells the viewer where the important decision occurs. Reserve it for the field, control, or state change that advances the task.

## Test comprehension before sharing

Give the draft to someone who did not help record it. Ask them to describe the starting problem, the action taken, and the resulting state. Then ask what they think the product did automatically. That last question can reveal misleading edits or missing handoff explanations.

Turn their observations into specific repairs. “I thought the customer replied instantly” suggests a missing time-transition label. “I could not tell which amount changed” suggests a framing or hold-time problem. Neither issue requires rewriting the entire company story.

Before release, play the exact delivery file from beginning to end. Confirm the opening label, readable result, spoken explanation, captions, and destination link. Keep an approved copy with its recording date so later viewers can distinguish it from an updated product version.

Use the video as the answer to the question you started with. If the next conversation concerns configuration, prepare that demonstration separately. One focused workflow can become a useful building block without turning into an exhaustive tour.
