Hire a video editor when the main task is shaping existing footage into a clear sequence. Hire a motion designer when the explanation depends on designed graphics moving over time. Some professionals do both, but assess the specific work, required handoffs, and portfolio evidence before assigning the whole project.

## Identify what carries the explanation

Watch the planned video in your head without the decorative elements. Does the message come from a speaker, a demonstration, or a sequence of recorded actions? If so, editing is likely central. Does the message depend on showing an abstract relationship, animated diagram, or visual transformation? Then motion design may be central.

The BLS editor profile describes arranging recorded footage. Its special effects and animation profile describes creating moving images, graphics, and visual effects. These provide broad occupational context; they do not define every freelance motion designer's services. [BLS editor profile](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/media-and-communication/film-and-video-editors-and-camera-operators.htm), [BLS animation profile](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/arts-and-design/multimedia-artists-and-animators.htm).

Treat a job title as a starting point for discussion. Ask what the individual will personally create. A video editor may be skilled at animation, and a motion designer may edit interviews, but neither capability should be inferred from the title alone.

Describe the hardest part of the assignment in one sentence. “Find a clear story in three interviews” and “explain how requests move between teams” point toward different portfolios and review conversations.

## Use a scene-by-scene role worksheet

This original worksheet assigns work by the evidence each scene needs. Complete it before asking one person to handle everything.

| Planned scene | Material available | Main work | Question for the candidate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder explains the problem | Recorded interview | Editorial selection and pacing | How would you preserve the full meaning? |
| User completes a task | Screen recording | Sequence and visual emphasis | How would you keep the interface understandable? |
| Requests move between teams | Written process description | Diagram design and motion | What must the viewer see change? |
| Customer describes an outcome | Interview and approved context | Story and supporting visuals | Which source moment anchors the claim? |
| Branded ending | Approved identity assets | Layout and possibly animation | Is this existing-template work or original design? |

Mark every row as footage-led, graphics-led, or mixed. Then identify who supplies the missing inputs. An animator cannot resolve a product model the company itself has not explained. An editor cannot manufacture an authentic customer statement that was never recorded.

A mixed scene needs an interface between roles: approved text, timing, dimensions, reference frames, and a decision owner. Budget for that coordination if different people will create and assemble the components.

## Evaluate an editor with complete sequences

Ask to see a finished sequence relevant to your project. For an interview video, look at how the opening establishes context, how answers connect, and whether supporting visuals make the story easier to understand. A fast reel can demonstrate range while revealing little about sustained explanation.

Ask the editor to identify their contribution. Did they choose the story, work from a prepared script, create graphics, or receive finished animations from another specialist? This makes the example useful for judging the proposed scope.

Then discuss your own source material. Ask which passage is hardest to use and what additional material would help. You are looking for a concrete explanation of the problem, such as missing context or an interface state that appears too briefly.

For a bounded trial, provide one representative sequence and the intended viewer question. Agree on compensation and an appropriate scope. Review the decisions and correction process alongside the finished output.

An editor who flags that the story needs a new sentence may be offering more value than someone who decorates the gap. Assess whether the proposed change helps the audience understand the subject.

## Evaluate a motion designer before animation starts

For motion work, ask to see how the designer develops an idea from a written explanation. Request relevant examples of sketches, style frames, storyboards, or an animatic where available. A final animation alone does not reveal how the communication problem was solved.

Describe the relationship the visual must explain. For example, the animation might need to distinguish an incoming request, a routing decision, and an assigned owner. Ask what viewers should understand at each moment and what can be removed without losing the message.

Use approval checkpoints before detailed motion. First approve the meaning and layout, then the sequence, then the movement and finishing. This is a proposed workflow to reduce ambiguous decisions; adapt it to the designer's process.

Be precise about whether a diagram is an illustration or a representation of actual product behavior. Give the designer a factual reference and a product reviewer. A polished visual should not silently imply a capability the product does not have.

Check the intended viewing size early. Ask to see a frame at the scale your audience will encounter. If essential labels are unreadable, solve the composition before spending time refining movement.

## Plan a fictional project with both roles

Imagine fictional startup QueueBridge needs a video explaining how a request reaches the correct team. The opening uses a founder interview. A short diagram explains the routing concept, and a product recording shows the real assignment action.

The editor first proposes the story sequence and identifies the point where a diagram would help. The motion designer receives the approved explanation, a timing window, brand assets, and a clear distinction between conceptual arrows and actual interface elements.

Before animation, the product owner approves the diagram's meaning. The editor then checks whether the planned motion fits naturally between the interview and demonstration. Once the animation is delivered, the editor assembles the final sequence and reviews the transitions, sound, and captions.

If one person can credibly handle both jobs, the same checkpoints still help. The work contains two types of judgment even when it has one owner. If two people collaborate, name who maintains the master sequence and who handles later changes to the diagram.

This example illustrates a division of responsibility. It does not claim that splitting roles always improves quality or that either role commands a particular rate.

## Commission the smallest complete team

Start with the work that carries the message and commission the necessary supporting skills around it. Avoid adding custom motion solely because the project is called an explainer. A well-recorded demonstration may already provide the clearest evidence.

Conversely, do not force an abstract explanation into a screen recording if viewers need to understand a relationship the interface does not show. Ask for a simple visual concept before deciding whether custom animation is warranted.

Define final deliverables and later editability. State which finished scenes, project materials, fonts, and linked assets are included, and which dependencies need separate arrangements. Have the receiving editor confirm that the proposed handoff is usable.

For the first assignment, request a proposal that identifies the person's contribution, the inputs you must provide, and any specialist work outside scope. Choose the team whose responsibilities cover the actual scenes rather than the broadest list of software skills.
