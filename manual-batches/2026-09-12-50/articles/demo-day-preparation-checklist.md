A startup demo day checklist should assign an owner and a concrete completion test to the pitch, media, event logistics, and follow-up. Start with the organizer's requirements, rehearse the actual presentation, test the delivery files, and prepare a way to capture requests so useful conversations can continue after the event.

## Turn event instructions into a working plan

Begin with the program's latest instructions. Record the presentation limit, submission deadline, required file type, presenter arrangements, arrival or connection time, and the person handling technical questions. If a requirement is unclear, resolve it before preparing a dependent asset.

Build your schedule backward from the earliest handoff, which may be earlier than the event. A deck submitted to an event producer can become difficult to change even while your team is still rehearsing. Identify that moment as a practical freeze point.

Give each item one responsible person. A small founding team may assign several responsibilities to the same person, but “everyone” makes it difficult to know who must verify completion. Put a backup name beside items that affect the live presentation.

Use four states on the working board: not started, in progress, ready to check, and checked. Reserve “checked” for work that passed its completion test. An exported file is ready to check; a file played successfully on the intended setup has evidence behind its status.

## Lock the story and the material facts

Before perfecting slide transitions, agree on the company description, the customer problem, the product's current state, and the main evidence. Keep a short approved-facts sheet beside the deck. Include the date and definition of every number you plan to show.

Check that the spoken pitch matches the visuals. A slide labeled “pilot organizations” should not become “customers” in the narration unless that description is accurate. If a feature is planned, its screen label and spoken introduction should both preserve that status.

YC recommends practicing with honest feedback and recording yourself when needed. Use that advice to test whether the approved story is actually the story you deliver. [YC's Demo Day presentation guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/)

Write down which late changes are worth reopening the deck. Factual corrections and serious comprehension problems usually deserve attention. A new decorative idea can wait. Every accepted change should include time to recheck the affected file and spoken passage.

## Use an owner-and-test checklist

The following original board combines preparation with a definition of completion. Adapt the roles to your team rather than adding people you do not need.

| Work item | Suggested owner | Completion test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Event requirements | Program contact owner | Latest instructions recorded and unresolved questions answered |
| Pitch story | Presenting founder | Listener can restate customer, product, and next step |
| Claims and numbers | Relevant fact owner | Definitions, dates, and wording match the approved evidence |
| Presentation file | Deck owner | Correct version opens and displays as intended |
| Optional demo media | Product owner | Starting state, action, and result remain understandable |
| Recording accessibility | Media owner | Captions and visual explanation checked in delivery version |
| Event setup | Presenter or producer | Sound, display, controls, and fallback rehearsed |
| Follow-up requests | Relationship owner | Requests have a contact, action, owner, and due date |

For each row, add the file location or note that demonstrates completion. That makes the board useful when someone else must step in. It also prevents a last-minute search through multiple similarly named exports.

Do not turn this checklist into a numeric readiness score. A missing presentation file cannot be offset by several completed low-impact tasks. Review unresolved items by the consequence of failure and the time needed to repair them.

## Rehearse the actual event conditions

Run the presentation with the equipment and constraints you expect to use. Stand if you will stand at the event. Advance your own slides if that is your responsibility. If a producer controls the deck, agree on clear cues and rehearse them.

Test the opening from silence. Avoid depending on an introduction that might change. Practice reaching the conclusion within the assigned limit and identify a supporting passage you can omit if the schedule is compressed.

Prepare a fallback explanation for any media-dependent point. If the display is unavailable, the presenter should still be able to explain the central product action. If the organizers permit a recorded fallback, test it locally and make its recorded nature clear.

Run a recovery rehearsal in which a teammate deliberately skips a slide or pauses playback. The goal is to practice continuing calmly with an accurate explanation. Keep the exercise focused on plausible problems in your setup; an exhaustive catalogue of unlikely failures will consume preparation time.

## Inspect the files that other people will receive

Treat the delivery version as a separate checkpoint. Open it from its final location, confirm that it is the approved version, and check its beginning, middle, and ending. Follow every link that the audience is expected to use.

For recorded material, include accessibility in the handoff. W3C's planning guidance explicitly places accessibility work in production planning and assigns responsibilities for captions, description, transcripts, and playback. [W3C's media-planning guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/planning/)

In practice, inspect the words that could change meaning: product names, numbers, negatives, and limitations. Also check whether captions cover the button or result being discussed. A technically available caption track still needs to work with the picture.

Prepare a simple file manifest with name, version date, purpose, and owner. Distinguish the live deck, the readable follow-up deck, and any optional demo recording. Share the appropriate item with the producer instead of sending a folder and asking them to identify the correct file.

## Prepare for the conversations after the pitch

Create a request log before the event. Use fields for contact, conversation context, exact request, promised response, owner, and next action. A short note immediately after a conversation is more useful than a vague recollection the next morning.

Draft a plain follow-up structure with space for the actual question. Keep any media link optional until you know it is relevant. If someone asks for a deck, the response should make that deck easy to find. If they ask about a product action, a focused demonstration may help.

Agree internally on who answers different questions. Product behavior, customer evidence, hiring, and financial information may have different owners. Avoid promising an answer by a particular time until the responsible person can provide it.

Finally, hold a brief preparation review: what is still unverified, who owns it, and when will it be resolved? After the event, use the same board to close requests and note improvements for the next presentation. This keeps Demo Day preparation connected to the actual work that follows.
