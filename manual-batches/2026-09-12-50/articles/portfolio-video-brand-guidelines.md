Video brand guidelines for a portfolio program should define what stays consistent across the series and what belongs to each company. Standardize identification, readability, accessibility, and review responsibilities. Let companies retain their approved names, visual identity, and product language, with clear rules for how the program appears beside them.

## Define the relationship between the brands

Start by deciding whose story the viewer is watching. A program overview can lead with the accelerator identity. A company's product explanation should make the company and product clear. A mentor lesson may use the program's teaching format while identifying the contributor and relevant role.

Write one rule for each common context. For example: company demonstration, founder interview, mentor lesson, and event recap. Specify the lead identity, supporting attribution, and final destination. This prevents an editor from making a new hierarchy decision on every assignment.

Do not combine company logos into a single invented identity. Ask each company for current approved assets and the person who can resolve usage questions. When the program has permission to display a logo, that does not automatically establish permission to alter it or imply a commercial relationship beyond the actual one.

Keep the wording of the relationship accurate. “Participating in the fall cohort” and “supported by the program's media workshop” describe different things. Choose the statement that is true for the specific asset and have the relevant owner check it.

## Separate shared rules from company choices

A useful guide makes mandatory decisions easy to find. Put shared standards in one column, company-controlled choices in another, and exceptions in a short decision record. Avoid a long presentation in which a production requirement looks the same as a visual preference.

Institutional video guidance can show the breadth of a working standard. UCSF's own video standards cover briefing, visual style, accessibility, readable text, and publication checks. [UCSF Video Standards](https://identity.ucsf.edu/photography-video/video-standards). Those are examples of categories to consider, not branding rules for your portfolio or an endorsement of a particular production service.

Here is an original ownership worksheet for a multi-company program.

| Element | Shared program rule | Company decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speaker identification | Show a readable name and role | Confirm current spelling and title |
| Opening context | Explain the series and intended audience | Approve the company description |
| Logo treatment | Use the agreed relationship and hierarchy | Supply approved variants and usage guidance |
| Product visuals | Preserve the information needed to understand | Approve screens, terminology, and depicted workflow |
| Captions | Review accuracy and avoid covering essential content | Confirm names and specialist vocabulary |
| Closing action | Give one clear next destination | Choose and verify the company destination |
| Reuse | Keep the version and use decision traceable | Approve materially different contexts |

Use this worksheet to settle ownership before debating fonts or transitions. A visually consistent series can still misidentify a speaker or send viewers to the wrong company page.

## Make readability an explicit design constraint

Choose a small set of text treatments for names, section labels, explanatory notes, and captions. Show examples at the actual playback size you expect viewers to use. A title that looks clear on an editing monitor may be difficult to read in a small embedded player.

W3C's minimum text-contrast guidance specifies a 4.5:1 ratio for ordinary text, with exceptions including qualifying large text and logotypes. [Understanding Contrast (Minimum)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html). Use that as a concrete reference when choosing informational text treatments, and check the result against the backgrounds that appear in the video.

For moving footage, a stable text panel can be easier to assess than a transparent overlay whose background continually changes. Test the brightest and darkest relevant frames. Avoid treating a logo exception as permission to make a speaker's name or explanatory text unreadable.

Specify how captions and speaker labels share the screen. If both occupy the lower part of the image, move one or separate their timing. Define a way to preview the intended layout with the destination's controls visible. Do not make a single crop template the answer to every platform and every demonstration.

## Treat captions and audio as part of the identity

A portfolio series should sound and read consistently enough that viewers can follow it comfortably. Provide a pronunciation and terminology field in the intake form. Give the editor the spelling of founders, companies, products, and specialist terms before caption review begins.

W3C explains that automatically generated captions need accuracy verification and generally require editing. [Captions/Subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/). Assign that review to someone rather than using the existence of a caption file as the acceptance test.

Write guidance for speech clarity, music, and pauses. Ask reviewers to listen to the whole piece with ordinary playback equipment. Music should not obscure an important explanation. A dramatic transition should not erase a pause that makes a change in topic understandable.

When a visual carries information that speech does not, decide how that information will be conveyed. A founder pointing to an unlabeled comparison needs an explanation of what differs. Decorative motion and essential product information should not receive the same treatment.

Record these requirements in the production brief so an outside editor can estimate and perform the work. A brand guide that introduces accessibility only at the final approval stage creates avoidable rework.

## Test the guide with contrasting fictional assets

Before approving a portfolio-wide template, try it on two deliberately different examples. One could be a fictional hardware founder explaining a physical component; the other could be a fictional software founder walking through a dense queue screen.

For the hardware explanation, check that the name label does not cover the component and that the company identity remains visible against the background. For the software example, check whether the frame leaves enough room to read the interface and captions together.

Now test an edge case: a long founder name, an approved company logo that needs a different background, or a contributor with two relevant roles. Write the solution into the guide as an example of how to apply the rule.

Keep the test focused on meaning and recognition. The purpose is to discover whether the standard survives ordinary variation, not to make both companies look identical. If a shared element blocks the explanation, revise the element or document a sensible exception.

Ask a reviewer unfamiliar with the project to identify the company, speaker, topic, and next step. Their answer is a practical check on whether the hierarchy communicates what the team intended.

## Maintain a small, usable guide

Publish the guide with a version date, owner, approved examples, and a short change log. Keep the current templates and company assets easy to locate through the organization's existing file system. Mark superseded versions so they do not appear equally valid.

Create an exception record with the asset, rule affected, reason, approver, and future applicability. A one-off layout adjustment should not silently rewrite the standard for the whole portfolio.

Use a final review in three passes: factual identity, readability and access, then visual consistency. This order helps the team resolve consequential errors before debating stylistic details. Check the uploaded version at its actual destination as well as the local file.
