Choose a video agency when your workload needs several coordinated specialties and a named production lead. Choose a freelancer when the job has a clear boundary and your team can supply direction. Compare actual people, deliverables, review responsibilities, and recovery arrangements before deciding which model fits your startup.

## Start with the work you cannot manage yourself

The useful opening question is which decisions and tasks you need someone else to own. An editor might shape an interview beautifully while expecting you to arrange the recording, choose the story, and approve the product claims. That can be a good arrangement if those responsibilities have owners.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes editors as arranging footage and working with producers and directors. Its producer profile separately covers budgets, schedules, crew, and production oversight. Those distinctions expose work hidden inside the phrase “make us a video.” [BLS editor duties](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/media-and-communication/film-and-video-editors-and-camera-operators.htm), [BLS producer duties](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/entertainment-and-sports/producers-and-directors.htm).

Before contacting providers, list the next real project from idea to delivery. Circle the stages that lack an internal owner. If the gaps include interviewing, shoot planning, and coordinating specialists, ask for production leadership. If the gap is turning approved recordings into finished edits, ask for editing capacity.

## Use this responsibility worksheet in both conversations

This is a proposed comparison worksheet, not a rating of agencies or freelancers. Send the same version to both candidates. Replace promises such as “full service” with a person's name and an observable output.

| Work to assign | Evidence to request | Internal owner if excluded |
|---|---|---|
| Story development | Outline with the central message | Founder or marketing lead |
| Capture preparation | Shot list and recording instructions | Producer or team coordinator |
| Editing | Relevant complete sample and scope | Content owner |
| Graphics and sound | Named specialist and included tasks | Creative lead |
| Review management | Feedback route and decision schedule | One consolidated reviewer |
| Final handoff | Example delivery inventory | Company file custodian |

Ask candidates to mark each row included, optional, or excluded. An optional item needs a quoting method. An included item needs a definition. “Graphics included” could mean inserting an existing title template or designing a new animated explanation; your comparison needs to distinguish those jobs.

Then mark how often each responsibility will recur. A launch film with one shoot and a weekly product series may deserve different arrangements. Avoid making the exceptional project dictate the operating model for everything else.

## When an agency arrangement makes sense

Consider an agency if you want one party to coordinate a shoot, manage creative development, and deliver work across several disciplines. Make that coordination an explicit requirement. A larger company name alone does not establish that the people assigned to your account will provide it.

Ask to meet the person who will run the project after the proposal is accepted. Request an explanation of how that person handles unavailable talent, missing footage, and conflicting stakeholder feedback. The answer should describe responsibilities and a sequence, rather than simply assure you that problems are rare.

Also ask where the agency expects your involvement. You may still need to approve a script, provide access to a subject, check product accuracy, and consolidate executive feedback. Put those appointments in the schedule before accepting a delivery date.

An agency is a weaker fit when its process adds coordination you do not need or makes a small recurring change difficult to request. Test that point with a realistic example: the product team changes a button label after the master is approved. Who receives the request, who estimates it, and who supplies the corrected version?

## When a freelancer arrangement makes sense

A freelancer is worth considering when you can describe a bounded job, provide usable inputs, and make decisions promptly. Direct collaboration can be valuable when the same specialist needs to learn your interview style or product vocabulary. Verify that fit through discussion of complete work, not a montage of unrelated highlights.

Ask what the freelancer personally handled in each sample. An attractive finished piece may also reflect another person's writing, animation, sound mix, or direction. Attribution makes the portfolio more useful; it helps you understand which results the proposed engagement actually covers.

Discuss availability around your likely peaks. Agree on what happens if a recording arrives late or a planned release moves forward. A calendar reservation, an estimate, and a guaranteed delivery are different commitments; ask which one the proposal offers.

For recurring work, specify how project files and current decisions will stay accessible to your company. This is useful even in an excellent relationship. A practical arrangement should let both parties take time away without leaving the startup unable to locate its latest approved assets.

## Compare a fictional launch and recurring series

Imagine a fictional startup, HarborDesk, planning a launch interview plus a continuing series of short product lessons. The launch needs location recording, interview direction, and custom motion graphics. The lessons use recordings prepared by its product lead and an established visual format.

For the launch, HarborDesk asks each candidate to include coordination of the shoot and graphics. A freelancer who brings a producer and motion designer may offer a suitable proposal. An agency that only supplies editing would leave important gaps. The relevant comparison is the offered team and responsibility map.

For the recurring lessons, HarborDesk supplies the approved outline and screen recording. It asks an editor to create one lesson and then make a realistic correction. That trial would reveal whether the inputs are sufficient and how much internal explanation is needed.

The startup could choose different providers for these two workloads. Alternatively, one candidate might demonstrate a credible approach to both. Neither outcome is predetermined by the agency or freelancer label. The example illustrates a decision process; it reports no actual trial, price, or performance result.

## Run a bounded trial and make the decision

Choose one representative assignment with agreed compensation, acceptance criteria, and limits. Include the original footage, a factual reference, brand assets, expected versions, and a deadline for feedback. Avoid an unpaid speculative assignment that asks candidates to solve your whole campaign.

Review the first draft for specific evidence: Does the opening answer the intended question? Can you follow the product sequence? Are the requested assets present? Record unresolved issues and internal time spent supplying missing direction. This distinguishes a craft mismatch from a brief that needs improvement.

Send one consolidated correction round. Evaluate the response and the final handoff as well as the finished video. Ask a colleague to locate the approved version and explain what can be edited later. Choose the arrangement whose remaining internal responsibilities your team can actually sustain.

Write a short decision note: chosen provider, workload covered, responsibilities retained, known limitations, and the date you will reconsider. A useful trigger for reconsideration is a changed workload, such as moving from prepared recordings to frequent location shoots.
