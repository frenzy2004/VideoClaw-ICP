Choose in-house video production when recurring work benefits from close product access and you can support a clear role. Outsource when demand is uneven or needs specific expertise. Compare the same workload, internal management time, total costs, and continuity needs; a hybrid arrangement may fit better than either extreme.

## Describe your demand before choosing a team

Build a record of recent video requests. Include work that was canceled or never started, because a wish list can otherwise look like a dependable production schedule. For each request, record the audience, source material, tasks, urgency, approver, and whether the finished asset was actually used.

Separate recurring work from peaks. Weekly product lessons, a quarterly customer story, and an occasional location shoot create different staffing needs. A team member who is excellent at one may still need outside support for another. Make that visible before assigning a single person an expansive job title.

Use a representative planning period that includes ordinary weeks and known launches. If your company is too new to have a history, label the schedule a forecast and revisit it after the first assignments. Hiring against a forecast is a different decision from staffing an observed queue.

The BLS editor profile distinguishes editing from camera work and describes collaboration with production roles. Use that distinction when describing the recurring responsibilities of your proposed hire. [BLS film and video editor profile](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/media-and-communication/film-and-video-editors-and-camera-operators.htm).

## Map where knowledge needs to live

Some decisions require ongoing company context: which product behavior is current, what customers can be shown, what the next launch includes, and which audience matters most. My recommendation is to assign those decisions to named internal owners regardless of who edits.

Then decide which production knowledge you want to build internally. An employee might maintain the recording practice and reusable lessons while a specialist handles occasional animation. Or an internal content lead might own scripts and approvals while outside editors handle all assembly. Both are legitimate designs.

Use this original responsibility map to make the arrangement concrete:

| Responsibility | Must know daily product changes? | Proposed owner | Handoff evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Select the next buyer question | Usually | Internal content lead | Approved topic |
| Verify a screen recording | Yes | Product owner | Dated accuracy check |
| Edit a prepared interview | Depends on complexity | Internal or external editor | Rough cut and questions |
| Build an occasional diagram | Brief can supply context | Motion specialist | Approved storyboard |
| Approve the final version | Needs company authority | Named internal approver | Version-specific decision |
| Maintain the archive | Needs continuity | Company custodian | Current file inventory |

The table is a starting point. Change an owner whenever your actual work requires it. What matters is that someone accepts each responsibility before production begins.

## Compare costs using matching boundaries

An outsourcing invoice and an employee's salary cover different things. BLS compensation reporting separates wages and salaries from benefits, including paid leave, insurance, retirement, and legally required benefits. It does not supply your company's complete cost of employing a video team. [BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.htm).

For an internal arrangement, collect actual compensation assumptions, equipment needs, software, recruiting, onboarding, management, and outside specialist support. Avoid counting paid leave twice if it is already represented in salary; account for its effect on available production capacity separately.

For outsourcing, record quoted production fees, source preparation, internal briefing and review time, transfer work, optional revisions, and handoff charges. Ask which tools or licensed assets are included. Separate one-time setup from recurring work so the comparison does not penalize either model unfairly.

Keep cash spending and internal time in separate columns. If you later value time using an internal rate, show that assumption. Do not treat an employee's available hour as automatically billable or pretend that every hour saved creates cash savings.

The denominator should match the decision. Compare a month of recurring lessons with the same month of recurring lessons, including approval and delivery. Do not compare a full employee role with one outside editing assignment.

## Test management capacity and daily access

An internal hire needs a manager who can set priorities, give useful feedback, and protect time for production. If every department can independently request urgent videos, the hire may inherit an unresolved planning problem. Define the intake route before recruiting.

Outsourcing also needs management. Someone must deliver usable source files and answer questions. Ask yourself who will explain a product change if the founder is unavailable, and who can settle a disagreement between sales and product. Outside capacity becomes usable when those decisions have dependable owners.

Examine access needs at the task level. A recurring editor may need approved screen recordings, not direct entry into your product's live customer environment. An internal producer may need frequent conversations with engineers. Write the narrow access requirement rather than assuming internal means unrestricted and external means disconnected.

Check where work waits during a small pilot. If footage sits untouched, you may need production capacity. If completed drafts wait for approval, adding editing capacity will not address that queue. Change the responsibility or review schedule that the observation identifies.

## Work through a fictional staffing decision

Consider fictional startup RouteBoard. Its product lead wants a steady tutorial library, while its marketing lead expects occasional customer films. The tutorial plan uses prepared screen recordings and needs frequent corrections as the interface changes. Customer films need interviews and location capture.

RouteBoard first assigns the product lead responsibility for recording accuracy and a marketing owner responsibility for prioritization. It then compares two concrete arrangements: an internal editor with outside filming support, and an external editor receiving a recurring source package from the product team.

For both arrangements, the company writes the same tutorial acceptance criteria and includes the customer-film work as a separately scoped peak. It tests one representative tutorial and asks how a later interface update would be handled.

If the tutorial queue proves stable and ongoing context is a meaningful part of the work, an internal role deserves serious consideration. If source preparation remains inconsistent, the company should fix that dependency before assuming a hire will fill the schedule. These are conditional decisions in a fictional scenario, not measured results or a prescribed staffing threshold.

## Choose a model you can review and adjust

Write the decision in terms of workload and responsibility: “We will outsource prepared tutorial edits while keeping recording, product accuracy, and approval internal.” That is more useful than simply writing “outsource video.”

Attach a review date and observable triggers for change. Examples include sustained recurring demand, growing internal briefing time, repeated need for an unavailable specialty, or a new requirement for frequent filming. Choose triggers that matter to your company rather than borrowing a universal break-even number.

Before starting, document the current files, templates, decisions, and incoming owner's first assignment. An internal hire should receive as coherent a handoff as an external partner. Keep the company archive understandable so a later staffing change does not require rediscovering how every video was made.
