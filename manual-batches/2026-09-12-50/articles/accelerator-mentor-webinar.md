An accelerator mentor webinar becomes reusable when it teaches one decision, demonstrates the reasoning with a concrete example, and gives founders a task to try afterward. Plan the recording around complete lessons, preserve the mentor’s qualifications and caveats, and separate public teaching material from confidential company discussion before creating clips.

## Choose a decision that the session can teach

A startup mentor webinar needs a narrower purpose than “learn about marketing.” Try a decision such as choosing the first customer question for an interview, deciding which product workflow to demonstrate, or diagnosing why a sales handoff is unclear. The participant should know when the lesson is relevant.

Ask the mentor to finish this sentence: after the session, a founder facing this situation should be able to do this specific thing. Use the answer to select the example and exercise. If the mentor cannot name a realistic action, shorten the subject or divide it into separate sessions.

The Techstars Mentor Manifesto asks mentors to distinguish opinion from fact, protect confidential information, and guide founders without controlling their decisions. [The Techstars Mentor Manifesto](https://www.techstars.com/blog/founder-advice/mentor-manifesto). Those principles are especially useful when a live conversation may later be viewed without its original context.

Give the mentor a brief that identifies the audience’s stage and experience. Advice for a founder with several sales conversations may not apply to a team still deciding whom to interview. State those prerequisites in the session description.

## Design a lesson with a beginning and an ending

Organize the teaching around a question, an example, a decision rule, an exception, and an exercise. This is an original session structure you can adapt. It makes the eventual recording useful to someone who did not attend the live introduction.

For example, a fictional session could teach founders how to choose a product demonstration. The mentor introduces an invented support-tool company with three workflows. Participants compare which workflow answers one buyer question, then explain their choice. The mentor discusses why a different buyer could justify a different demonstration.

Keep the example visible while the reasoning happens. If the audience must remember a long verbal setup, pause and restate the few facts that matter. Introduce unfamiliar terminology before using it to explain a decision.

Close the lesson by returning to the original question. Say what the method helps decide and where it stops. A lesson about selecting a demo workflow does not also establish a pricing strategy or prove customer demand. A clear ending gives the editor a sensible place to stop the clip.

## Separate reusable teaching from private discussion

Tell participants which part of the session is being recorded and how that recording is intended to be used. Arrange any required permissions through the program’s existing process. Avoid inviting sensitive company examples into a session advertised as public learning material.

Consider recording the prepared teaching segment and holding company-specific discussion afterward under the program’s chosen arrangements. If a participant introduces confidential detail during the recorded portion, note the moment and resolve it before distribution.

The moderator should have a simple intervention ready: pause the company detail, restate the general question, and let the participant continue in the appropriate setting. Do not rely on the editor to identify every sensitive implication later.

Give anonymous questions the same scrutiny as named ones. Removing a founder’s name does not necessarily remove identifying details about a product, customer, or recent event. When a real case cannot be shared appropriately, build a clearly fictional example that tests the same decision.

For remote cohorts, offer a way to submit questions before the session. That allows founders in different time zones to contribute and gives the mentor time to decide which questions suit a reusable lesson.

## Create a lesson extraction worksheet

After the session, review the recording for complete units of meaning. Do not begin with a target number of clips. Use this original worksheet to decide whether a proposed excerpt can stand alone.

| Lesson field | What the editor needs | Fictional example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founder situation | When the advice applies | A founder must choose one workflow for a buyer demo |
| Core question | The decision being taught | Which workflow answers the buyer's immediate concern? |
| Example | Enough setup to understand the reasoning | Three candidate workflows from an invented support tool |
| Recommendation | The mentor's actual position | Choose the workflow that resolves the named concern |
| Limitation | What would change the answer | A different buyer role may need a different workflow |
| Practice task | What the viewer should do | Write the buyer's question and compare two workflows |
| Review owner | Who checks the excerpt | Mentor for meaning; program editor for completeness |

An excerpt is ready to edit when those fields have answers. If the limitation appears much later in the session, bring the context into the accompanying explanation or choose a longer segment. Do not join separate remarks in a way that implies an argument the mentor never made.

Keep the original recording reference beside the worksheet. If the mentor questions an edit, the team can revisit the full exchange without searching every file.

## Package the lesson for learning and access

Provide a title that names the task, a short description of the relevant situation, the video, and the exercise. A viewer should be able to decide whether the lesson applies before pressing play.

Include reviewed captions and a transcript with necessary visual context. W3C distinguishes a basic transcript of relevant audio from a descriptive transcript that also conveys visual information needed to understand the content. [Transcripts](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/transcripts/). A worksheet walkthrough may need the latter because spoken phrases such as “this column” are incomplete on their own.

For the fictional demonstration lesson, describe the choices shown on screen and the reason the mentor crosses one out. The transcript does not need to inventory decorative slide elements. It needs to preserve the information that explains the decision.

Ask the mentor to review the edited lesson and its title together. An accurate clip can still be misrepresented by an overly broad headline. Add the recording date when changing tools, market conditions, or program practices could affect how the advice is used.

## Assign one exercise and learn from the responses

Give participants one small task to complete after watching. For the demo-selection lesson, ask for a buyer question, two possible workflows, and the reason for choosing one. That response reveals whether the lesson helped them apply the reasoning.

Offer feedback on the decision, not the founder’s presentation style. A short written answer can be sufficient. If several participants choose workflows that do not answer their stated question, revise the example or add a second demonstration of the method.

Maintain a resource entry with the audience, task, mentor, recording date, worksheet, and next review date. Revisit lessons when the relevant tools or assumptions change. Retire an outdated excerpt or attach a clear update instead of letting it become the default answer indefinitely.
