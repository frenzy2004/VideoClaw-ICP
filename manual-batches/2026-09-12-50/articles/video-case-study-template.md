A video case study should show a specific customer's problem, the work undertaken, and evidence supporting the account of what changed. Build the script from an interview and a claim-evidence worksheet. Preserve relevant conditions, distinguish observation from interpretation, and have the customer review the exact wording and edit before release.

## Select a story with a verifiable change

Start with a customer situation that your intended buyer can recognize. Describe the task, the people involved, and what made the previous approach difficult. A story about a specific handoff is easier to investigate than a broad claim that an organization transformed its entire operation.

Ask what changed in the work. The answer might be a documented process, a newly visible status, or a measured outcome. These support different types of story. Do not turn an improved process description into a performance claim without evidence for that additional statement.

The FTC's advertising guidance says advertisers need support for express and implied claims before an advertisement runs, and customer statements do not automatically substantiate claims requiring objective evaluation. [FTC advertising guidance for small businesses](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business).

My editorial recommendation is to choose the strongest story the available evidence can actually support. If the customer can verify the workflow but has no reliable outcome measurement, make the process the subject. A specific account can be useful without a dramatic percentage in the opening.

## Interview for actions and conditions

Ask the customer to reconstruct an actual example. What triggered the task? Who owned it? Which tools or documents were involved? What did the team change, and what remained difficult? Follow the sequence before asking for a summary judgment.

Separate observations from interpretations in your notes. “The team began assigning an owner when requests arrived” describes an action. “That made us more efficient” is an interpretation until the customer explains what they mean and how they know.

Use questions that leave room for a mixed account. Ask which conditions made the change possible, what additional work was needed, and where the approach may not fit. Those answers help a buyer judge relevance and prevent the story from becoming a generic endorsement.

Avoid feeding the interviewee a desired claim. If a prepared script is needed, draft it from approved interview material and supporting records. Let the customer correct the account in their own words. Keep a source reference for each selected statement so editing does not sever it from its context.

## Build a claim-evidence worksheet

Use this original worksheet before writing the final script. The example is fictional: an invented equipment-service team is documenting how it assigns incoming maintenance requests. No business results are asserted.

| Proposed story element | Evidence to request | Editorial treatment | Open question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Requests arrived through several channels | Customer explanation and permitted examples | Describe the starting process | Which channels belong in the story? |
| Team added an assignment step | Approved workflow record or demonstration | Show the change in the work | Who performs the step? |
| Staff can inspect request ownership | Current example screen | Demonstrate the visible state | What access does the viewer need? |
| Work became faster | Defined before-and-after measurement | Hold the claim pending evidence | What was measured, over what period? |
| Approach suits other teams | Evidence beyond this single account | Avoid a universal conclusion | Which conditions may differ? |

For quantitative claims, record the definition, baseline, comparison period, population, calculation, and source owner. Ask about other changes during the same period. Even a correct calculation may not establish that one product caused the difference.

For qualitative claims, preserve who observed what and under which circumstances. Label a reenacted example as a demonstration when viewers might otherwise mistake it for the original event. Do not use stock footage as apparent proof of work performed by the customer.

## Use a script structure that keeps evidence close

The following original script template is designed to be filled with verified material. Leave unsupported fields out rather than writing around their absence.

1. Situation: “Our team handles [specific task] for [relevant audience or operation].”
2. Problem: “Previously, [documented process] made [specific part of the work] difficult.”
3. Action: “We changed [specific step], with [named roles] responsible for the work.”
4. Evidence: “Here is [approved record, demonstration, or defined measurement] showing [supported observation].”
5. Conditions: “This account applies to [relevant scope], and [important limitation or additional work].”
6. Next step: “To evaluate a similar approach, review [resource that actually exists].”

Choose visuals that serve each line. A process diagram can clarify a handoff; a readable screen can show a state; an approved chart can display a measurement with its definition. A montage of happy people may establish tone, but it cannot carry the evidentiary role of those materials.

Keep qualifications near the claim they limit. If the result concerns one team during one period, the viewer should encounter that scope while hearing the result. Do not rely on a distant closing note to repair an overbroad opening.

## Review the edit as an account of evidence

Give the customer the exact cut, on-screen wording, captions, and proposed surrounding copy. Ask them to confirm the sequence, quotations, visual accuracy, and relevant conditions. Approval of an interview does not tell you whether a later excerpt preserves the same meaning.

The FTC's endorsement guidance explains that specific-results endorsements can imply what others should expect, and that generic “results may vary” wording does not necessarily change that interpretation. [FTC guidance on endorsements and expected results](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking). Have the responsible reviewer assess the final claim and presentation rather than treating a disclaimer as a substitute for that review.

Check implied claims in the visual sequence. A “before” screen followed by a celebratory result chart may suggest a causal relationship even when the narration avoids saying so. Review the picture, headline, audio, and captions as a combined message.

Record the approved version and what the approval covers. If a customer changes the scope of a statement, update the script, graphics, and derivatives together. A corrected transcript should not coexist with an older public clip making the broader claim.

## Prepare useful derivatives and maintenance

Create excerpts only when each retains enough context to remain accurate. A short problem-and-action segment may be usable independently. A result statement that depends on a detailed qualification may need to remain in the longer case study.

Build a distribution note for sales or marketing that explains who the story helps, what it demonstrates, and what it does not establish. This gives teammates useful context when sharing it with buyers facing a different situation.

Assign a refresh owner and a review trigger. Examples include a changed product workflow, a customer correction, or evidence that no longer supports the wording. Keep the source interview, supporting records, and approvals accessible to authorized reviewers.

Before starting your next case study, complete the situation and evidence rows first. If the evidence row is empty, the next task is discovery with the customer. If it supports only a process account, write that account precisely and let the buyer assess its relevance.
