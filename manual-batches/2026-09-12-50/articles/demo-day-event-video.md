To film a startup demo day, decide which recordings are needed and who may receive them before planning camera positions. Capture intelligible speech, readable demonstrations, and clearly identified speakers. Track permissions and changes during the event, then review each company’s recording separately before delivering approved follow-up assets.

## Separate the event's different video jobs

An event highlight, a complete founder presentation, and a private recording for rehearsal review are different deliverables. List them separately. A highlight needs a sense of the occasion; a presentation needs the speaker’s complete argument; a coaching recording may be useful only to the presenting team.

Do not infer the intended audience from the words “demo day.” YC, for example, describes its Demo Day audience as invite-only investors and media. That is a reminder to check each organizer’s distribution plan rather than assuming every presentation is a public social asset. [Y Combinator Demo Day](https://www.ycombinator.com/demoday/).

For each deliverable, name the recipient, publication destination, company reviewer, and latest useful delivery date. Ask whether a recording should remain private, be shared with a defined audience, or become public after review. Confirm the decision through the organizer’s existing permission process.

If an investor presentation needs a separate communications review, place that review in the schedule. The filming plan should not make assumptions about whether offering-related material can be published.

## Build a capture sheet from the running order

Use the event schedule as the backbone of the recording plan. Give each presentation a stable identifier even if the speaking order changes. This lets the crew connect notes, footage, slides, and approvals without depending on memory.

The following original worksheet shows how to plan one session. The entries are fictional, including the company and presentation details.

| Capture field | Fictional entry | Check before recording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Segment identifier | D07: dispatch workflow demonstration | Same identifier on crew notes and company record |
| Speaker identity | Morgan Lee, founder of Route Example | Spelling, pronunciation, and displayed role confirmed |
| Audio | Stage microphone and separately monitored backup | Listen to a short test recording |
| Visual material | Presenter view plus a clean slide feed | Product text readable at playback size |
| Audience use | Company review copy first | Public version requires recorded approval |
| Sensitive moment | Customer name on an early slide | Replace slide before the session |
| Follow-up destination | Company-approved overview page | Destination opens and matches the presentation |

Add a notes column during the event. Record a microphone interruption, slide correction, restart, or changed audience instruction against the segment identifier. These observations are more useful to the editor than a general note that the session “went well.”

Share the sheet with the stage manager, crew, and company coordinator. Everyone should know who can authorize a last-minute change.

## Rehearse the sound and screen capture together

Test the setup using an actual presenter and representative slides. Listen to the recording, not only the room speakers. A microphone can sound acceptable in the venue while the recorded feed is missing, distorted, or much quieter than expected.

Check what happens when a speaker moves away from the lectern, turns toward a demonstration, or takes a question. If the event includes remote founders, test their screen-sharing and audio path separately. Give remote participants the same instructions about names, pacing, and review that in-person presenters receive.

W3C’s event guidance recommends microphone use and repeating questions that are not audible through the sound system. [Making Events Accessible](https://www.w3.org/WAI/teach-advocate/accessible-presentations/). Build that into the moderator’s instructions so both the live audience and recording receive the question before the answer.

Ask presenters to explain essential visual changes aloud. “The exception moves from the shared queue to the named dispatcher” carries more meaning than “as you can see here.” Verify that the recording captures the screen content needed to follow that explanation.

A stable wide view can provide continuity when a close view misses a movement. Choose the camera setup according to crew capacity; an unmonitored extra angle does not replace a reliable primary recording.

## Give permissions an operational owner

Assign one coordinator to maintain the current recording and distribution decisions. That person should be available when a founder changes a slide, a guest joins a panel, or an attendee asks not to appear in promotional footage.

Use the organizer’s approved participant notices and permission records. The practical requirement for the crew is an unambiguous instruction: capture this segment, avoid this seating area, keep this interview private, or wait for clarification. A camera operator should not need to interpret a long agreement while a presentation starts.

Keep audience reaction shots separate from presentation capture. Plan views that do not reveal readable badges, private screens, or people who should not be included. Ask interview participants where their comments may be used before recording a spontaneous testimonial.

If the event’s purpose changes, revisit the use decision. A recording made for founder feedback should not quietly become promotional material for the next cohort. Document the new request and obtain the appropriate decision before editing it into another asset.

## Log the event so an editor can reconstruct it

At the beginning of each presentation, record enough identifying context to match the speaker with the schedule. Keep a live event log using one consistent clock or recording reference. When something goes wrong, note the approximate moment and the action taken.

Protect complete presentation recordings before creating highlights. Confirm that the available copies can be opened and that the audio and visuals match. Check at least the start, a middle segment, and the ending of each presentation before releasing the crew from any remaining capture responsibilities.

Then create a first review copy for each company. Flag anything the editor could not resolve: a missing screen image, unclear phrase, excluded question, or factual correction. Do not disguise a new recording as though it happened on stage. If the company supplies a replacement explanation, present it in a way that accurately reflects its origin.

Keep the polished event recap separate from the company’s review copy. The recap may be ready sooner, but its release should not imply that every full presentation has also been approved.

## Deliver follow-up assets with enough context

Give each founder a small handoff record containing the approved version, intended audience, speaker details, caption status, and destination. A recipient should be able to understand what they are receiving without searching the event archive.

For a public clip, include a descriptive title and a next step that matches the company’s current wishes. For a restricted recording, use the agreed access settings and check them from the intended recipient’s perspective. Record who can handle a correction or remove an outdated version from an owned destination.

Evaluate the event capture against the deliverables you planned. Which presentations have complete usable recordings? Which need corrections? Which companies have received their approved assets? Those questions are more actionable than simply counting all the files the crew produced.
