A portfolio company newsletter should help a defined audience act on useful founder updates. Select a few relevant developments, explain why each matters, and attach a clear next step. Use video when seeing or hearing the founder adds meaning, while keeping the essential information available in the email itself.

## Decide what subscribers expect to receive

A venture capital portfolio newsletter might serve fellow founders, potential customers, job candidates, or partners. Choose a primary readership before asking companies for updates. A technical hiring announcement and a request for customer introductions may both be useful, but not necessarily to the same list.

Write a one-sentence editorial promise. For example: each issue helps operators in our portfolio find practical lessons, relevant opportunities, and specific requests they can answer. Use the promise to accept or decline submissions.

Keep the newsletter's role distinct from a confidential company report. Ask companies to submit material intended for the named readership. Do not forward information from a private founder update merely because it would make an interesting item.

Tell subscribers what they are signing up for and use your existing email program's preference and unsubscribe controls. A partner contact list is not automatically a newsletter audience. The editorial plan should work with the organization's established subscription practices.

Pick a cadence the team can sustain with approved material. A smaller useful issue is preferable to inventing news to fill a calendar slot. If the available items do not match the promise, wait or publish a focused edition.

## Turn submissions into useful editorial units

Ask every contributor for the development, its relevance to the audience, one approved supporting link, the desired action, and a contact owner. Add a date when an opportunity expires or the description needs checking.

A submission such as “we have exciting momentum” needs more work. Ask what specifically changed and who should care. A better item might explain that a company has opened applications for a defined role and describe the work involved. Another might share a permitted lesson from a product implementation.

Give submissions a clear size limit based on your intended issue format. The purpose is to make editing manageable, not to imply that a particular word count improves results. Tell founders that longer source material is welcome when it helps verify the short version.

Use a selection record with audience fit, readiness, action clarity, and previous inclusion. That helps prevent the issue from becoming a recurring showcase for the most vocal companies. Rotate attention while preserving relevance; equal space is not a reason to include an item that has no useful next step.

## Use this issue-planning template

This original worksheet helps an editor make each item earn its place. The sample issue is fictional and does not report real companies or outcomes.

| Issue position | Fictional item | Reader value | Next action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening note | This issue focuses on customer implementation | Explains why the items belong together | Choose the relevant lesson or request |
| Main lesson | A founder explains an ambiguous handoff in a demo | Makes one operational problem concrete | Watch the explanation and use the accompanying checklist |
| Company request | A team seeks feedback from experienced dispatchers | Names the expertise needed | Reply to the designated company contact |
| Opportunity | A portfolio company is hiring an implementation lead | Describes the actual work | Read the approved role page |
| Closing invitation | What implementation question should the next issue address? | Lets readers shape coverage | Send one question to the editor |

Do not treat those positions as mandatory departments. An issue with one strong lesson and one useful request can be complete. Remove a section when it adds neither context nor action.

Draft the subject line after selecting the content. Name the actual topic or opportunity instead of implying urgency that the items do not support. Write preview text that adds information rather than repeating the subject.

## Make video useful without making it compulsory

Include a video when the founder's explanation, a product sequence, or a visible comparison helps the reader understand. Summarize the key point in text so that readers can still act when they cannot watch.

Mailchimp's legacy-builder documentation explains that most email clients do not support embedded video; its video block links a thumbnail to the video's address. [Use Video Content Blocks in the Legacy Builder](https://mailchimp.com/help/use-video-content-blocks/). For your newsletter, a linked preview with a descriptive text link is a practical approach to test in the email system you already use.

Choose an image that accurately represents the linked content. Avoid a play button on a graphic that leads to a registration form without explaining that destination. State whether the link opens a public video, a member resource, or a page requiring access.

Write text such as “Watch the founder explain the dispatch handoff” rather than “Click here.” Add meaningful alternative text to the preview image. Check the email with images unavailable to ensure that the essential information and next step remain understandable.

On the destination page, verify captions, the accompanying explanation, and the contact or action link. The newsletter is only one part of the reading experience.

## Build an approval and correction routine

Assign one editor to consolidate changes. Give each company its item, headline, image, and link for review together. A founder who approves a paragraph may still object to a thumbnail that changes its meaning.

Set the submission and review deadlines in a time zone that every contributor can interpret. For a US-wide portfolio, state the date and zone explicitly rather than saying “end of day.” Offer a later issue when an item is not ready.

Before sending, inspect the entire issue as a recipient. Open every destination, check names and role titles, verify dates, and confirm that requests have an owner who expects replies. Remove expired opportunities and links to unfinished pages.

Keep a record of the exact issue sent and the approved destinations. If an error is material, decide how to correct it in the hosted version and whether recipients need a follow-up. Give companies a single contact for reporting a problem so corrections do not disappear across several conversations.

## Measure whether the issue helped readers act

Track delivered messages, relevant link activity, replies, and the status of the requests you included. Keep the definitions from your email service visible. A request owner can tell you whether replies matched the need, even when the newsletter cannot establish a complete path from reading to action.

Treat open data cautiously. Apple explains that Protect Mail Activity downloads remote content in the background whether or not the recipient engages with the message. [Mail Privacy Protection & Privacy](https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/mail-privacy-protection/). That behavior limits what a tracked open can tell you about actual reading.

Ask a small number of subscribers what they found useful and what they skipped. Compare those answers with the editorial promise. If readers mainly respond to practical lessons, test a lesson-led issue. If requests receive irrelevant responses, make the desired expertise more specific.

Close the loop with contributors. Tell a founder when an issue produced useful questions, and ask permission before turning the answers into a later story. Do not turn a reader response into an invented customer success claim.
