A founder LinkedIn content strategy should connect the founder’s expertise to a defined professional audience and a few recurring questions. Use videos to explain decisions, demonstrate useful work, and invite specific discussion. Give each post a clear purpose, support the conversation after publishing, and judge the strategy by relevant responses and sustainable effort.

## Define the professional audience before the posting rhythm

Choose the people you want to help and the work they are trying to improve. “Startup professionals” is too broad to guide a useful post. “Operations leaders deciding how to handle approval exceptions” gives you a recognizable audience and a set of practical questions.

Write a short audience statement: “I help [role] think through [recurring decision] using experience from [work I actually know].” Keep it narrow enough that the founder can explain why they are qualified to discuss the subject.

Then identify the role of the company in the content. Sometimes a product example helps make an argument concrete. Sometimes the useful contribution is a decision method that applies without the product. Choose the treatment based on the question.

LinkedIn’s guidance says video visibility depends on factors including relevance, quality, and member feedback, and can change over time. Build the strategy around clear value for that audience rather than assuming a posting trick produces dependable distribution. [LinkedIn’s explanation of video visibility](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a10902072).

## Choose a small set of recurring editorial promises

Create three recurring promises that reflect the founder’s experience. For example: explain a difficult decision, show a useful workflow, and discuss a lesson that changed the team’s approach. These are proposed editorial choices, not platform-prescribed categories.

Make each promise specific enough to reject weak ideas. A decision post should compare plausible options and explain when the answer changes. A workflow post should show an action the audience can understand. A lesson post should connect an actual observation to a revised practice.

Avoid treating funding as the main subject of every post after the announcement. It may explain why the team can pursue the next stage of work, but it does not replace the reasoning the audience came to learn.

Keep a list of topics the founder cannot discuss from experience. If a question falls outside that boundary, bring in a qualified colleague, cite an appropriate source, or leave it out. A visible professional presence does not require a position on every industry event.

## Make each video answer one question

LinkedIn’s own video guidance recommends focusing on a single idea, outlining the main points, and using specific examples and an actionable close. Use that as a presentation starting point, then adapt the format to the subject and audience. [LinkedIn’s guide to creating feed videos](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/how-create-videos-for-linkedin-feed).

Write the question first. Under it, note the answer, the reason, and one example. If there are two independent arguments, record two videos. If the answer depends on a condition, include that condition where the recommendation appears.

A talking-head explanation suits a judgment call. A screen demonstration suits a visible task. A simple diagram suits a relationship that the interface hides. Choose the visual because it helps explain the question, not because the calendar needs variety.

Preview the actual post on a small screen. The founder’s face, captions, and any product labels should remain readable together. If a wide product view becomes too small, use a closer crop or a simpler visual rather than expecting viewers to decode it.

## Use this four-post strategy worksheet

The following fictional sequence is for a founder addressing engineering managers responsible for incident handoffs. It illustrates four distinct reader jobs rather than four promised business results.

| Post | Audience question | Concrete contribution | Useful invitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decision explanation | When should a handoff become a meeting? | Compare ambiguity, urgency, and written context | Ask which condition changes the viewer’s choice |
| Workflow demonstration | What should the next owner see first? | Show a sample handoff with open question and responsibility | Invite the viewer to inspect their own handoff |
| Operating lesson | What did our original checklist miss? | Explain a documented revision to the team’s process | Ask about a similar missing step |
| Boundary discussion | When does a short checklist stop being enough? | Describe an exception requiring deeper coordination | Link to a fuller explanation |

Before scheduling, add a source note and a reviewer to each row. Replace the fictional examples with real, permitted material or clearly label a teaching scenario. The founder should be able to explain the reasoning if someone challenges it in the comments.

Try the sequence at a pace the team can support. You can run it over several weeks. The table is a topic plan, not a demand for daily output.

## Make the accompanying post carry its own meaning

Write the first sentence so someone can identify the question without playing the video. Follow with a concise explanation of why the decision matters. Include any condition necessary to interpret the recommendation.

Avoid copying the entire spoken script into a long introduction. The post text can orient the reader, while the video supplies the example. If an important link is needed to apply the lesson, describe what the reader will find there.

Make the invitation specific. “Which part of the handoff usually needs clarification?” gives people a concrete way to contribute. A generic request for engagement does not tell them what experience would be useful.

Keep the founder’s company relationship clear when discussing the product. Use accurate names and avoid implying that a sample workflow is a customer result. If someone else helped supply the example, credit that contribution with permission.

Review the text and video together. A measured spoken answer can become exaggerated under an absolute headline. The overall post should communicate the same scope as the underlying example.

## Plan for conversation after publication

Set aside time to read substantive responses. The founder should answer questions that need their judgment; a teammate can organize topics and route operational questions to the right person.

When someone supplies a credible exception, ask enough to understand it before defending the original advice. The result may be a narrower recommendation or a useful follow-up post. Correct factual errors in a way that people encountering the original can understand.

Do not treat every comment as a sales opening. A person sharing experience may be contributing to the discussion without seeking a vendor conversation. Offer the relevant next step when they express that interest.

Keep a response note containing the question, the founder’s answer, and whether a fuller resource would help. Over time, that record becomes a source of topics grounded in actual professional discussion.

If the founder cannot participate after publication, choose a lower-demand post or publish when follow-up is feasible. The operating plan should account for conversation as well as production.

## Review the strategy with a decision log

After a complete topic cycle, compare what each post was intended to do with the responses it received. Record audience relevance, the specificity of discussion, visits to a relevant destination when available, and the effort required to produce and support the post.

Treat platform counts as observations, not explanations. A widely seen post may attract people outside the intended audience. A smaller discussion may reveal a useful buying question. Neither proves a revenue effect without further evidence.

Choose one adjustment for the next cycle: clarify the audience, narrow the topic, change the example, or improve the destination. Changing all four at once makes it harder to understand what helped.
