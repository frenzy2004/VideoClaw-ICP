A Demo Day social media plan should help a defined audience understand what your startup does before the event and what is worth exploring afterward. Prepare a small set of useful posts, coordinate announcement timing, give each clip enough context, and reserve time to answer the questions those posts create.

## Choose one audience and one useful action

Start by deciding whom the social plan serves. Potential customers may want to see a task completed. Fellow founders may respond to a specific lesson. Investors who missed the event may need a concise company explanation and a route to request more information.

Choose a primary audience for each post. A post that simultaneously asks for customers, investors, hires, press, and partners makes the intended response harder to understand. Other audiences can still benefit without becoming five equal calls to action.

Write an audience card with three fields: what this person already knows, what the post will clarify, and what they can do next. Use it to decide whether a stage photo, product clip, or written explanation is the right material.

Keep the company description consistent across the plan. Adapt the emphasis to the audience, but do not let the customer post promise a product capability that the investor update describes as future work.

## Coordinate what can be announced and when

Confirm the event's public details and the material you are permitted to reuse. Record which announcements have already been made and which require coordination with the program, customers, or other participants.

A practitioner guide hosted by YC explicitly recommends aligning social posts and company announcements with agreed news timing. Use that as a reason to put a release time on your own content plan. [YC-hosted guidance on funding and launch stories](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/a-guide-to-pitching-funding-and-launch-stories/)

Distinguish attendance, participation, selection, and endorsement in your wording. A photo at an event can establish that you were there when supported by the context; it does not establish that everyone pictured recommends your product.

Ask the relevant owner before reusing event footage or another person's appearance. Keep the answer with the asset. If permission is unclear near the deadline, use your own approved product material or a text post instead.

Finally, identify who will check the scheduled posts if the event plan changes. A rescheduled announcement should not leave an old date circulating automatically.

## Build a small before-and-after calendar

The original calendar below is a planning example, not a posting-frequency benchmark. Change the timing to match the event and the material you can prepare well.

| Window | Post purpose | Material | Next action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before the event | Explain the customer task | Short founder explanation or annotated product image | Invite a relevant question |
| Close to the event | Share approved event context | Accurate participation note and official event link | Point to the appropriate viewing or contact route |
| After the event | Show the product action | One complete workflow clip | Offer a deeper demonstration |
| Following days | Answer a recurring question | Focused explanation with evidence | Continue the specific discussion |
| Later, when ready | Share a real change or learning | New product or customer insight | Ask for relevant feedback |

Add an owner, readiness state, and source asset beside each planned post. Do not fill a slot merely because it exists. If the only available material repeats the previous message, use the time to answer comments or improve the next explanation.

A small plan also makes it easier to check whether the sequence develops an idea. The audience should learn something new with each substantive post.

## Select clips that survive without the full pitch

A clip must make sense to someone who did not watch the event. Before choosing the opening, identify the context that existed immediately before it in the original presentation. Restore any information needed to interpret the claim.

For a fictional scheduling startup, “And now it assigns the job” is incomplete. A social viewer needs to know who requested the job, whether a person approves the suggestion, and what is actually shown on screen.

Use this original clip card:

| Field | Prompt |
| --- | --- |
| Viewer question | What will someone understand after watching? |
| Opening context | Which user and task must be named? |
| Visible evidence | Which action or result supports the explanation? |
| Important limitation | What must remain in the edit? |
| Ending | What specific next step fits this viewer? |

Reject a clip if the necessary context cannot fit without making it confusing. A new short explanation may be more useful than extracting an energetic but incomplete moment from the stage recording.

Do not let applause or an enthusiastic introduction substitute for product evidence. Those moments can convey event atmosphere, but they serve a different purpose.

## Review the post as a complete viewing experience

Check the clip, caption, thumbnail, and accompanying text together. They should describe the same product state and next step. A carefully qualified video can become misleading if its headline makes a broader claim.

W3C's caption guidance explains that captions convey speech and relevant non-speech audio, and that automatic output needs accuracy review. Apply that review to the final social edit. [W3C's captions and subtitles guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/)

Play the clip muted and at the size viewers are likely to encounter. Check that the key product action is still understandable and that captions do not cover the result. Then listen to the audio separately to find references that depend on unseen details.

When adapting the frame, inspect the important label, control, and outcome. Cropping a screen can remove the information that explains whose action is being shown. Reframe deliberately instead of assuming the central part of the picture contains everything that matters.

Keep a short written explanation alongside the video so the post offers useful context before playback.

## Plan who will answer the response

Assign a person to read comments and direct requests during a realistic response window. The owner should know which questions they can answer and which need a product or company fact checked.

Prepare a few response notes, not canned praise. One can explain the current product status. Another can point to an approved demonstration. A third can acknowledge a question that requires verification. Adapt each response to what the person actually asked.

Move a conversation to a private channel when it requires account details or information unsuitable for a public thread. Do not ask someone to expose customer data just to demonstrate interest in the product.

Capture recurring questions in a simple log. A repeated question about the same workflow may justify the next post. That gives the calendar a practical feedback loop and reduces pressure to invent another announcement.

## Evaluate usefulness and retire stale material

Define useful outcomes before reviewing platform numbers. You might count relevant questions answered, requested demonstrations delivered, or conversations that identified a clear buyer problem. Record those observations without claiming the social post caused a funding outcome.

Review which posts created confusion as well as interest. If several readers thought a prototype was already available, repair the explanation and consider whether the clip should remain in circulation.

Maintain a refresh trigger for each durable post: changed product behavior, outdated availability, or a broken destination link. Update the surrounding text or replace the asset when the old version no longer represents what you intend to communicate.

For the next event, retain the posts that answered real questions and revise the weak explanations. The useful legacy of a Demo Day social plan is a set of accurate, understandable assets and a clearer view of what people want to know.
