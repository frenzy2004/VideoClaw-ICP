A founder story video should connect a specific encounter with a customer problem to the decisions that shaped the company. Record an interview that explores what happened, what the founder learned, and what changed. Build the final narrative from those answers, retaining uncertainty and checking details before adding supporting visuals.

## Find the decision that makes the story worth telling

Start with a choice, not a biography. The useful question is often why the founder pursued one problem after seeing something others treated as normal. Education and past jobs belong in the story only when they explain that perspective.

Choose a viewer who needs the explanation. A prospective customer may want to know whether the founder understands their work. A candidate may want to understand the problems the team cares about. That choice determines which episode deserves time.

Michael Seibel’s YC guidance describes a startup’s distinctive insight as something grounded in experience, customer conversations, or analysis, and emphasizes specificity over dramatic language. Use that distinction to select an interview subject: a decision the founder can explain in detail. [YC on pitching a company](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-pitch-your-company/).

Write a provisional story question, such as “Why did we stop trying to automate the whole process and focus on one handoff?” Do not write the ending yet. The interview may reveal that your first interpretation was incomplete.

## Prepare an interview that permits unexpected answers

Send the founder a short description of the audience, purpose, and likely topics. Explain which material may become public, who will review the edit, and whether short excerpts may be used later. Agree on topics that are confidential or out of scope.

The Oral History Association recommends preparation, open-ended questions, follow-up inquiry, and respect for the narrator’s own language. Its guidance also calls for contextualizing excerpts and avoiding manipulations of the speaker’s meaning. Those principles are useful for a company interview even though a marketing film has a different purpose from an oral-history archive. [Oral History Association best practices](https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/).

Build an outline of six prompts rather than a list of desired quotes. Reserve space for a surprising answer. If the founder disagrees with the premise, investigate the disagreement instead of steering them toward the planned sound bite.

Choose a quiet setting and record a short sample first. Listen to the sample rather than relying on a moving audio meter. Technical interruptions during an important memory can make a relaxed conversation feel like a performance.

## Ask for scenes, choices, and consequences

Use the following original question sequence as an interview guide:

1. What was happening when this problem first became hard to ignore?
2. Who was doing the work, and what were they trying to finish?
3. What did you initially think the solution should be?
4. What observation made that first answer feel incomplete?
5. Which decision followed, and what did you choose to leave out?
6. What are you still trying to understand?

Follow vague answers with a request for a particular instance. If the founder says customers were frustrated, ask what someone actually did or said, whether the team has permission to share it, and what happened next. A remembered conversation should not become a verbatim quotation unless a reliable record supports it.

Allow pauses. After an answer, ask whether the founder wants to add anything before moving on. Sometimes the explanation that matters arrives after the polished first response.

Keep follow-up questions neutral. “What changed after that decision?” leaves room for a complicated answer. “How did that breakthrough transform the company?” assumes both a breakthrough and a transformation before either has been established.

## Build a paper edit from the actual interview

Review the recording and mark passages by their role in the story: scene, assumption, contradiction, choice, and current perspective. Add timestamps and a brief note about any fact requiring confirmation.

Arrange the passages as text before working on transitions. Read them aloud in order. Does the founder’s reasoning remain understandable when the interviewer’s questions disappear? If an answer begins with “that,” include enough context to identify what it refers to.

Use this fictional edit map for an unnamed startup that helps teams manage supplier documents:

| Story element | Illustrative material | Editorial question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scene | A review meeting stalls over an unclear document status | Can the founder describe the event accurately? |
| Initial assumption | The team first thought the problem was collecting files | Is this a recollection or a documented decision? |
| Changed understanding | Ownership of the next action was unclear | What observation supports this interpretation? |
| Product choice | The team concentrated on review status and responsibility | Can current product footage illustrate that choice? |
| Open question | Different teams still divide responsibilities differently | Does the edit preserve this boundary? |

This is a fictional narrative, not a customer account. Use it to see the relationship between an event and a product decision, then replace it with the founder’s own material.

## Preserve the founder’s voice without preserving every detour

Remove repetition, false starts, and tangents when doing so leaves the meaning intact. Keep words that reveal the founder’s level of certainty. Changing “we began to suspect” into “we knew” creates a different account even if the new sentence sounds cleaner.

Do not join statements from different questions in a way that implies an invented causal relationship. If two useful passages need a bridge, record a short connecting sentence and label it in the edit notes. Ask the founder to review the complete sequence, not merely approve isolated quotes.

Keep distinctive language that helps the audience understand. A precise phrase from the founder’s working vocabulary may be more valuable than a polished slogan. Explain unfamiliar terms once, preferably with a concrete object or action.

Read the final transcript without the person’s name. It should contain choices that could not belong to every startup. If it describes only passion, innovation, and a desire to change the world, return to the interview and find the moment when the founder had to choose.

## Use visuals to clarify time and evidence

Match each visual to its actual role. A current product recording shows present behavior. An old sketch can illustrate an earlier idea. A reconstructed scene explains an event but should be identified as a reconstruction when viewers could mistake it for documentary footage.

Keep dates or stage labels where they prevent confusion. The interface shown today may not have existed during the origin story. A simple transition from “early sketch” to “current product” can make the development understandable.

Avoid decorating a personal account with unrelated customers, logos, or stock footage that implies relationships. If no historical visual exists, let the founder tell the scene or use a clearly illustrative diagram.

Ask for one factual review and one narrative review. The first checks events, names, permissions, and product details. The second checks whether a new viewer can follow the change in thinking. Those reviews answer different questions and both should happen before the final polish.

## End with the work that continues

A useful ending explains what the founder is working toward now and what interested viewers can explore next. The funding round may explain increased capacity, but it need not resolve the story. Leave an honest open question if the team is still learning.

Retain the full interview and the approved transcript so future edits can be checked against context. When producing a shorter excerpt, include enough of the problem and reasoning for it to stand alone.
