Portfolio founder interviews should reveal how a person recognized a problem, made a decision, and learned from the work. Use a consistent core of open questions, then follow the details that make each company different. Agree on the intended audience, recording use, and review process before asking for personal or company stories.

## Choose the interview's editorial purpose

Decide what a reader should understand after the interview. A company introduction, a founder learning story, and a practical lesson about hiring need different questions. Pick one purpose for the session and tell the founder what it is.

For a portfolio series, a useful recurring purpose is understanding a decision in context. That gives an early-stage company something valuable to contribute without requiring a dramatic funding event or a public performance claim. It also makes room for founders in industries where progress is difficult to show in a short product demonstration.

Write a short audience statement before preparing questions: this interview is for founders facing their first customer implementation, or for prospective employees who want to understand the team's work. Avoid the undefined audience “the startup ecosystem.” A named reader makes follow-up questions easier to choose.

Agree on what the session will not cover if the founder raises boundaries. Explain where the finished interview may appear, which excerpts are planned, and who will review them. Resolve those expectations before recording, when there is time for a clear conversation.

## Prepare a question set with room for discovery

The UK government's service research guide recommends open, neutral questions, follow-ups, and concrete stories rather than general accounts of what should happen. [Using In-depth Interviews](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews). That is interview-method guidance; the following question set is an original adaptation for an editorial founder series.

| Core question | What to listen for | Useful follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What problem first made you pay attention? | An observed situation | What was happening when you noticed it? |
| Who was affected, and how did you learn that? | Specific people and evidence | What could you observe directly? |
| What decision did the team need to make? | A real choice | Which alternatives did you consider? |
| What did you try first? | Actual work | Can you walk through one attempt? |
| What changed your understanding? | A turning point | What did you do differently afterward? |
| What remains uncertain? | Limits and open questions | What would help you decide the next step? |
| What could another founder take from this? | A usable lesson | When might that lesson not apply? |

Send the themes beforehand, with a request to bring one relevant example. Do not require polished written answers to every question. You want the founder to remember the experience, not perform a memorized brand statement.

Review public material before the session so you can ask informed questions. Verify names and dates with the founder rather than assuming an old company biography remains current.

## Follow the decision instead of collecting slogans

Start with a concrete scene. If the founder says the team “listened to customers,” ask what one customer was trying to accomplish and what the team learned from the exchange. The goal is to make the work understandable.

Then examine a choice. Ask what alternatives existed at the time and why one seemed reasonable. This prevents hindsight from making an uncertain decision appear obvious. A founder can explain their reasoning without claiming it was the only possible answer.

Leave a pause after a substantial response. Do not rush to the next prepared question when the useful detail has just appeared. If an answer becomes broad, return gently to the example: what happened next, who did that work, or what changed in the process?

Distinguish what the founder observed from what they infer. “We saw three customers ask about setup” and “the market wanted a simpler product” are different kinds of statement. Preserve that distinction in the eventual edit. Ask for the basis of a result without turning the conversation into an adversarial interrogation.

If a claim cannot be verified for publication, shape the story around the documented work or the founder's stated interpretation. Do not add a confident number because the narrative feels unfinished.

## Use a fictional example to practice the follow-up

Imagine a fictional founder of a maintenance scheduling tool says: “Our first pilot taught us that usability matters.” That is a theme, but it does not yet give another founder something to understand or try.

A useful follow-up is: “Which task was difficult during the pilot?” Suppose the founder explains that a technician could not tell who owned a repair request. Ask what the screen showed, what the technician expected, and which change the team made.

The resulting story might describe replacing an ambiguous status with an explicit owner and discussing the revised workflow with the pilot team. It does not need an invented improvement percentage. Its value lies in the connection between observed confusion and a design choice.

Ask one more question: “What would you check before applying that change elsewhere?” That could reveal that some teams share responsibility and need a different approach. The exception makes the lesson more useful, not less persuasive.

Use this practice exercise with interviewers before the series begins. Have one person give a vague answer and another pursue a specific event, choice, and limitation. Review whether the follow-up suggested an answer or invited the speaker's own account.

## Keep an evidence and approval record beside the edit

Create an interview log with recording reference, proposed excerpt, factual details to confirm, supporting material, and review status. This connects the story to its source without cluttering the published article with production notes.

The Oral History Association recommends documenting informed consent, intended uses, and review expectations when planning interviews. [Oral History Best Practices](https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/). Its guidance concerns oral history; for a founder series, it offers a useful starting point for a clear recording conversation through your program's existing process.

When preparing the first edit, identify quotations that depend on omitted context. Keep the qualification, use a longer excerpt, or summarize accurately with approval. Do not join remarks from different questions to create a conclusion the founder did not express.

Ask the founder to check names, chronology, product descriptions, and any factual claims. If another person or customer appears in supporting material, confirm the appropriate use decision separately. Founder approval alone does not resolve every third-party detail.

Keep the original recording distinct from the approved excerpt. Future editors should be able to tell what was recorded, what was cleared, and what remains outside the published version.

## Build a series that makes different experiences visible

Use common fields for the library: topic, company stage at the time, decision discussed, recording date, and intended audience. Organize interviews by the problems they help readers understand, not only by company name.

Vary the contributors and the questions you pursue. Include remote founders, regional programs, and companies with different business models. Do not treat confidence on camera as a proxy for the usefulness of someone's experience.

After publication, ask readers which part helped them think about a similar decision. Ask the founder whether the story represented the work accurately. Use those answers to improve the next interview brief.
