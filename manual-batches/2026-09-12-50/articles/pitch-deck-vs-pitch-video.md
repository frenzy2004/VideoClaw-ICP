Choose a pitch deck when the recipient needs to scan, compare, or revisit business information, and a pitch video when narration or motion clarifies a specific question. Start with what the investor requested. When both are useful, give each a distinct job and keep their facts, dates, and next steps aligned.

## Start with the recipient's task

Write down what the recipient is trying to do with the material. They may be reviewing the company alone, preparing a partner discussion, checking a product behavior, or deciding whether another meeting would be useful.

A deck lets a reader move between sections and inspect a chart at their own pace. A video lets you guide a sequence and explain what changes on screen. Neither property means one format is always more persuasive.

YC's seed-fundraising guide describes a deck that remains coherent as a leave-behind. That is a useful test for material someone will review without the founder present. [YC's seed-fundraising guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-raise-a-seed-round/)

Honor an explicit request first. If an investor asks for the deck, send an understandable deck. You can offer a short supporting recording if it answers a relevant question, but the recipient should not have to watch it to discover where the requested material is.

## Distinguish the assets before comparing them

A live presentation deck supports a speaker. It may use sparse text because the founder supplies context. A follow-up deck must include enough explanation to stand on its own. These are different uses even when the slides share a design.

A pitch video records the company explanation. A product demo records or explains a particular workflow. The video that answers “What does this company do?” may not answer “How does this approval step work?”

Application videos can have their own instructions. YC, for example, separates its founder application video from the product demo and asks for founders speaking in the former. Do not assume one recording fits every submission or follow-up context. [YC's application-video instructions](https://www.ycombinator.com/video/)

Name the asset by its purpose when you send it: company overview, current product workflow, or readable pitch deck. A specific label helps the recipient choose what to open and prevents a short demonstration from being mistaken for the full business explanation.

## Use a question-to-format comparison

The following original comparison focuses on the recipient's work rather than a universal winner.

| Recipient's question or task | Useful starting format | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does the company do, and why might it matter? | Readable deck or concise overview | Clear company description and evidence |
| How does a particular user task work? | Focused demo video | Visible starting state, action, and result |
| Can I review the market and business assumptions? | Deck or written note | Definitions, sources, and assumptions are inspectable |
| Can I introduce this to a colleague? | Short written introduction with the requested asset | Enough context to forward accurately |
| What changed since our last meeting? | Written update with an optional demonstration | Baseline, change, and current status agree |
| Can we discuss several uncertain technical issues? | Live conversation with supporting material | Appropriate owners and verified answers |

Use the table to pick a default, then adjust for the actual request. A person who prefers text should not receive a mandatory video simply because the product is visual.

Consider the maintenance burden as well. If a feature changes frequently, a focused recording may be easier to replace than a long company video containing many screens.

## Make a deck useful without narration

Read the deck silently as if you have never heard the pitch. Identify any slide whose meaning depends on an anecdote, unexplained acronym, or spoken qualification. Add the minimum context required to interpret it correctly.

Sequoia's business-plan guidance includes the customer problem, alternatives, business model, team, and vision. Use those themes to check completeness where they fit your company's stage and the recipient's request. [Sequoia's guide to writing a business plan](https://sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan)

Write chart titles that say what is measured and keep necessary definitions close to the data. Separate current evidence from projections. A graph should not require the founder's voice to reveal that its final section is a plan.

Check the reading order, links, and version date. If the deck includes a demo link, explain which workflow it shows. Keep the company explanation complete enough that the reader can understand the deck even if they choose not to open the recording.

## Make a video answer a bounded question

Begin the recording with the question it answers. For a fictional purchasing startup, that might be how a requester submits an order and a manager reviews it. Show the relevant task, identify the people involved, and finish on the result.

Do not turn the recording into a narrated reading of every slide unless the recipient actually needs that presentation. A short explanation of a difficult mechanism may offer more value than repeating information already easy to scan.

Use prepared sample data and make the product status clear. If the sequence omits setup or waiting time, explain the omission where it changes interpretation. Keep business claims distinct from the behavior visible in the recording.

Include a written summary of the answer and a clear next step. Review captions and ensure the important visual action is also explained in words. The video should remain useful when shared outside the original email, with enough context to identify the company, workflow, and date.

## If you send both, assign each a different job

Create a small handoff map before assembling the message:

| Asset | Job | Must agree with the other asset |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Email introduction | Restore conversation context and state the request | Company description and next step |
| Follow-up deck | Explain the company and its business evidence | Product status, dates, definitions |
| Optional demo | Show the specific workflow under discussion | Current behavior and limitations |

For the fictional purchasing company, the deck might explain the buyer and adoption plan while the video shows the approval interaction. The email can say exactly that. The two assets then complement each other without asking the recipient to consume the same explanation twice.

Maintain one approved facts sheet for shared statements. When a material fact changes, identify every asset that contains it. Update the affected deck slide, narration, caption, and email description together, or clearly label an older recording.

## Run a recipient-side decision check

Open the materials from the final links and follow the route the recipient will take. Confirm that the asset names are clear, permissions work, and the correct versions appear. Test the video playback and inspect the deck without using your presenter notes.

Then answer three questions: Is the requested information easy to find? Does each additional asset earn its place? Can the recipient take the proposed next step without asking where to go?

If the answer is no, simplify the package. You may need only the deck, only a written answer, or a demo followed by a conversation. Choosing less material can be a sound editorial decision when it makes the response complete and easier to use.
