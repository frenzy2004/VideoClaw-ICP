A product launch content calendar should sequence the questions a buyer needs answered before and after release. Use the first week to prepare the problem, product proof, and access details; use the second to launch, explain setup, and respond to actual questions. Assign an owner, dependency, and destination to every item.

## Start with the release conditions

Write down what becomes available on launch day, which audience can access it, and what happens after someone expresses interest. If these answers are unsettled, reserve calendar space for preparation rather than scheduling promises the team may need to withdraw.

The SBA’s marketing guidance recommends connecting a target market, goals, actions, budget, and measurement in a maintained plan. Apply that principle at a smaller scale: every item in this two-week calendar should serve a named buyer decision and have a feasible owner. [SBA’s marketing and sales guidance](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/manage-your-business/).

Choose one primary destination for the release. It might explain access, host a walkthrough, or describe a pilot. Check that it can answer the question raised by the video. A content calendar is incomplete when it schedules attention but leaves the next step undefined.

Treat the dates below as a proposed operating plan. They are not a benchmark for launch success. Move the whole sequence when product readiness changes instead of preserving a date at the expense of accuracy.

## Create three source pieces before multiplying formats

Prepare a problem explanation, a product demonstration, and a short set of access answers. These pieces can support different posts without requiring a new recording every day. Each should have a current source and an owner who can answer factual questions.

The problem explanation names a situation the audience recognizes. The demonstration shows a current workflow under clear conditions. The access answers cover who can use the release, what setup involves, and where to go next.

Keep each piece separable. If availability changes, you should be able to update that answer without rebuilding the problem explanation. If the interface changes, replace the demonstration and check any derivatives that use its screens.

For a Product Hunt launch, the platform’s own guide recommends choosing the day when you are prepared and treats community activity as extending beyond launch day. That is relevant to scheduling readiness and follow-up, without implying that the platform is right for every startup. [Product Hunt’s launch guide](https://www.producthunt.com/launch).

## Use this two-week calendar

This original example assumes a Monday release in the second week. “Internal” entries are work sessions, not public posts. A day without a new video still has a useful purpose.

| Day | Work or asset | Buyer question or team decision | Dependency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday, week one | Internal launch brief | What is available, and for whom? | Release owner confirms scope |
| Tuesday | Problem video | Why does this task become difficult? | Publicly usable example |
| Wednesday | Internal demo capture | Which sequence shows the task clearly? | Stable product and sample data |
| Thursday | Workflow preview | What will I be able to inspect? | Approved demo excerpt |
| Friday | Access explanation | Can my team use this release? | Accurate access destination |
| Saturday | Internal review | Do captions, screens, and claims agree? | Review copy complete |
| Sunday | Internal destination check | Does the next step work? | Published or staged page |
| Monday, week two | Launch video | What is new, and what should I do? | Final release confirmation |
| Tuesday | First-task walkthrough | How do I reach the first useful result? | Current setup sequence |
| Wednesday | Response collection | What is confusing interested viewers? | Comments and support notes |
| Thursday | Question-response video | What does the recurring question mean in practice? | Actual question selected |
| Friday | Fit explanation | When is this workflow appropriate? | Confirmed limitations |
| Saturday | Internal review | What needs clarification or correction? | Response and destination records |
| Sunday | Next-cycle plan | Which unanswered task deserves a full guide? | Team review |

Add the responsible person, review deadline, publication channel, and destination URL to each row. A single founder may own several tasks, but the workload should remain visible.

## Distinguish production dates from publication dates

Work backward from the review deadline. A video needed on Monday cannot first enter factual review on Sunday if the product owner is unavailable. Put capture, editing, fact review, caption review, and final approval on the working calendar.

Use a dependency rule: an asset cannot move to scheduled status until its supporting product behavior and destination are confirmed. This rule helps the team see why a piece is waiting. “Blocked by access details” is more useful than “video not ready.”

Keep a small buffer for a changed interface or an unclear explanation. If time becomes tight, reduce the number of adaptations. Preserve the core demonstration and access information because later pieces depend on them.

Assign a response owner as carefully as a publishing owner. The launch video can create questions that need product or support judgment. Reserve time for that work in advance so the founder is not expected to answer everything between meetings.

## Adapt each asset around a new question

Do not treat a shorter crop as a new editorial idea by default. A preview should introduce the task. A launch piece should explain the release. A setup tutorial should help someone complete the first action. Each can reuse footage while serving a different need.

Write the question at the top of every adaptation brief. Remove material that answers a different question, then add the context required for this version to stand alone. An excerpt beginning halfway through a sentence is not ready simply because it meets a length target.

Keep filenames and destinations linked to their source pieces. If the demo changes, the team should know which previews and tutorials contain the older footage. A simple source column in the calendar is enough to make that relationship visible.

Use the same vocabulary across the sequence. If one asset calls a product state “pending review” and another calls it “approved,” verify whether the workflow changed or the wording drifted. Consistency should reflect actual behavior.

## Build a delay plan before you need it

Decide which items depend on the release date. The launch video, access explanation, and availability language are usually sensitive to a delay. A general problem explanation may remain useful if it does not promise a specific release.

If readiness changes, pause the dependent items and update the source brief first. Check queued posts and already shared previews for dates or promises that need correction. Notify the people responsible for replies so they use the same explanation.

Have one replacement activity available: answer a durable question, explain a design decision, or publish a useful worksheet. Do not disguise an unavailable feature as an educational demonstration. A substitute should make sense on its own.

When the release resumes, recheck the actual product and destination. Approval from the previous week may no longer describe the current interface or access process.

## Evaluate the sequence by what it helped people understand

At the end of the second week, compare the intended buyer questions with the questions people actually asked. Repeated uncertainty about eligibility suggests the access explanation needs work. Confusion about the first action suggests a gap in the walkthrough.

Record relevant inquiries, destination activity, unresolved support questions, and production effort separately. Avoid adding them into one invented launch score. The team needs to know both whether the explanation helped and whether the schedule was sustainable.
