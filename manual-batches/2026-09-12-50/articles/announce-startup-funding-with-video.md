Announce startup funding with a short video that connects the financing to a real customer problem, shows what the company does today, and explains the next step. Start from approved round facts, choose one audience, and pair the founder’s message with a useful demonstration and a written announcement.

## Decide what the announcement should help someone do

Before writing a celebratory opening, finish this sentence: “After watching, a prospective customer should understand ___ and be able to ___.” For a newly funded company, a useful answer might be “understand our supplier approval workflow and request a walkthrough.” That decision gives the video a job beyond repeating the amount raised.

A customer, candidate, and journalist may all encounter the same news, but they need different follow-up information. Choose the primary audience for the main cut. Put the other routes on the accompanying announcement page. This avoids ending a brief film with competing requests to buy, apply, subscribe, and contact the founder.

YC’s seed fundraising guide connects capital to building the company and closes by directing founders back to that work. Use that perspective to frame the raise as resources for a plan. The guide does not establish that any particular financed product has proved its usefulness. [YC’s guide to seed fundraising](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-raise-a-seed-round/).

## Make a fact card before a script

Create one page containing the exact company name, approved round description, participating organizations, announcement date, spokesperson names, and current product availability. Leave out details the team has not cleared. The editor needs a usable set of statements, not a folder of contradictory drafts.

Beside each statement, add the person responsible for confirming it. Ask the product owner to check the demo independently of the person checking financing details. These are different kinds of knowledge. Agreement on the announcement does not automatically validate a sentence about product behavior.

The PRSA Code of Ethics asks its members to communicate accurately, investigate information released for clients, and correct errors promptly. Our practical recommendation is to make those responsibilities visible on the fact card before recording. [PRSA Code of Ethics](https://www.prsa.org/professional-development/prsa-resources/ethics).

Use a separate “planned next” field for ambitions. “We plan to expand our onboarding team” should not become “every customer now gets dedicated onboarding” during editing. If financing language raises a company-specific question, route that sentence to the person handling the company’s financing review rather than improvising an answer in the video.

## Build a four-part announcement with a customer thread

Try four connected beats: the problem, the news, the present-day demonstration, and the invitation. This is a proposed story structure, not a formula for coverage.

Open with a recognizable work situation. Name the person and the obstacle: a procurement lead cannot tell which supplier documents still need review. Then state the funding news plainly. Explain the work it is intended to support in words that make sense to that person.

Move promptly into a current product example. Show the relevant document, its review state, and the next human action. A recording of those steps supports an explanation of the workflow. It does not establish a claim about savings across all customers.

Close by saying who should take the next step and what they will get there. If the product is in a limited trial, the invitation should describe that trial. If viewers can only request information, do not script an immediate start.

As an initial editing constraint, give the demonstration more room than the investor roll call. Read the whole script aloud with the planned screen sequence. Shorten the introduction if the actual work becomes rushed.

## Use this announcement worksheet

The following is a fictional planning example for an unnamed supplier-document startup. It illustrates editorial choices, not company results.

| Decision | Draft entry | Check before recording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary viewer | Procurement lead reviewing supplier submissions | Use language this role recognizes |
| Everyday problem | Missing evidence is hard to distinguish from completed review | Confirm the example reflects an actual supported task |
| News | Approved funding sentence goes here | Copy from the fact card |
| Present proof | Open a sample supplier record and identify an incomplete item | Use the current interface and labeled sample data |
| Next chapter | Expand the team working on document review | Confirm this is an approved plan |
| Invitation | Request a walkthrough of this workflow | Test the destination |

Turn each row into one spoken sentence or visual action. If a row needs a paragraph of explanation, decide whether it belongs on the written page. Do not make the narrator speak every internal review note.

Then try removing the funding sentence temporarily. The remaining video should still explain something useful about the company. Restore the news once that underlying explanation works. This editing exercise helps reveal whether the announcement depends entirely on prestige.

## Record the founder and product as complementary pieces

Record the founder’s explanation in a quiet place, then capture the product separately. This lets the editor keep the founder’s meaning intact while choosing screens that actually support it. Ask for a second take of any sentence that contains an unclear number or an ambiguous promise.

Prepare sample records specifically for filming. Check names, browser tabs, notifications, and account menus before capture. Give the editor a note identifying anything simulated, shortened, or unavailable outside the demonstration environment.

Let the product action finish on screen. When a task includes a waiting period, either show it or identify an edit that skips elapsed time. A polished transition should not change what viewers think the product does.

Make captions part of the review copy. Check company names, financing terms, and product labels manually against the fact card. Watch once on a small screen to see whether the important interface state remains readable. If it disappears under captions, change the framing or use a closer view.

## Coordinate release and keep a correction route

Choose a single written announcement as the reference destination. It should contain the approved news, a concise company explanation, the video, and audience-specific links. Give colleagues that URL along with the approved media so independent posts lead back to the same information.

Assign an owner for each release action: publish the page, verify playback, share the founder post, and answer incoming questions. The order matters more than a theatrical countdown. A viewer who arrives early should find a working next step.

Keep a simple release log with the asset name, version, destination, and owner. If a caption or factual detail changes, the log shows which copies need attention. Save the corrected version with an identifiable date, and explain material corrections wherever people could otherwise retain the wrong understanding.

Prepare replies to predictable operational questions before release: who can use the product, how to see the workflow, and which roles are actually open. Send questions outside the approved facts to the appropriate owner.

## Plan the useful follow-up

After publication, review the questions people ask about the work. If viewers understand the raise but cannot explain the product, the next asset should show the missing workflow. If qualified candidates ask about team responsibilities, make a separate role-specific explanation.

Track destination visits, relevant inquiries, and repeated points of confusion as separate observations. A congratulatory comment is encouraging, but it does not tell you whether a customer understood the offer. Compare what people asked with the intended action written at the start.
