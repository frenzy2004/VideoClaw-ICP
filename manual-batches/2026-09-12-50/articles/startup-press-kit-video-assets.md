A startup press kit should give someone the facts and media needed to describe the company accurately without waiting for a custom reply. Include an approved company summary, a reachable media contact, logos, founder photos, product screenshots, and clearly labeled video assets with captions, context, usage information, and current download links.

## Organize the kit around the recipient’s task

Imagine someone needs to identify the company, understand its product, select an appropriate visual, and ask one follow-up question. Build the kit in that order. A large folder of unlabeled files forces the recipient to reconstruct the story and guess which version is current.

Start with a short index page containing the company description, the subject of the announcement, a date, and a contact route that someone monitors. Beneath it, group materials by use: company facts, people, product, and video. Use plain labels rather than internal project names.

Stripe’s official newsroom asset page provides a concrete example of grouping logos, product screenshots, and executive photos alongside usage information. It is an example of asset organization, not an endorsement or evidence that your startup needs the same volume of material. [Stripe’s newsroom information and assets](https://stripe.com/newsroom/information).

For a newly funded startup, keep the funding announcement easy to find but distinct from the evergreen company summary. A recipient should be able to describe the company without repeating an old round announcement.

## Select a small set of useful video assets

Include a finished company or announcement video when one exists and remains accurate. Give it a preview image and a sentence explaining what it covers. A recipient should know whether they are opening a founder interview, a product demonstration, or a general introduction.

Add a short product sequence that shows a recognizable task. If you offer footage intended for editing into another piece, identify whether it includes narration, music, or on-screen text. A silent product capture may be easier to explain in a recipient’s own format, while a narrated demonstration may be clearer as a standalone reference.

A founder interview excerpt can help when the speaker explains a relevant decision. Include the speaker’s name, role, recording date, and context for the question. Do not provide a quote fragment that requires a missing sentence to preserve its meaning.

Avoid uploading every take from a recording session. Select usable assets and describe them well. Keep raw outtakes and unfinished materials in the team’s working area so they cannot be mistaken for approved public footage.

## Make screenshots and photos understandable without an interview

Caption each screenshot with the task being shown, the product version or capture date, and an explanation of any sample data. Use the same names in the caption and the interface. If the screen is a concept, say so clearly.

Choose screenshots that explain a product action rather than simply showing the largest dashboard. One close view of a review status may communicate more than an entire application window with unreadable labels. Provide enough context to identify what the image represents.

For founder photos, include names in left-to-right order when more than one person appears. Add the photographer credit and the usage instructions the team has confirmed. A filename alone is not a reliable substitute for that information.

Supply the logo versions that the brand owner has cleared, with guidance about backgrounds and spacing. Do not include investor or customer logos merely because they appear in a presentation. Each item should have an identifiable reason to be in the public kit.

## Use an asset register to prevent guessing

This original register is a planning example. Replace the illustrative entries with your own reviewed material.

| Asset | What it explains | Context to attach | Maintenance trigger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Company overview | What the startup does and for whom | Approved description and review date | Positioning changes |
| Founder portrait | Who is speaking for the company | Name, role, photo credit, usage instructions | Role or approved portrait changes |
| Product still | One current user task | Capture date and sample-data label | Interface or workflow changes |
| Demo clip | Starting state, action, and result | Duration, audio status, prerequisites | Demonstrated behavior changes |
| Interview excerpt | Founder’s reason for a decision | Question context and approved transcript | Context becomes misleading |
| Announcement film | The news and its practical meaning | Announcement date and source page | A material fact needs correction |

Add a file location and an owner to each row. The owner should know whether the asset remains accurate and who can authorize a replacement. If nobody can answer those questions, keep the item out until responsibility is clear.

Use names that distinguish purpose and version. “Supplier review demo, September update” is more understandable than “final-final-3.” Preserve stable descriptive links in the kit even when the underlying file changes.

## Pair video with usable text

W3C distinguishes a basic transcript of meaningful audio from a descriptive transcript that also explains necessary visual information. It recommends making transcripts easy to find from the media. [W3C’s guidance on transcripts](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/transcripts/).

For a press kit, place the transcript link beside the video preview. Identify speakers and describe any essential screen state that the narration leaves implicit. Someone reading the transcript should understand the demonstrated task without hunting through an unrelated document.

Keep the text synchronized with the released edit. A transcript of an earlier take may contain a statement the final video deliberately removed. Check the beginning, ending, names, and any numerical claims against the actual file.

Provide a brief plain-language synopsis as well. The synopsis helps a recipient decide whether the asset is relevant; the transcript lets them inspect what it says. Those are different uses, so neither needs to duplicate the other.

Avoid relying on captions burned into a preview as the only available text. Recipients may need readable text for reference, quotation checking, or accessibility.

## Test the kit as an outside recipient

Open the index in a browser session that does not have your company’s account access. Check that previews load and the intended files are available without unexpected permission requests. If access is intentionally restricted, say how to request it and who responds.

Download a sample from each asset group and inspect the actual file. A link can work while pointing to an outdated version. Check filenames, dimensions, audio status, captions, and whether the preview matches the downloaded material.

Ask a colleague unfamiliar with the folder structure to find a founder photo, a current product image, and the announcement facts. Note where they hesitate. Rename categories or move links to fix those points rather than writing a long set of navigation instructions.

Then test the contact route. A press kit is less useful if a recipient cannot resolve a question about an image or quote. Assign a backup person during the announcement period so the route remains usable when the founder is busy.

## Maintain the kit as a public reference

Review the kit after material product, leadership, or company-description changes. Retire obsolete assets from the main index and explain corrections when someone may have relied on inaccurate information. Keep an internal record of what was replaced and when.

Use incoming requests to guide additions. If several people ask for the same product angle, create a clearly explained asset for that need. Do not assume a bigger kit is a better kit; prioritize files that reduce ambiguity or prevent repeated custom work.
