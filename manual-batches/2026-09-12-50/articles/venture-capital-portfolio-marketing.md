Venture capital portfolio marketing works best as a shared support service with clear limits. Give companies access to editorial help, reusable production resources, and specialist advice while letting each company own its message and release decisions. Prioritize requests by audience need, readiness, and available capacity rather than partner visibility.

## Define the service companies can actually request

A portfolio marketing team needs an understandable offer. “We help with content” leaves founders guessing whether they can request a launch film, a script review, or ongoing editing. Write a service menu that describes the help, the inputs needed, and the point at which responsibility returns to the company.

A shared model can include advisory sessions, guided production, and specialist referrals. An advisory session might turn an unclear brief into a recording plan. Guided production might cover a prepared interview and a defined edit. A referral might connect a company with a suitable specialist when the requested work exceeds the team’s remit.

There is a real institutional precedent for operating support: a16z’s Enterprise team describes providing founders with expertise, insights, and an expert network. That statement establishes the broad support model, not a promise about a particular media service or its results. [a16z Enterprise](https://a16z.com/enterprise/).

For your portfolio, publish only the services you can staff. Name exclusions in the menu itself, such as daily channel management or an event requiring an outside crew. Founders can then plan around a concrete offer.

## Sort requests by the work they need

Ask for an audience, a communication task, a target date, available material, and a company approver. Those five inputs help distinguish a production request from a strategy conversation.

Consider three fictional companies. A cybersecurity startup has approved demonstration footage but no clear explanation. A food logistics company wants an event video next week but has not arranged capture. A hiring startup needs help deciding what story to tell. These are different jobs, even if each request arrives as “we need a video.”

Route the first company to an editorial session, the second to a feasibility discussion, and the third to a short discovery exercise. Starting all three in the editing queue would hide the work that still needs to happen.

Use a visible triage record: requested, clarified, accepted, waiting for input, in progress, and delivered. Add a reason to every waiting state. “Needs a named product reviewer” tells a founder how to move forward; “pending” does not.

Give companies a way to challenge a routing decision. A missing detail may reflect an unclear form rather than an unready team. The service should make requests easier to resolve, not create an obstacle course.

## Allocate capacity with a transparent queue

Start with the staff hours and review slots available for the next planning period. Separate time spent preparing a story from time spent producing it. Otherwise, a complicated brief can consume the capacity reserved for several ready assignments.

A practical priority order is deadline relevance, audience value, readiness, and prior access to support. Treat these as discussion criteria rather than a numerical ranking of companies. A time-sensitive customer explanation with approved material may be ready to proceed, while an ambitious brand film needs a later planning slot.

Hold a small amount of capacity for corrections and urgent factual updates. Decide that allowance from actual workload experience. Do not promise same-day support across the portfolio unless there is someone scheduled to deliver it.

For teams spread across US time zones, define a response window in business days and give dates with time zones when a deadline is exact. Offer asynchronous feedback where a live session would exclude a participant. A service that is available only at the platform team’s convenient hour may be less accessible than its menu suggests.

## Write a service agreement for each assignment

The following original worksheet makes one assignment reviewable without requiring a lengthy proposal. Complete it together with the company before production starts.

| Assignment field | What to agree | Fictional example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Company task | The explanation the audience needs | Show a dispatcher how an exception is assigned |
| Shared support | What the platform team will do | Outline review, one recording session, one defined edit |
| Company inputs | What must arrive first | Prepared demo, approved terminology, named reviewer |
| Review boundary | What each reviewer decides | Company checks facts; platform checks the agreed brief |
| Delivery condition | What counts as complete | Approved explanation with reviewed captions and a working destination |
| Change trigger | What requires replanning | A different audience, workflow, or recording session |
| Reuse decision | Where future use is allowed | Company support page; other uses require a new decision |

This is a planning example, not a contract or measured service promise. Adapt it to your organization’s existing agreements.

Before accepting the work, ask the company approver to describe the intended result in their own words. If they expect a sales campaign while the editor expects a tutorial, resolve the difference now. Agreement about file length alone does not settle the purpose.

## Share production resources without pooling company information

Keep reusable prompts, naming conventions, review checklists, and blank visual templates in the common resource library. Keep customer examples, unreleased material, and company-specific review discussions in the appropriate company workspace.

NIST describes its Privacy Framework as a voluntary tool for identifying and managing privacy risk. [NIST Privacy Framework](https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework). Applied to a shared media service, a useful operational question is who needs access to each input and for what task. The folder choices here are recommendations for answering that question, not a claim that a particular setup meets a regulatory standard.

Give an outside editor only the material needed for the assigned work through the organization’s approved sharing process. Record who can grant access and who will close it when the assignment ends. Do not use another company’s material as a sample simply because it is easy to find.

Maintain separate records for source footage and approved deliverables. A final public clip may be reusable while its source recording contains excluded discussion. Clear labels prevent an editor from treating the whole interview as an approved asset library.

## Make the handoff useful to the company

Deliver the asset with a short usage note: intended audience, approved description, destination, version date, and owner for corrections. Include the agreed supporting text and caption material. Ask the company to confirm that its actual publishing destination works.

Then separate completion from adoption. An assignment can be delivered on time and still fail to meet the company’s need. Follow up on whether the team used it, what prevented use, and what further questions viewers asked. Record the answer without claiming that the asset caused a sale or investment.

Review patterns across assignments. Repeated requests for basic story help suggest a workshop. Frequent caption corrections suggest a better terminology intake. Repeated scope changes may indicate that the service menu is too broad.
