Good customer testimonial video questions uncover a specific situation, the customer’s actual use of the product, and what changed under identifiable conditions. Ask open questions about real events, follow up on vague answers, and verify objective claims separately. Plan permission, context, and the final review before recording anything for publication.

## Choose a customer with a story they can explain

Start with the experience, not the customer’s willingness to praise you. Look for someone who can describe a particular task, how they handled it before, what they tried, and how the product fits into their work today.

Ask who actually used the product and who can authorize the company’s participation. Those may be different people. A senior sponsor can explain the business context, while a daily user may supply the clearest account of the workflow. Decide whether the film needs one voice or both.

Agree on the intended use before recording: a website story, a social excerpt, a sales reference, or another defined format. Clarify who checks quotes, screens, logos, and numerical claims. Use the company’s approved permission process; a friendly conversation alone leaves too many practical questions unanswered.

Give the participant an outline of topics, not desired answers. Ask them to bring one example they can discuss publicly. If their strongest example contains sensitive information, prepare a permitted way to explain the task without exposing it.

## Ask questions in the order the work happened

The GOV.UK service manual recommends open, neutral interview questions, follow-ups, and a focus on real examples rather than generalities. Its guidance concerns user research; the question design is useful here, while testimonial publication requires its own permission and claim review. [GOV.UK’s in-depth interview guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews).

Use this original sequence as a starting point:

1. What were you responsible for when you first considered the product?
2. Tell me about a specific occasion when that task became difficult.
3. How were you handling it at the time?
4. What made you look for another approach?
5. What did you need to check before trying this product?
6. Walk me through the first real task you used it for.
7. What did your team have to change or learn?
8. What is different in the workflow now?
9. Which parts still require effort or another tool?
10. Who would find this approach useful, and under what conditions?

You do not need to ask all ten mechanically. Follow the story and return to missing details at a natural pause. The goal is a usable account, not a completed questionnaire.

## Turn vague praise into a concrete explanation

When someone says a product is easier, faster, or better, ask what they are comparing and what they actually observed. Do not supply a number or suggest the answer you hope to hear.

| Initial answer | Useful follow-up | What it clarifies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “It is easier to use.” | Which task feels easier, and what do you do differently? | The actual workflow change |
| “Our team loves it.” | Who uses it, and what have they told you? | Attribution and scope |
| “It saves time.” | Have you measured that, or is it your impression? | Evidence versus opinion |
| “Setup was simple.” | What was already prepared, and who helped? | Preconditions |
| “We are more organized.” | Can you describe a recent example? | A visible behavior |
| “Everyone should try it.” | What kind of team is it best suited to? | A useful boundary |

This table is an editorial interview aid. It does not make an unsupported claim publishable. If a participant cannot substantiate a numerical statement, record the uncertainty and return to it after the session.

Leave room for mixed answers. A customer who explains a useful change and a remaining inconvenience gives the audience more information than a sequence of unqualified superlatives.

## Separate customer opinion from product claims

The FTC explains that endorsements must reflect honest opinions and cannot be used to make claims the marketer could not make directly. It also addresses unexpected material connections and presentations of exceptional results. A testimonial therefore needs more than an enthusiastic speaker. [FTC endorsement guidance and questions](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking).

During review, classify statements as personal opinion, observed workflow, or objective result. “I prefer seeing the owner beside each request” describes a preference. “Our approval time fell” describes an outcome that needs a definition, period, and supporting record before you decide how to present it.

Ask what else changed during that period. A new process, additional staff, or a different workload may matter. Do not edit those conditions away and imply the product alone caused the result.

Document any benefit or relationship associated with participation and have the relevant reviewer determine how it should be explained. The same FTC guidance notes that advance expectations of a benefit, including being featured in advertising, can matter. Do not assume that an unpaid interview needs no disclosure consideration.

## Use a fictional example to test your question design

Imagine an operations coordinator at a fictional company discussing a supplier-document tool. Their first answer is, “Now everything is in one place.” That sounds positive but leaves the viewer unable to understand the task.

The interviewer asks which information previously lived elsewhere. The coordinator explains that requests and ownership notes were separate. Asked to describe a recent review, they explain how they now open a supplier record and see who handles an incomplete document.

A useful edit could connect the earlier confusion, the current action, and the coordinator’s preference for seeing ownership clearly. It does not need an invented time-saving percentage. A labeled sample record can illustrate the workflow if public use of a real record is unsuitable.

Before using the sequence, ask the coordinator whether it accurately represents their experience and ask the product owner whether the screen reflects current behavior. Their approval answers different questions. One confirms the customer’s account; the other checks the product illustration.

Run this exercise with a colleague before the real interview. If your questions keep generating adjectives instead of examples, revise the prompts.

## Edit without manufacturing an endorsement

Keep the question context available to the editor. An answer to “What would you like the product to do next?” must not become a description of current functionality. Mark hypothetical statements and future wishes in the transcript.

Cut repetition when the meaning survives. Retain conditions that affect interpretation, such as “for our small team” or “after we reorganized the process.” If a concise excerpt requires removing those conditions, choose another passage or include the context in the surrounding explanation.

Use supporting footage that matches the account. A demonstration can show the task, but unrelated celebratory imagery should not imply a result the customer never described. Identify recreated or sample visuals when the distinction matters.

Send the exact proposed cut and accompanying text through the agreed review. Include the headline and any quote card, because an accurate spoken answer can become misleading under an exaggerated headline. Keep the approved versions together.

## Turn the finished story into a useful buyer resource

Publish enough context for a reader to understand who is speaking, what work they do, and which product use they describe. Provide captions and a readable account of the example. A sales colleague should be able to explain when the story is relevant before sharing it.

Review the asset when the customer’s role, product use, or featured workflow changes. If you create short excerpts, check that each retains the conditions and disclosures needed for its own presentation.
