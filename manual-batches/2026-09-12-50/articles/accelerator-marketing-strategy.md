An accelerator marketing strategy should connect the program’s public promise with useful founder support. Build a media program around specific audience questions, give companies a manageable way to contribute, and distribute approved stories where those audiences already look. Judge the program by useful participation and follow-up, alongside its reach.

## Decide whose next decision the program serves

An accelerator can speak to applicants, current founders, mentors, customers of portfolio companies, and regional partners. Each audience needs different information. A prospective applicant may want to understand the weekly workload. A founder’s customer needs to understand a product. A mentor needs a clear way to contribute expertise.

Start your strategy with one priority audience and one decision. For example: help founders considering the next cohort decide whether its working style fits them. That choice creates a reason to show a real workshop, a typical assignment, and an honest account of founder participation. A compilation of celebrations would answer a different question.

Techstars describes its accelerator experience in terms of mentorship, workshops, and a network over a three-month program. Those are concrete program elements an operator can explain through media, without treating another accelerator’s format as a universal model. [Inside a Techstars Accelerator](https://www.techstars.com/accelerator-hub).

For your own program, write down the experience you can actually offer. Use that description to select stories. If individual coaching is occasional, do not make it appear to be a daily entitlement.

## Connect founder needs with public stories

Invite each participating company to name a communication problem: explaining an unfamiliar workflow, recruiting for a specific role, teaching customers a first step, or answering a recurring question. Select a media assignment that helps with that problem and can also demonstrate the program’s support.

Keep the company’s useful output explicit. A founder who needs an onboarding explanation should leave with a coherent customer asset. The accelerator can tell a separate story about the workshop that helped develop it, with the company’s agreement. Combining both purposes into one video often produces an unclear audience and competing calls to action.

Create a short intake conversation around three questions. Who needs to understand something? What can you show that makes it understandable? Where will you use the finished piece? When the answers are vague, spend the session clarifying them before booking a shoot.

Offer a written or audio-first preparation route for founders who need more time to organize their thoughts. For distributed US cohorts, rotate live session times and provide an asynchronous review option. Participation should depend on having useful experience to share, not proximity to the program’s office.

## Use a cohort media charter

The following worksheet is an original planning tool. Complete it before adding publishing dates. It turns a broad startup accelerator marketing strategy into a bounded service that founders and staff can recognize.

| Charter field | Decision to record | Fictional program example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Priority audience | Who needs help deciding? | Founders considering a remote manufacturing cohort |
| Audience question | What do they need to understand? | How hands-on is the program between group sessions? |
| Founder benefit | What usable asset will participants receive? | A short explanation of one customer workflow |
| Contribution | What will the founder provide? | A prepared example, recording time, and factual review |
| Program contribution | What will staff provide? | Interview prompts, editorial help, and distribution |
| Publication owner | Who clears each version? | Company lead for its story; program lead for program facts |
| Learning signal | What will change the next cycle? | Questions applicants still ask after watching |

The example is fictional and contains no measured results. Replace its choices with the actual cohort’s needs. A regional university program may prioritize explaining eligibility to local researchers; a national industry cohort may prioritize showing how founders work with domain mentors.

The charter should also name a stop condition. If the founder benefit disappears and the assignment becomes only a promotional obligation, revisit the brief. A useful story should justify the participant’s effort.

## Plan a small sequence around the program calendar

Start with three connected moments: orientation, an applied working session, and a reflection after the work. This is a recommended pilot structure, not a required accelerator schedule. It gives the editor something more specific than a request to keep social channels busy.

At orientation, record the question a founder is trying to answer. During the working session, capture an explanation or permitted example of the task. At reflection, ask what changed in the founder’s approach and what remains unresolved. These moments can support a coherent story even when there is no dramatic milestone.

Schedule review dates alongside recording dates. Reserve time for a founder to check a description, a customer to approve an appearance when needed, and an editor to make corrections. Avoid building the calendar on the assumption that all companies will become ready at once.

Plan accessibility at the same point. W3C recommends incorporating accessibility requirements and responsibilities into media planning and production. [Planning Audio and Video Media](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/planning/). For this program, assign a person to review captions and identify visuals that need spoken explanation before the first session.

## Match distribution to the question

Choose a primary destination for each piece. A program experience story may belong beside application information. A founder’s product explanation may belong on the company’s own page. A mentor lesson may be more useful in the cohort resource library than in a public feed.

Then write the surrounding context. State who the piece is for, what it helps them understand, and what they can do next. A video about workshop participation should link to accurate program details. A customer workflow video should lead to the company’s appropriate next step, with its approval.

For a national portfolio, allow companies to choose channels suited to their audience. An industrial buyer, a school administrator, and a developer may encounter information in different places. The shared program can standardize the preparation process while leaving channel selection to the people who know those readers.

Use a distribution record with destination, owner, publication date, and correction contact. When an application deadline or product description changes, that record tells the team where an outdated version might still be visible.

## Review usefulness before expanding the program

After the pilot, speak with contributors and intended viewers. Ask founders whether they used the asset, what required unexpected effort, and what support they would request again. Ask viewers what became clearer and what information they still needed.

Look for specific changes you can make. If applicants still cannot explain the time commitment, the next story should show a typical week. If founders receive a finished file but never use it, examine the chosen destination and next step. If reviews routinely stall, reduce the number of simultaneous assignments.

Do not let one unusually visible company define the whole program. Record participation across the cohort, including companies that declined or needed a different format. That information helps you decide whether the service fits the program you actually run.
