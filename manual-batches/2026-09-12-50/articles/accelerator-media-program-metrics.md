Evaluate an accelerator’s media support program by tracking who receives help, what usable assets are completed, how companies use them, and which audience actions follow. Define each measure and its denominator before reporting. Keep production activity, audience behavior, and company outcomes separate so the scorecard supports decisions without overstating impact.

## Start with the decision your report must support

A platform lead may need to decide whether to repeat a workshop, add editing capacity, change the intake process, or discontinue an unused format. Name that decision before selecting metrics. A report about every available number can still leave the team unable to choose its next action.

The Kauffman Foundation’s introduction to accelerator measurement explains that founders, program managers, investors, and other stakeholders may have different performance objectives. [Measuring Accelerator Performance](https://www.kauffman.org/reports/measuring-accelerator-performance/). For a media support program, translate that distinction into a clear reporting purpose.

For example, a staff-capacity decision needs information about demand, effort, waiting time, and completed work. A decision about a tutorial series needs information about use, comprehension, and the next questions viewers ask. Fundraising totals across the cohort do not answer either operational question on their own.

Write a reporting agreement with the decision owner, period covered, participating companies, available data, and known gaps. Define whether the report covers one workshop, a cohort, or an ongoing portfolio service. Keep those populations consistent when comparing results over time.

## Measure the service from request to actual use

Start with a small set of measures tied to the work. Count eligible companies, companies accepting support, assignments completed, approved assets, and companies confirming use. Those stages show where an apparently busy program may lose momentum.

Define completion carefully. A recording session held is different from an approved video delivered. An approved video delivered is different from a company using it at the intended destination. Give each stage a distinct label and date.

Add effort and delay only where the team can record them consistently. Separate staff work from time waiting for company input. If founder time is not measured, say so when reporting production effort. Avoid describing staff hours as the total cost of the program.

Use this original metric dictionary as a starting worksheet.

| Measure | Definition to adopt or revise | Useful decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Participation | Companies accepting support divided by eligible companies | Is the offer reaching the intended cohort? |
| Approved completion | Participating companies receiving an approved asset divided by participating companies | Where does delivery stall? |
| Confirmed use | Companies confirming use divided by companies receiving approved assets | Does the handoff fit company needs? |
| Review delay | Time from review request to recorded company response | Should review scheduling change? |
| Relevant follow-up | Audience responses matching the asset’s stated next step | Is the content prompting useful questions? |
| Staff effort | Recorded staff hours for a defined set of assignments | Is the service scope manageable? |

Treat these as proposed definitions, not industry benchmarks. If one company receives several assets, keep company-level coverage separate from asset-level output.

## Keep audience measures tied to their source

For each destination, record the metric name, source, reporting window, and relevant counting rule. A platform view, a website visit, and a form submission describe different actions. Do not add them together and label the total “people reached.”

Google Analytics lets an organization mark a collected event as a key event when it represents an important action. [About Key Events](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9267568?hl=en). For a program resource page, choose an action that matches the page’s job, then verify that the underlying event represents what its label says.

A clicked resource link is not necessarily a completed download. A submitted form is not necessarily a qualified request. Where possible, connect the observed action with a later owner review, keeping each status separate.

For video, inspect retention patterns alongside the content. YouTube explains that a spike can reflect rewatching or sharing, including rewatching because a section was unclear. [Measure Key Moments for Audience Retention](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en). Watch the relevant section before deciding that a spike proves the explanation worked well.

When companies use different channels, present their measures in separate columns or grouped views. Compare similar assets and periods when the definitions permit it. Keep unavailable analytics marked as unavailable.

## Work through a fictional cohort example

Imagine a fictional media program with 20 eligible companies. Twelve accept support, nine receive an approved asset, and seven confirm that they used it. The other two recipients have not answered the follow-up. These numbers are illustrative and are not results from an actual program.

Participation is 12 divided by 20, or 60 percent. Approved completion among participants is nine divided by twelve, or 75 percent. Confirmed use among approved recipients is seven divided by nine, approximately 78 percent. The remaining two recipients have unknown use status; they should not automatically be labeled non-users.

Now ask what decision follows. The eight companies that did not participate may have declined, missed the invitation, or needed a different service. The three participants without approved assets may be waiting on review or still in production. Those are questions for the operating record, not conclusions supplied by the percentages.

Suppose all seven confirmed users came from one workshop group. That pattern might justify investigating whether the workshop prepared better inputs. It does not establish that the workshop caused adoption: attendance, readiness, company stage, and other differences could also matter.

Add a brief context note to the report: the follow-up period, whether every recipient was contacted, and which statuses remain open. A reader should be able to reproduce the calculations and understand what they leave unresolved.

## Use qualitative evidence to explain the numbers

Ask a few repeatable questions after delivery. Where did the company use the asset? What work did it replace or support? What stopped it from being used? What did viewers still need explained? Keep the response connected to the assignment.

Distinguish a founder’s report from an independently observed event. “The founder said the video helped prepare a customer conversation” is useful feedback. It does not prove that the video caused a contract. Preserve that distinction when summarizing the program to leadership.

Include examples of friction as well as positive comments. An editor may discover that a video was approved but the company did not have a suitable destination. Another founder may have needed a written resource more urgently. Those findings can change the service menu.

For a national portfolio, review whether geography, time zones, company stage, or participation format affected access to support. Use the information to improve scheduling and delivery options. Avoid publishing small-group comparisons that reveal individual company information without an appropriate basis.

## Turn the scorecard into a short decision meeting

Before the meeting, select the two or three findings that bear on the named decision. Attach the metric definitions and a concise explanation of missing data. Keep the complete working record available for anyone who needs to inspect a calculation.

End with an action, owner, and review date. If approvals stall, reserve review slots earlier. If completed assets go unused, improve destination planning and the handoff. If requests exceed capacity, narrow the service or adjust resources based on observed work.

Keep the next reporting period comparable where possible. When a definition changes, mark the change and explain whether earlier data can still be compared. Do not quietly replace the denominator because the new percentage looks better.
