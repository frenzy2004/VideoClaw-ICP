To record a product demo for a pitch, prepare a clean example account, define one complete task, test capture and audio, then record the actions and result. Review the delivery file for legibility, accurate narration, sensitive information, and captions. Keep a tested copy ready for the intended meeting or follow-up.

## Prepare a capture brief before opening the recorder

Write a brief with the viewer, the question, the starting state, the action, and the finish. A recording session becomes easier to control when you know the exact product behavior you must capture.

For a fictional customer-support product, the question might be how an agent assigns an unresolved request to a specialist. The starting state is an open request. The finish is the same request with the new owner and a visible handoff note. That is enough scope for one recording.

List the screens required to connect those states. Put optional features in a separate list so they do not interrupt the capture. Decide whether the recording will include live narration, a later voiceover, or a combination.

Also record the current product version and the date. These details help you identify which clip needs replacing when the interface or behavior changes before the next pitch.

## Clean and reset the demonstration environment

Use a dedicated sample account with fictional names and appropriate example content. Avoid relying on postproduction blur as the first defense against exposing real customer information. Cleaning the environment beforehand also makes the story easier to follow.

Inspect the whole capture area: browser tabs, address bar, bookmarks, account menu, sidebar, notification banners, and any desktop visible during switching. Close unrelated applications and pause notifications for the session. Check that the example does not reveal internal project names or private links.

Write down how to restore the starting state. A repeatable reset may involve reopening a sample request, restoring its status, and removing a previous handoff note. Without that reset, a second take can accidentally show a different sequence.

Run the workflow once without recording. Confirm that the actions still work and that the final state is suitable to show. If the behavior differs from the brief, revise the brief before capturing it.

## Choose capture settings and test the sound

On a Mac, Apple's Screenshot controls open with Shift-Command-5. Apple documents selecting a capture area, choosing a microphone in Options, and stopping the recording from the menu bar. Available controls vary by macOS version. [Apple's instructions for recording the screen](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102618)

Choose the capture area that includes all necessary actions while keeping the important text readable. Make a short test rather than assuming that the preview matches the saved file. Play it back and check the pointer, menus, text, and spoken explanation.

If you need application sound, verify that your chosen recording setup actually captures it. Selecting a microphone should not be treated as proof that every sound from the computer will be included.

Listen for room noise, clipping, and changes in level when you turn toward the screen. Move the microphone or change the recording position if needed. A simple quiet setup with intelligible speech is more useful than elaborate equipment that has not been tested.

## Record in complete, repeatable segments

Use the original capture sheet below to plan takes and preserve the important transitions.

| Segment | What must be visible | Reset or review note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orientation | Sample request and its current owner | Confirm the same request appears throughout |
| Decision | Agent selects the specialist | Keep the selection readable |
| Handoff | Agent adds the note and confirms | Show the actual confirmation action |
| Result | Updated owner and handoff note | Hold long enough to inspect both |
| Explanation | Any limitation and next step | Match the product's current behavior |

Start recording before the first necessary action and leave a little stillness after the result. These handles make it easier to trim the clip without cutting away useful context.

Keep pointer movement deliberate. Say what the action accomplishes rather than narrating every click. If narration distracts you from accurate capture, record the workflow cleanly and add the explanation afterward, matching it carefully to the footage.

When a take goes wrong, note what happened and reset. Do not keep navigating through unrelated screens in the hope that a confusing take will become usable.

## Edit for clarity while preserving the sequence

Remove obvious dead time and mistakes, but retain the actions that cause the result. A viewer should be able to understand who made a decision and what the product did in response.

If processing or another person's response occurs between scenes, identify that transition. Do not let an edit imply that a manual handoff or delayed event happened automatically. Keep a short note of any removed waiting period for the final reviewer.

Enlarge or highlight a key field only when it helps the viewer inspect the point being discussed. Check that zooming does not hide a condition or label that changes the meaning. Avoid using a close-up of the final result as a substitute for showing how it was reached.

Compare the finished narration with the recording. Words such as “always,” “instantly,” and “automatically” deserve particular attention because they can make a broader claim than the shown example supports. Narrow the wording when the evidence is narrower.

## Review captions, details, and comprehension

W3C says automatically generated captions need accuracy verification and often editing. Treat them as a draft to review against the final soundtrack. [W3C's guidance on captions and subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/)

Check product names, field labels, numbers, and words that reverse meaning. If you change the voiceover, revisit the affected captions. Play with captions enabled and confirm they do not obscure the specialist name or handoff note in the example.

Then review the image independently of the sound. Pause on menus and transitions to inspect for private information. Check the result at the intended viewing size, rather than relying only on a large editing monitor.

Give the clip to a colleague who did not record it. Ask what task was completed and which actions were performed by the user. A mistaken answer points to a specific missing cue. Repair that cue before adding more explanation elsewhere.

## Prepare the file for its actual use

Name the approved file with the workflow and version date. Keep the capture originals separately so a small correction does not require repeating the entire session. Record who approved the final wording and product depiction.

For a live meeting, test playback on the device you will use. If you intend to play locally, open the local copy without relying on a web connection. Prepare a brief spoken explanation of the workflow in case playback is unavailable.

For an asynchronous follow-up, test the recipient's viewing route and include a short description of what the recording answers. Link to the approved version, and make sure the surrounding message does not claim more than the video establishes.

Recheck the clip when the relevant product behavior changes. An older recording can remain useful if its date and limitations are clear, but it should not silently become the demonstration of the current experience.
