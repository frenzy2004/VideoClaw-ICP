Choose a stock footage license by matching one identified asset to its purchaser, client, placement, and intended reuse. Do not approve a clip solely because a vendor calls it royalty-free. Record the applicable terms and unresolved questions before purchase, then retain evidence that connects the finished video to the licensed asset.

## Name the purchaser and the intended client

A freelancer finding a clip is not necessarily the same as your company holding the relevant entitlement. Ask which account will make the purchase, which legal entity the account represents, and how the library permits work for a client.

Write the actual company name in the production order. “For our customer” leaves ambiguity when an agency has several clients, a contractor operates under a different business name, or the same template is reused across accounts.

[Adobe Stock's license terms summary](https://stock.adobe.com/license-terms) permits client transfer under its stated conditions and requires separate licensing for each client. Treat that as an example of why the relationship matters, not as a rule shared by every library.

Ask the supplier to identify the relevant provision and provide the asset receipt or license record available under its workflow. If the business needs to license directly, arrange appropriate contractor access without sharing personal account credentials. Clarify this before the editor commits the clip to an approved sequence.

## Describe the use more precisely than commercial

“Commercial video” can conceal several distinct plans. The initial project may be a website film, while a colleague expects to put the same clip into paid advertisements, a trade-show display, and a downloadable template.

List the actual planned placements and audiences. Include whether the clip stays embedded in a finished production or whether someone expects access to the source asset. Record likely adaptations, additional clients, and any resale or distribution of editable materials.

Adobe's summary restricts standalone asset distribution and distinguishes editorial-only content. Those conditions demonstrate why “we bought the file” does not answer every later-use question. Avoid converting a short summary into your own interpretation of the full agreement.

Give the licensor a concrete scenario when asking for help. “May our named company publish this edited film in these placements, with this agency preparing the files?” is easier to answer than “Is this business safe?” Ask for a written response tied to the asset and relevant license.

## Use a license-to-use matching form

The following original form is a procurement aid, not a legal opinion. Complete one record for each shortlisted asset before authorizing the purchase.

| Field | What to record | Unresolved question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset identity | URL, asset ID, contributor and preview | Is this the exact clip in the edit? |
| License designation | Applicable license and dated terms | Is it editorial-only or commercially available? |
| Buyer of record | Account holder and purchasing entity | Can the intended client rely on this purchase? |
| Intended client | Named company and project | Does another client need another license? |
| Access | Editor and any subcontractor | What access or sharing is permitted? |
| Placements | Website, ads, event or other named use | Are there channel or context restrictions? |
| Later reuse | New edits, new projects, source delivery | Which uses need clarification or a new purchase? |

End the form with one decision: buy, clarify, or replace. “Clarify” should name the person asking, the exact question, and the date an answer is needed.

Do not use a numerical safety score. One unresolved restriction can be decisive even when every other field is complete. A signed internal checklist also does not alter the licensor's terms.

## Check people, property, and the surrounding claim

Recognizable people and places deserve a separate review from the technical license selection. Ask what release information is associated with the asset and what the library says about its use.

[Adobe's usage and licensing guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/stock/web/common-questions/usage-licensing.html) explains that commercial assets containing recognizable people have model releases, while editorial assets are not cleared for commercial use. Apply that distinction to the asset's actual designation; do not infer clearance from a professional appearance or a search filter alone.

Then review the complete scene. A clip of a person working at a laptop could be neutral illustration, but an adjacent caption could imply they are your employee, customer, or endorser. A sensitive subject can also change the question the licensor needs to answer.

Have the person responsible for the business statement examine the preview in context. If rights, privacy, publicity, or advertising issues remain unresolved, consult the licensor and an appropriately qualified adviser. This guide helps collect the facts; it does not declare any particular use lawful.

## Preserve evidence without redistributing the library

Keep a small evidence package alongside the production record: asset ID, purchase date, account or purchasing entity, applicable license version, receipt, and any written clarification. Also record where the asset appears in the final cut.

The business does not necessarily need a copy of every source file to maintain that record. Distinguish entitlement evidence from redistribution of the raw library asset. Ask what the license permits your editor to deliver and what you may retain or pass to a replacement contractor.

If a vendor sends only a screenshot of a search result, request the actual purchase record. A thumbnail can identify an attractive candidate without proving that anyone licensed it. Likewise, a general subscription invoice may not connect a specific download to a client project.

Set a practical owner for these records. The goal is that a future editor can locate the relevant information without relying on a departed freelancer's memory or requesting access to their personal account.

## Make the final choice before the edit depends on it

License review becomes more expensive when a disputed clip is already central to the story, music timing, and stakeholder approval. Resolve the sourcing decision while replacement remains straightforward.

Ask the producer to keep a fallback candidate for any material awaiting clarification. If the alternative changes the visual story, note that tradeoff rather than quietly substituting it at export.

Before accepting the finished video, reconcile the footage list against the approved purchase records. Remove rejected previews and identify any late substitutions. A final asset inventory should correspond to the actual delivered cut, not an earlier storyboard.

Your purchase decision is complete when the named parties understand the intended uses, the evidence is available, and material uncertainties have an owner or have been removed by replacing the clip. That is a more useful outcome than collecting a folder of license PDFs nobody has connected to the work.
