Pitch a startup story by identifying a real change, explaining why it matters to a particular publication’s readers, and offering evidence the journalist can inspect. Keep the first message focused on one angle and one next step. Treat funding as background when the customer consequence is the more useful story.

## Start with a change outside the company

A new budget, an enthusiastic board meeting, or a completed internal project may matter enormously to your team. The question for a press pitch is what changed for someone outside it. Write that change before writing a subject line.

Imagine a fictional startup called Fieldnote, which makes software for maintenance teams. Its founder has three possible stories: the company moved offices, its team finished a new scheduling interface, and customers can now coordinate a repair across companies without sharing an entire internal workspace. The third development has a clearer external consequence. It changes how people can collaborate, not just how the startup describes itself.

This distinction fits Michael Seibel’s emphasis on timely developments in [YC’s startup press guidance](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/getting-press-for-your-startup). Use it as a starting question, not a promise that any milestone will receive coverage.

Ask what happened, who is affected, and why someone would care this week. If the answer depends entirely on the founder’s excitement, keep looking.

## Build a news-angle workbench

Give each possible story its own row. Do not combine every achievement into a single pitch because each additional theme competes with the main reason to read.

| Candidate development | Reader consequence | Evidence available | Reason to act now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fictional Fieldnote changes offices | Little effect on maintenance work | Office photographs | No clear external urgency |
| Fieldnote redesigns scheduling | Possibly easier planning, still untested | Current interface | A release exists, but the consequence needs explanation |
| Fieldnote introduces cross-company repair coordination | Contractors can receive selected task details | Working example and documented access boundaries | The workflow has become available |

These are fictional examples, not reported customer results. Notice that the strongest row still needs careful wording. Availability does not establish time savings, adoption, or business impact.

Add a fifth column for what the evidence cannot show. A working example may demonstrate an interaction but cannot establish that every customer completes it successfully. This column helps narrow the proposed story before a reporter has to ask.

Choose the row with a concrete consequence and inspectable support. Keep the others in a separate idea list.

## Match the angle to the reporter’s readers

A reporter covering industrial labor may see a coordination story differently from a reporter covering enterprise software. Read recent work to understand the questions their articles examine.

Write one sentence describing that audience: “People responsible for coordinating work between facilities and outside contractors.” Then explain how your development connects to an issue the reporter has already explored. Reference the actual article and the relevant question, not an unrelated headline selected to make the email seem personalized.

[Qwoted’s pitching advice](https://www.qwoted.com/best-practices-for-pitching-journalists/) recommends making the connection to the reporter’s work and relevant expertise explicit. Your application should be concrete: what could this founder help the journalist understand?

Do not infer interest from a job title alone. A technology reporter might cover policy, consumer devices, or large-company earnings. None automatically makes a small workflow launch relevant.

If you cannot describe the connection without a generic sentence such as “your readers love innovation,” pause the pitch and improve the match.

## Write the first message around one question

The first email should let the reporter decide whether further reporting is worthwhile. It does not need to contain the entire company history.

Here is an original fictional example:

> Subject: A new way for maintenance teams to share repair tasks  
> Your recent article examined the information gaps between facilities teams and outside contractors. Fieldnote has introduced a workflow that lets a facilities manager share selected repair tasks without opening the full internal workspace.  
> I can show the current workflow and explain the access boundaries. We can also discuss what it does not solve, including coordination when a contractor has no reliable connection. Would this development be relevant to your coverage?

The example contains no claimed savings, invented customer endorsement, or assumed relationship. A real pitch should name the specific article and substitute verified product facts.

If a short recording genuinely clarifies the development, add a descriptive link: “Current task-sharing workflow, including its limitations.” Avoid making the reporter watch a long founder introduction before reaching the point.

Include your name, role, and a practical contact route. Keep optional background below the main request.

## Prepare the reporting path before you send

Imagine the reporter replies, “Interesting. How does this work, and who can confirm it?” You should know what you can provide before creating that expectation.

Prepare a small response package with the current product state, a factual explanation of the change, and the relevant person who can answer detailed questions. If a customer might speak, obtain their agreement before offering an introduction. If no customer can participate, state that honestly.

Separate useful support from promotional decoration. A diagram showing which task information moves between teams may be more helpful than a glossy montage. A short founder recording can explain a tradeoff, but it should not replace the underlying document or working example.

Check every link in a signed-out browser. Name any access restriction in the message. Do not promise an unrestricted demonstration if the material requires a private account or contains customer information.

Also identify the question you cannot yet answer. A precise limitation makes the conversation easier to manage than discovering an evidence gap during the interview.

## Follow up with information and learn from the answer

Choose a reasonable follow-up point based on the story’s timing and any response from the journalist. One useful follow-up might clarify availability, add a relevant document, or report a material change. Repeating the same pitch with increasing urgency does not make its angle stronger.

Keep a simple outcome note: interested, declined, wrong beat, evidence insufficient, timing unsuitable, or no response. These are observations about this attempt. They do not establish the quality of the company or a reliable success rate.

If the journalist declines, examine the specific reason. A weak fit suggests revisiting the recipient. A thin evidence base suggests improving the source material. An old development suggests waiting for a real next event.
