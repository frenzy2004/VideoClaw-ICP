An embargo coordinates when agreed information may be published; an exclusive reserves a defined reporting opportunity for one journalist or outlet. Choose according to the story, the available evidence, and the access you can genuinely offer. Confirm the scope and timing explicitly before sharing material under either arrangement.

## Separate timing from sole access

Founders often use embargo and exclusive as if they were interchangeable labels. They answer different questions.

Timing asks when information becomes public. Sole access asks who receives a particular opportunity before others. A story might involve one, both, or neither. For example, several reporters could receive advance information with the same agreed publication time, while one receives a separate interview opportunity.

[YC’s media guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/a-guide-to-pitching-funding-and-launch-stories/) distinguishes these arrangements and recommends explicit agreement. The practical implication is simple: describe the proposed arrangement in ordinary language before relying on a label.

Start with the actual need. Do reporters need time to understand a complex development? Are you offering one person unusual access? Or do you simply want the announcement to appear soon?

If the news is already public, advance publication timing is no longer the same proposition. Check what customers, partners, employees, and existing public pages have already revealed before making an offer.

## Choose based on the reporting opportunity

An embargo may be useful when several people need time to inspect the same development and the company can coordinate when its own materials appear. It creates preparation time, but it also creates coordination work.

An exclusive may make sense when the reporting opportunity has enough depth for one journalist to invest attention. [Michael Seibel’s startup press advice](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/getting-press-for-your-startup) presents exclusives as one approach. That recommendation is a viewpoint to evaluate, not an obligation for every company.

Ask what the journalist receives that is valuable: time with a knowledgeable founder, a documented product development, or access to someone who can explain the customer context. The word exclusive does not compensate for a thin story.

Also consider your constraints. A partner might already plan its own announcement. A customer may be available only for a narrow period. Your team may be unable to keep several external conversations coordinated. These facts should shape the first offer and every later request. If outreach is already underway, inventory it before promising exclusive access.

## Work through three founder scenarios

The following situations are fictional and illustrate different decisions.

**A complex product change with several trade audiences.** A startup is making an existing workflow available to a wider customer group. Several journalists may need to understand the technical details. The founder proposes advance briefings with a shared publication time, provided each journalist accepts it. The company does not describe this as exclusive.

**An unusual reporting opportunity.** A founder can explain a difficult design decision and provide access to a participating customer who has agreed to speak. The founder proposes a defined exclusive opportunity to one reporter. The offer says whether it covers the whole story, the interview, or a particular piece of supporting material.

**A development already discussed publicly.** A partner has posted the key facts on its website. The founder cannot meaningfully promise that the information has not been released. The useful offer becomes additional context or access, with an accurate explanation of what is already public.

**A late request after private outreach.** Nothing is public, but Reporter C asks for the whole announcement exclusively. The founder first completes this fictional inventory:

| Recipient | Already offered or shared | Accepted terms and timing | Current status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reporter A | Announcement facts and advance briefing | Accepted publication hold until September 18, 10 a.m. Eastern | Briefing completed |
| Reporter B | Same announcement pitch, no private documents | No timing restriction accepted | Awaiting response |
| Reporter C | General introduction only | No arrangement yet | Requests whole-story exclusive |
| Customer interview | Permission to participate, no reporter access offered | Customer available September 17 | Still unoffered |

The whole-story exclusive is unavailable as described: A already has the facts and an accepted arrangement; B has an unrestricted pitch. The founder does not retroactively restrict B or silently cancel A's access.

The founder tells C: “The announcement has already been pitched elsewhere, and another reporter has received an advance briefing under an agreed publication time. I cannot offer the announcement exclusively. A separate customer interview has not been offered to any reporter; I can discuss that narrower opportunity if it is useful.”

If C accepts, the founder confirms the exact interview scope and timing without conflicting with A's arrangement. If C insists on the whole story, the founder declines. Any change to existing terms requires the affected participant's express agreement and a written record; silence is not consent.

The key distinction is the reporting opportunity you can actually provide, not the announcement status you would prefer to have.

## Write the scope before making the offer

Use this original access worksheet to remove ambiguity.

| Question | Decision to write down |
|---|---|
| What is the subject? | The exact development being discussed |
| What is already public? | Existing pages, statements, or customer access |
| What has been offered privately? | Recipient, material, angle, access, accepted terms, timing, and status |
| What is offered in advance? | Facts, documents, interviews, or recordings |
| What is exclusive, if anything? | The whole story or a precisely named component |
| When does the arrangement end? | A date, time, and timezone |
| Who must coordinate? | Company, customer, partner, and relevant participants |
| What happens if the offer is declined? | The next contact or ordinary public release |

Keep the scope narrow enough that everyone can explain it the same way. “Exclusive access” is vague if one person means a private interview and another means that no other journalist receives the story.

If a founder video is part of the material, identify whether it is a private briefing, an attributable statement, or a public asset scheduled for later. Do not leave the journalist to infer its intended status from the filename.

## Confirm the arrangement in plain language

Here is original sample wording for a proposed embargo:

> We can share advance details of the development if you agree not to publish those details before the specified release time. The proposed time is [date], [time], [timezone]. Please confirm whether that arrangement works before we send the advance package.

For an exclusive, the wording must also name the scope:

> We are offering your outlet the first interview with the founder about [specific development]. Other reporters may receive the public announcement after [agreed point]. This offer covers the interview opportunity, not exclusive ownership of the entire subject.

These are communication examples, not legal agreements. Adapt them to the actual arrangement and any established company requirements.

Once accepted, keep a written record of the same facts everyone agreed to. If a participant requests a change, confirm the revised version rather than relying on a phone conversation remembered differently by different people.

Avoid promising the journalist a particular headline, placement, or favorable treatment. Those are editorial decisions outside the founder’s control.

## Coordinate the release and handle a changed plan

Walk through the release as an event sequence. Who sends the final factual material? When does the company page become available? Does a partner have a scheduled post? Is someone available to answer questions at the agreed time?

If the plan changes, tell the affected journalist promptly and explain the actual change. Do not assume that moving an internal calendar entry updates an external agreement.

If information appears early, first establish what became public and where. Communicate that fact clearly. Avoid making an accusation before understanding whether the material was already available through another route.

Afterward, record which coordination step failed and improve that step for the next story. The lesson may concern public-page timing, partner communication, or an unclear definition of the material involved.
