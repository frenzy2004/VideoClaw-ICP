Pay for a small audio-repair sample before committing to a full restoration. Test difficult, typical, and clean sections for intelligibility and unwanted changes, then compare the achievable result with a real rerecording option. Set a spending limit and a stop rule so an irreplaceable recording does not become an open-ended repair project.

## Identify what is wrong with the recording

Listen to the best available original before describing the problem. “Bad audio” can mean noise behind the speaker, distortion on loud syllables, intermittent interruptions, or a recording that is simply too quiet. Those problems call for different assessments.

iZotope's technical documentation distinguishes clipping, broadband noise, hum, and intermittent sounds. That distinction is useful when describing the source to a repair specialist, even if you never operate the software yourself. [iZotope's guide to identifying audio problems](https://downloads.izotope.com/docs/rx6/68-audio-problems/index.html).

Mark timestamps where the issue is most severe and where it is typical. Include a section that already sounds acceptable so the specialist can hear the intended voice quality. Preserve the original file and work from an authorized copy.

Ask whether another source exists before buying repair. A separate microphone track, camera reference, or locally recorded participant file may provide a better starting point than the combined export you first received. Have the editor investigate those sources; a different file can change the decision more than additional processing of the wrong one.

## Define the result the purchase must achieve

State where the audio will be heard and what the listener must understand. A private reference recording and a prominently featured customer interview can have different acceptance needs. Neither benefits from a vague promise to make it “studio quality.”

Write a short acceptance statement: the required words must remain understandable, the voice must sound natural enough for the intended use, and processing must not introduce distracting artifacts. Add any project-specific requirement, such as preserving a particular answer without changing its meaning.

Ask the specialist to explain what they believe can improve and what will remain limited. They should distinguish a likely repair from an uncertain attempt. Request the best available example from your own source instead of treating a demonstration on unrelated material as proof.

Avoid asking for total silence between every word. The strongest noise reduction is not automatically the most usable result. A quieter background can come with altered speech or abrupt transitions. Your decision should be based on listening to the complete phrase in context.

## Commission a bounded three-section sample

Use three short sections: the worst relevant passage, a typical passage, and a relatively clean reference. Give the specialist enough surrounding audio to work sensibly, and agree what the sample fee covers.

Specify the deliverables: an unprocessed reference, the repaired version, a brief explanation of the work, and remaining limitations. Ask the specialist to keep timing compatible with the edit unless a different approach is agreed. This makes the sample easier to assess and later incorporate.

iZotope describes De-clip as a process that reconstructs waveform peaks above a selected threshold. That explains one possible mechanism; it does not establish that any particular damaged sentence will become usable. [iZotope's De-clip documentation](https://downloads.izotope.com/docs/rx6/22-de-clip/index.html).

Do not authorize unlimited successive attempts. Agree one sample and a decision point. If the result suggests another narrowly defined approach, request a separate explanation of what changes and why it may help. The purpose is to buy evidence about feasibility before buying the entire repair.

## Listen with a comparison sheet

Ask the specialist to provide versions at comparable listening levels, then review without labels when practical. Otherwise, a louder version can appear more impressive even when the actual speech quality has not improved.

Use headphones and at least one ordinary playback device representative of the final audience. Keep the environment and playback level consistent across versions.

| Listening question | Worst section | Typical section | Clean reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Are the required words understandable? | Record uncertain words | Record uncertain words | Confirm original clarity |
| Does the voice sound natural? | Note metallic or watery changes | Note changes in timbre | Check for unnecessary alteration |
| Is the background distracting? | Describe what remains | Describe what remains | Compare room character |
| Do edits or processing draw attention? | Mark transitions | Mark transitions | Check pauses and breaths |
| Is the result usable in context? | Accept, revise, or replace | Accept, revise, or replace | Keep original if preferable |

Write observations before discussing the versions. If reviewers disagree, identify the exact phrase or artifact causing disagreement. Do not settle the question with an unexplained average score.

Then listen inside the video sequence. An isolated defect may be less noticeable in context, while an unnatural voice change can become more obvious when the repaired segment sits beside clean speech.

## Compare repair with a real replacement option

Obtain a practical rerecording estimate, including participant availability, setup, production support, and editing changes. If rerecording the whole piece is unnecessary, ask whether one answer or narration segment can be replaced honestly and coherently.

In a fictional example, the middle answer of an interview is damaged while the rest is usable. The team could repair that answer, record a replacement with the participant, omit it, or restructure the sequence around another supported statement. Each option preserves different parts of the original commission.

Keep sunk production spending out of the next-step comparison. Money already spent does not make a poor repair more useful. Focus on the additional cost, time, participant effort, and quality each available option can deliver.

If the material cannot be repeated, that raises its potential value but does not guarantee repairability. Set a ceiling that reflects the importance of the content and the evidence from the sample. Consider a narrower use for the recording if its original intended quality is no longer achievable.

## Approve the full repair with a stop rule

Authorize the remaining work only after the sample meets your acceptance statement or establishes a clearly agreed limitation. Define which sections receive treatment and which should remain untouched.

Ask for a final review point before the specialist spends beyond the agreed amount or attempts a materially different process. If new damage appears, they should flag it with an example rather than silently extending the project. Keep the original and intermediate files so the editor can compare or revert particular sections.

On delivery, check both the worst passages and transitions between treated and untreated material. Confirm timing, file naming, and compatibility with the edit. A technically repaired file still needs to work in the final sequence.

Record why you chose repair, replacement, or omission. Use that decision to improve future capture purchases, especially where a recurring microphone or monitoring problem caused the damage. A disciplined salvage decision buys the improvement the source can support and stops when further spending no longer serves the video.
