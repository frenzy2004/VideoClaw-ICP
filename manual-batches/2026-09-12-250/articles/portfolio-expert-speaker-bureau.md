Build a portfolio speaker bureau as an opt-in matching service, not a public list of impressive titles. Record what each person can credibly discuss, the audiences they can help and the conditions under which they accept invitations. Use a request-to-speaker match sheet so introductions reflect expertise, availability and permission.

## Define the bureau's promise

A platform operator may receive requests for a panelist, a workshop facilitator, a podcast guest or a company spokesperson. Those are different speaking jobs. The bureau's useful promise is to identify a plausible person and coordinate an authorized introduction, not to guarantee acceptance or a successful event.

[AAE Speakers organizes speaker discovery by topic](https://www.aaespeakers.com/). The portfolio adaptation is to make expertise discoverable without assuming that a senior title establishes fit. An operator who recently redesigned a customer-interview workflow may be more useful for a practical session than a founder with broader visibility.

Define the audience the bureau serves and who can submit requests. An internal founder workshop and an external commercial conference may require different checks. Keep those distinctions in the request form so a speaker does not discover the event's purpose only after an introduction.

State what the platform will not do automatically. Listing a person does not authorize sharing private contact details, accepting an invitation on their behalf, negotiating terms or recording their session. Those decisions remain with the appropriate people.

## Collect specific, permissioned profiles

Ask each potential speaker to describe two or three topics through the questions they can answer. “Building a customer-interview process with a small marketing team” gives a requester more information than “growth.” Add examples of relevant experience, approved public links and formats the speaker is comfortable handling.

Separate expertise from availability. A person can remain qualified while temporarily unavailable. Record a review date and an availability state rather than removing useful knowledge whenever someone takes a break. Give speakers a simple way to pause introductions or narrow the topics they accept.

Capture sharing preferences field by field. A biography may be approved for event organizers while a personal phone number remains private. A previous recording might be available only to portfolio members. Do not publish every field merely because it sits in the same roster.

[First Round's portfolio offering](https://founders.firstround.com/template-of-sheet) describes a private network and structured mentorship. That is context for permissioned expertise exchange, not evidence for any particular bureau design. Keep your roster grounded in what participating speakers have actually agreed to provide.

## Populate a speaker-to-request worksheet

Imagine a fictional accelerator planning a workshop for first marketing hires. The request asks for a practical session on collecting customer stories, with time for participants to draft an interview brief. It is not a keynote about the future of marketing.

| Candidate | Confirmed relevant experience | Preferred format | Current constraint | Match decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nia, Alder marketing lead | Built an interview intake and approval process | Small working session | Available remotely; no customer names | Ask permission to introduce |
| Tomas, Beacon founder | Public talks about company positioning | Keynote or moderated interview | Cannot prepare a workshop this month | Not the first match |
| Priya, Cobalt customer lead | Runs onboarding calls and captures product feedback | Peer discussion | Has not agreed to external invitations | Consider only for an internal discussion |

The worksheet records the audience, task, format, date, preparation expected, recording plan and named organizer. Next to each candidate, explain the fit and the unresolved question. A match is provisional until the speaker sees the full brief.

For Nia, the operator sends a private invitation summary explaining that participants will produce an interview brief and that examples can use fictional customers. Nia can accept, decline or propose a narrower discussion. The bureau does not send her details to the organizer first and treat silence as acceptance.

## Check the event context before introduction

Ask who is organizing the event, who will attend, how the session will be described and whether sponsors influence the agenda. Clarify whether the speaker is expected to represent their company, their own experience or the portfolio program. Avoid letting a generic biography imply an endorsement the speaker has not agreed to make.

Confirm the intended treatment of recordings, slides and quotations before the speaker commits. An invitation to join a private discussion is not the same as permission to publish clips later. Record the speaker's stated conditions and route unresolved questions to the responsible event owner.

Check for practical conflicts. A session may involve a direct competitor, an unreleased project or a customer example that cannot be discussed. Ask for enough information to assess fit without requesting confidential details that the bureau does not need.

If the context changes, reopen the match. A private workshop becoming a public livestream is a material change, not a small logistics update. Tell the speaker what changed and ask whether the original acceptance still applies.

## Make the introduction useful and bounded

An introduction should carry the agreed brief, the reason for the match and the next decision. For example: “Nia is open to discussing the internal customer-story workshop described below. Please confirm the session outline and preparation expectations directly before treating the booking as final.”

Name who owns logistics after the introduction. The platform might coordinate the first conversation while the event organizer handles timing, materials and accessibility arrangements. The speaker should not receive contradictory instructions from several portfolio staff members.

Keep a simple status trail: candidate identified, permission requested, introduction authorized, discussion in progress, accepted, declined or closed. These states distinguish an available expert from a booked speaker. Do not report a successful match merely because an email was sent.

When a speaker declines, record only the reason needed for future matching and only at the appropriate visibility. “Unavailable for external events this quarter” may be sufficient. The roster does not need personal explanations or speculative judgments about willingness to support the community.

## Maintain expertise without turning it into a ranking

After an engagement, ask whether the topic, audience and format matched the original brief. Invite the speaker to update their profile based on what they now feel prepared to discuss. Keep feedback about logistics separate from judgments about expertise.

A useful closure note might say: “The working-session format fit; future requests should allow participants to bring a draft brief.” That helps the next match. A popularity score based on applause or social reactions may not explain whether the speaker can help a different audience.

Periodically review profiles that have stale contacts, unconfirmed roles or repeatedly declined requests. Archive inactive entries from everyday matching while preserving whatever administrative history the program needs under its own policies. Do not keep circulating an old biography because it remains convenient.

The finished bureau is a maintained set of permissioned expertise records and traceable introductions. Its value lies in finding someone who can help with a specific conversation while respecting that person's choices—not in accumulating the longest possible list of founders.
