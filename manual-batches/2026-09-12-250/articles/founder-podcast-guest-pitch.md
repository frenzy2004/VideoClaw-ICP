Pitch yourself as a podcast guest by offering a specific conversation the show’s listeners would find useful. Connect a real founder experience to an unanswered audience question, then suggest a few discussion turns and concrete examples. Keep your biography brief and make it easy for the host to assess the episode idea.

## Listen for the show’s real audience

A podcast’s category is only a starting point. Two shows about startups may serve very different listeners: first-time founders, experienced operators, technical builders, or people interested in business stories.

Listen to a complete relevant episode. Notice how much context the host provides, how deeply guests explain their decisions, and whether the conversation emphasizes tactics, personal experience, or industry analysis.

[HBR recommends listening before assessing fit](https://hbr.org/tip/2019/04/getting-booked-as-a-podcast-guest). Apply that test by writing a specific audience description rather than assuming that every business show wants your fundraising story.

Record one question the host returned to and one question that remained open. These observations are more useful than a generic compliment about the show.

A newly funded founder should also check whether their experience is mature enough for the proposed topic. You can explain an early decision honestly without presenting yourself as an expert on operating a large company.

## Find an episode-sized idea

An episode idea needs enough tension and detail to sustain a conversation. “My founder journey” leaves the host to discover the point. “How we decided which customer requests to decline during our first pilot” offers a more concrete thread.

Start with a decision you can reconstruct. What did you believe at first? What made that belief harder to maintain? What alternatives did you consider? What did you choose, and what remains unresolved?

The idea should connect to the audience’s work. A founder can have an interesting experience that does not fit this particular show. Keep it for a different opportunity rather than stretching the connection.

Avoid packing several unrelated lessons into the pitch. A host can expand a focused idea more easily than they can find a coherent episode inside a list of topics.

Write the proposed listener takeaway as an action: “Listeners can separate an essential pilot requirement from a request that belongs in a later engagement.” That gives the conversation a practical destination.

## Build a show-to-episode fit card

Use this original worksheet before drafting the email.

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| Listener situation | The problem this audience recognizes |
| Relevant episode | A real episode and the useful connection |
| Proposed question | One question your experience helps answer |
| Narrative tension | The choice, tradeoff, or surprise in the story |
| Concrete examples | Situations you can describe accurately |
| Discussion turns | A beginning, a complication, and a practical lesson |
| Relevant experience | Why you can contribute to this conversation |
| Boundary | What you cannot responsibly generalize |

For a fictional founder, the card might connect a show’s discussion of early customer relationships to a story about narrowing an overextended pilot. The examples could include a request that changed the project’s purpose and a conversation that clarified the actual learning goal.

These are invented teaching examples. Replace them with real experiences you can discuss, or label a hypothetical scenario openly.

If the card is mostly empty, improve the idea before contacting the host.

## Send a pitch that offers a conversation

Here is an original fictional booking email:

> Subject: An episode idea about knowing what a pilot should prove  
>   
> Your conversation about early customer relationships raised a question I have been working through: when does being helpful turn a pilot into an undefined project?  
>   
> I am a founder who has had to separate essential pilot work from requests that belonged elsewhere. I could discuss the warning signs, a difficult customer conversation, and a question-setting exercise listeners can use. Would that fit a future episode?

A real version should identify the actual episode and your relevant experience. Do not imply that the fictional example is a published appearance or customer result.

Add a short biography and a link that helps the host understand how you communicate. A brief founder recording may be enough; it does not need to be a polished promotional reel.

Make one request. Asking simultaneously for a podcast appearance, newsletter feature, introduction, and social promotion creates unnecessary decisions.

## Help the host prepare after they respond

Once the host is interested, shift from pitching to collaboration. Ask what angle they want to explore, how the show is structured, and what background would be most useful.

[Riverside’s host preparation guide](https://riverside.com/blog/podcast-interview-preparation) connects guest selection and preparation to audience interests. As the guest, help the host by providing examples and context they can use to form better questions.

Offer a short preparation note with the central story, the relevant people or roles, and any facts that require exact wording. Flag private material before the recording rather than hoping it never comes up.

Do not demand that the host follow your proposed questions. Their job is to create a useful conversation, and an unexpected question may uncover the most interesting part.

Confirm the practical details separately: recording time, expected duration, whether video is involved, and who provides the recording instructions. Keep logistical preparation from becoming a substitute for a strong episode idea.

## Make the appearance worth the invitation

Before the recording, choose a small set of examples you can explain clearly. Practice the opening of each story, but leave room to answer naturally.

During the conversation, respond to the host’s actual question. Give enough context for a listener encountering your company for the first time. If you use a technical term, explain why it matters in the situation.

Treat uncertainty as part of the experience. You can say that a decision seemed reasonable at the time and that you would now handle it differently. That is often more useful than presenting every choice as inevitable.

After recording, provide any information you promised. If the host asks for links or a biography, send the agreed materials in a compact form. Do not treat participation as permission to edit, redistribute, or reuse the show’s recording beyond the arrangement you have made.
