Buy a music license for the named client, intended placements, and publication timeline, not simply for access to a track. Confirm what happens to existing videos and later edits when the subscription ends. Use a dated coverage sheet to obtain written answers before the soundtrack becomes part of an approved delivery.

## Separate track access from the intended permission

A music library account can let an editor find, preview, or download a track. That activity alone does not explain what your company may publish, where it may publish it, or what happens after the supplier's subscription changes.

Start with the actual production. Name the business receiving the video, the editor or agency obtaining the music, and the accounts or venues where the finished film will appear. Identify paid placement separately from an ordinary website upload, even if the same file will be used.

The [U.S. Copyright Office's musician guidance](https://www.copyright.gov/engage/musicians/) explains that a composition and its recording are separate protected works that may be licensed separately. Your practical question is therefore what rights the proposed agreement supplies for the complete soundtrack and intended use.

Do not decide coverage by interpreting “royalty-free,” “commercial,” or “all platforms” in isolation. Ask the vendor to point to the applicable agreement and clarify how it covers your specific plan. This article organizes that conversation; it is not a legal clearance opinion.

## Build a publication timeline, not a download log

List when each version is expected to become public. A master approved in October may not be published until November, after a contractor has ended a subscription. A revised ending created months later introduces another event to check.

[Epidemic Sound's public solutions page](https://www.epidemicsound.com/solutions/) states that content published during an active subscription remains licensed after cancellation. That provider-specific summary makes publication timing important, but it is not a substitute for confirming your account, client, and use under the applicable plan.

Record the subscription start and end dates where relevant, the intended first publication, and planned subsequent releases. If dates are uncertain, show a range and ask what must be true on the eventual publication date.

Keep download dates as evidence, but do not let them stand in for the coverage question. The correct comparison is between the license conditions and the actual events, not between the age of the audio file and the date on an invoice.

## Complete the music coverage sheet

Use this proposed worksheet for each music purchase or subscription arrangement. Its example entries are hypothetical questions, not confirmed permissions.

| Event or party | Record | Question for the licensor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subscriber | Editor's agency account | Does this arrangement cover the named client? |
| Client | The business commissioning the film | Must the client hold its own plan? |
| Initial publication | Website film on a planned date | What conditions apply when it first goes live? |
| Paid placement | Named advertising channel and territory | Is this placement included in the quoted entitlement? |
| Subscription end | Known cancellation or renewal date | What remains covered afterward? |
| Later version | New closing card or shortened edit | Is this treated as a new covered publication? |
| Claims support | Person and retained evidence | Who handles a platform claim and what is required? |

Add the track ID, recording version, relevant terms link, and answer date. A remix or alternate recording should not be silently treated as the same licensed asset.

Send the completed sheet with the inquiry. Ask for answers to the unresolved rows, not a general assurance that the library serves businesses. If the answer relies on a different plan or custom license, update the purchase comparison before the editor proceeds.

## Compare subscriptions with project-specific coverage

A subscription may fit a business producing many videos over an ongoing period. A project-specific arrangement may be worth comparing when the use is narrow, the publication schedule is uncertain, or the client wants evidence attached to one commission.

Those are decision hypotheses, not pricing rules. Request the actual offer for your use from each supplier. Compare what each quote includes, the relevant dates, permitted clients, publication conditions, and how future changes are handled.

For a subscription, ask whether an editor's account can cover client work and what records the client receives. For a project-specific license, ask precisely which production, versions, placements, and duration it covers. Neither arrangement should be judged merely by whether the first invoice is smaller.

Do not invent a universal break-even number of videos. A single unusual placement can change the required entitlement, while repeated ordinary work may still need separate client arrangements. Put the confirmed coverage beside the actual quoted cost and any administrative work needed to maintain it.

## Test the handoff and future-edit scenario

Ask the supplier to walk through one realistic change: a different editor will shorten the video after the original subscription ends. Who can access the track? What evidence travels with the project? Does the intended new publication require another entitlement?

The answer may differ from the status of the unchanged original film. Avoid bundling those two questions into “Can we use it forever?” That phrase can conceal a distinction between keeping an existing publication online and creating a new use.

Name a business owner for the coverage records. They should be able to identify the track, the agreement, the covered production, and the relevant contact without needing the freelancer's personal login.

Also ask about platform claim handling. Do not promise that the absence of a current claim proves permission, or that a license guarantees no automated claim will ever appear. The useful purchase question is how a legitimate user can document the use and obtain help if a claim occurs.

## Approve the license with a short decision record

Write a final note stating the selected entitlement, named parties, covered plan of use, and unresolved limitations. Attach the current terms and written clarifications available for the transaction.

If an answer remains unclear, pause that asset's approval, seek qualified advice where needed, or choose another soundtrack arrangement with clearer fit. A familiar song or an already approved edit is not a reason to overlook a material gap.

Before delivery, confirm that the final cut uses the track and version on the record. If the music changed during revisions, repeat the check rather than carrying forward the previous asset's evidence.

The desired outcome is a soundtrack purchase your company can explain later: who obtained permission, what it was intended to cover, and which future changes require another conversation. That is a manageable buying decision, not a blanket promise about music law.
