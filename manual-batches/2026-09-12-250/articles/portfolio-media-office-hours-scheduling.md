Portfolio media office hours work best when each appointment has one decision to resolve, enough preparation time and a clear cancellation path. Publish bookable capacity after allowing for preparation and follow-up. Use a visible waitlist, protect private company discussions and end each session with a documented next step rather than an open-ended production commitment.

## Decide what belongs in an appointment

An office hour is useful when a short conversation can unlock a decision. A founder may need to choose the main audience for an interview, understand why a company profile is unclear or decide which evidence belongs in a proposed story. Those are different from asking the expert to write a complete campaign during the appointment.

Define the service in one sentence: bring one media decision and the smallest relevant set of material; leave with a decision or a named next action. Give examples of unsuitable requests, including full editing jobs, urgent release approval without source material and meetings whose only purpose is a general status update.

[First Round's description of its resources and mentoring](https://founders.firstround.com/template-of-sheet) illustrates that portfolio support can combine peer knowledge and structured advice. Your scheduling system should make the advisory element usable without suggesting that every conversation creates an ongoing engagement.

If a request does not fit, redirect it before booking. Offer a resource, a production-intake route or a separate scoping discussion. Avoid letting an appointment become the only doorway into every other service.

## Calculate bookable time from the whole task

Start with the expert's actual available blocks. Subtract preparation, necessary breaks and follow-up time before dividing the remainder into appointments. The meeting visible on the founder's calendar is only part of the work.

For a fictional example, suppose an adviser offers a two-hour block. The platform reserves fifteen minutes for reviewing submitted materials, three twenty-five-minute conversations and thirty minutes for follow-up and gaps. Those allocations total two hours. They are planning assumptions for this example, not a recommended appointment length or a productivity benchmark.

The [Calendly office-hours guide](https://calendly.com/blog/office-hours/) describes duration, buffers, minimum notice and booking limits as separate controls. Whatever scheduler you use, check each control against the way the adviser actually works. A calendar that offers consecutive conversations may look efficient while leaving no time to capture decisions.

Publish fewer slots when the work requires more preparation. If a complex interview outline needs a longer discussion, offer a separate appointment type instead of letting it routinely overrun a shorter slot. One unusually long meeting can otherwise consume time promised to another company.

## Build a two-week booking grid

Use a grid that makes the full allocation visible to the coordinator while showing founders only information they need. The following example uses fictional availability.

| Week | Adviser block | Company slots | Reserved work | Release rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One | Tuesday morning | Three focused reviews | Preparation and notes | Book after submitting a decision |
| One | Thursday afternoon | Two complex discussions | Longer source review | Coordinator confirms fit |
| Two | Tuesday morning | Three focused reviews | Preparation and notes | Same published eligibility |
| Two | Friday afternoon | Two follow-up slots | Review prior actions | Only where follow-up was agreed |

Keep company names and sensitive subjects out of any public calendar. A slot can be shown as unavailable without revealing who booked it or what they are discussing. Shared calendar visibility is a scheduling convenience, not permission to expose company plans.

Use consistent time-zone labels in the invitation and confirmation. Ask the founder to confirm their local time if the session crosses regions or a seasonal clock change. The coordinator should not manually convert every invitation from memory.

Do not release all appointments to the fastest responders if that has excluded parts of the portfolio. You can reserve an initial booking window for companies that have not recently attended, then release unused capacity to everyone under a stated rule.

## Collect just enough preparation

Ask for the decision, the intended audience, the relevant artifact and what the company has already tried. A short paragraph and one link are usually more useful than an entire folder. Request a non-sensitive excerpt where the full material is unnecessary.

Return a confirmation that states the appointment type and preparation deadline. Explain what happens if material does not arrive. Depending on the service, the session may become a general diagnostic or be returned to the waitlist. Choose that policy before the appointment rather than improvising an uncomfortable conversation at the start.

Review the submission for scope, not polish. A rough draft can be appropriate for advice. Missing context about the audience may prevent any useful discussion. Tell the founder which gap matters and why.

Where similar questions recur, offer a group session only when participants agree that their examples are suitable to share. Do not move a private company appointment into a group merely to increase the number of attendees. Group teaching and private advice are different services with different expectations.

## Make cancellations release real capacity

A cancellation process needs more than a cancel button. The coordinator must know when a slot becomes available, which waitlisted request fits it and whether there is enough preparation time for a replacement.

Order the waitlist using the same eligibility rules as the original booking. Record the preferred session type and feasible times so you do not offer every cancellation indiscriminately. Give a replacement requester a clear response window chosen for the situation. If nobody can prepare adequately, use the gap for follow-up rather than forcing a low-value meeting.

For a fictional case, Beacon cancels a complex review after its customer interview is postponed. Cobalt is waiting for a short outline discussion, while Delta has already supplied material for a complex review. Delta is the closer fit even if Cobalt joined the waitlist earlier. Explain the matching rule in the published process.

Handle repeated no-shows privately. First establish whether reminders, time zones or unclear preparation caused the problem. If the company keeps reserving unused slots, require coordinator confirmation for its next booking. Make that an access-management decision, not a public judgment about the founder.

## End with a useful record and a bounded follow-up

Reserve the final part of the appointment to state what was decided. Capture the proposed action, the reason, who will act and what remains uncertain. A founder should be able to use the note without replaying the entire conversation.

For example: “Revise the interview opening around the buyer's first operational problem; keep the company history for later. The marketing lead will supply a revised outline. No production booking has been made.” This is more useful than “great discussion about storytelling.”

If another appointment is necessary, explain the condition for it. A follow-up might depend on receiving the revised outline or confirming a participant. Avoid routinely booking a second meeting before anyone has acted on the first.

Review the booking process using actual cases: unused slots, mismatched session types, preparation gaps and whether recipients could name their next action. Adjust the grid and examples accordingly. The aim is useful access to expertise across the portfolio, not the largest possible number of meetings.
