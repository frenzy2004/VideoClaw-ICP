Renew a video production partner when documented delivery and the next production requirements justify the commitment. Review accepted work, missed commitments, internal management effort, and unresolved obligations before deciding. Separate supplier execution from changes your team introduced, then choose renewal, a specific renegotiation, or a bounded paid retest.

## Define the purchase you are reviewing

Start with the agreement and the work actually commissioned. Identify whether the next decision concerns another project, reserved capacity, a retainer, or a broader production relationship.

Record the renewal decision date and ask which notice, cancellation, or outstanding-payment provisions apply. Read the actual agreement rather than assuming that every supplier uses the same automatic-renewal mechanism.

Describe the next period's likely work. A partner selected for interview editing may face a different assignment if you now need location production or complex animation. That is a capability-fit question, not proof that earlier work was poor.

Name the decision owner and the people who can supply evidence. Include the person who briefs production, the person who accepts the files, and anyone responsible for purchasing or sensitive material. Avoid a review based only on the most senior person's recollection.

## Build a commission-level evidence sheet

Select completed commissions that reflect the purchased work, plus unfinished items that could affect renewal. Do not choose only the best showpiece or the most frustrating exception.

For each commission, locate the approved brief, agreed deliverables, changes, review exchanges, and final acceptance. Record missing evidence openly. A blank acceptance record is a reason to investigate, not automatic evidence of failure.

[Upwork's milestone guidance](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/44564821903763-Understanding-milestones-on-fixed-price-contracts) uses defined deliverables, due dates, and payments to structure work. Those fields provide a useful starting point for this review; its platform procedures are not universal contract rules.

Use this original worksheet to prepare the renewal memo.

| Commission | Promise to compare | Evidence to attach | Renewal implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Completed work | Agreed files and quality criteria | Accepted version and approval | Repeatable fit or a specific quality concern |
| Missed commitment | Original and revised delivery event | Dated changes and handoffs | Capacity issue, changed scope, or unresolved cause |
| Management effort | Expected buyer involvement | Briefing and correction records | Clarify responsibility or change service scope |
| Open obligation | Missing file, correction, or handover | Owner and current status | Resolve before another commitment |
| Next requirement | New production capability | Relevant sample or paid test | Renew existing scope or qualify an expansion |

Keep descriptions concrete. “Poor communication” should become an identifiable missed question, unclear decision, or absent status update that affected the commission.

## Separate causes before assigning responsibility

Reconstruct a few important events rather than scoring every email. When were usable inputs supplied, when was the first draft delivered, when did feedback arrive, and when was the final accepted?

Distinguish an original commitment from a revised one. If your team added a new sequence after approval, preserve that change and any agreed consequence. If the supplier missed an unchanged requirement, record that separately.

Use “unresolved” when the records cannot establish the cause. Invite the partner to identify a missing handoff or different understanding before finalizing the review.

Consider a fictional example: a film arrives after the original target, but the buyer supplied replacement interview footage after the first cut. The useful question is whether the revised delivery was agreed and met—not whether the original date was missed in isolation.

Another fictional commission might arrive on time but contain an incorrect product claim that was clearly marked in the approved brief. Timeliness would not erase that acceptance failure.

This distinction produces actionable purchasing questions: do you need more reserved capacity, clearer briefing, a specialist, or better change control?

## Evaluate management effort and future fit

List the work your team performed beyond the expected buyer role. Examples might include repairing project organization, repeatedly identifying the same specification error, or coordinating specialists that the proposal appeared to include.

Do not treat all collaboration as waste. Creative production requires decisions, and some service models intentionally leave more direction with the buyer. Compare actual effort with the purchased arrangement.

Ask whether the next work requires capabilities the partner has demonstrated. Request relevant full examples, identify who would do the work, and use a paid test where the uncertainty is material.

Include changes in confidentiality or access requirements. The [FTC's service-provider security guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/start-security-guide-business) advises communicating security expectations and verifying provider performance against them. That supports this security check, not a general score for creative quality.

Confirm whether the proposed team, subcontracting arrangement, and file-handling route still fit. A familiar supplier should not receive automatic approval for a materially different production environment.

## Write a renewal recommendation with conditions

Keep the recommendation short enough for the decision owner to use. State the proposed purchase, the evidence supporting it, the unresolved risks, and the conditions required before commitment.

Choose among three practical outcomes. Renew when the demonstrated service fits the next work. Renegotiate when the relationship is useful but scope, capacity, responsibility, or commercial terms need a specific change. Retest when a consequential capability remains uncertain.

Avoid a universal pass percentage or a weighted score that hides a serious unresolved obligation. A missing essential master file cannot be made irrelevant by several pleasant status calls.

Make conditions observable. Replace “improve quality” with a named acceptance requirement and review event. Replace “communicate better” with the status information, responsible person, and agreed escalation route needed for the next commission.

Ask the partner to respond to factual findings and proposed changes. Record disagreements rather than smoothing them into a falsely unanimous recommendation.

## Close the current work before the next commitment

Review outstanding deliverables, agreed corrections, invoices, access, and handover responsibilities. Identify who will resolve each item and what evidence will show completion.

If changing suppliers is under consideration, gather the current asset inventory and contractual handover questions before assuming a new editor can resume the work. Do not claim ownership or transfer rights without checking the relevant agreement.

Set a review point for any renewed or revised arrangement. Use the same evidence fields so the next decision can compare like with like.

Keep campaign outcomes in their appropriate review. Views, leads, and sales may depend on distribution, offer, audience, and other factors beyond production. They should not silently replace the supplier's agreed production obligations.

The final memo should explain why this partner is suitable for this next purchase, under stated conditions. That is more useful than declaring the agency universally good or bad.
