Record a founder apology by naming the customer harm, taking responsibility, and saying plainly that you are sorry. Explain the remedy already underway, distinguish it from work not yet completed, and provide a clear follow-up route. The video should support accountable action after a mistake, not ask customers to reward the founder for expressing regret.

## Establish what happened and who was affected

Before writing, assemble the facts with the people responsible for the event and response. Identify the mistake, the affected customer group, the consequence, and the current state of any remedy. Separate confirmed information from questions still being investigated.

This article concerns an apology for a mistake whose basic impact is known. It is not a live incident-response plan. If the situation involves legal duties, safety, security, or ongoing investigation, coordinate with the relevant specialists before communicating sensitive details.

Write the harm in customer terms. “An internal workflow failed” describes your system. “We sent you conflicting setup instructions, which caused you to repeat work” describes the consequence, if that is verified.

Avoid guessing how every person feels. You can acknowledge inconvenience, lost time, or uncertainty without claiming to know each customer's emotional response.

The founder should know what they are apologizing for. A recording prepared around an evasive summary will remain evasive no matter how sincere the delivery appears.

## Replace defensive lines with responsible ones

Use a two-column rewrite exercise before drafting the full script. The following examples are original and fictional.

“We are sorry if anyone was confused” becomes “Our instructions contradicted each other. We are sorry for the extra work that caused.” The replacement identifies the company's contribution rather than making the customer's reaction the condition.

“This happened because we are growing quickly” becomes “We did not give the updated instructions a clear owner before sending them.” Use that replacement only if ownership was the verified failure; do not invent a neat cause for rhetorical effect.

“We take feedback very seriously” becomes “We have paused the conflicting message and are contacting the affected group with the corrected instructions.” Again, the action must actually be underway.

[GitLab's historical database-outage postmortem includes a direct apology from its CEO to affected users](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/postmortem-of-database-outage-of-january-31/). That example illustrates explicit responsibility. It does not establish that an apology repairs harm by itself or that another company's incident should become your script.

## Build a remedy-status sheet before making promises

List each response action, its owner, current status, evidence of completion, and customer consequence. Use plain states such as planned, started, completed, or waiting on a dependency.

For the fictional instructions mistake, the sheet might contain: pause the conflicting message; verify the correct instructions; contact affected customers; review how future messages are approved. Those are separate actions. Completing the first does not mean the whole problem is fixed.

State what customers can do now and what your team will do without requiring them to repeat their story. If individual situations differ, name the route for a person to review them.

[CDC's emergency-communication guidance emphasizes empathy and acknowledging uncertainty](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Spokesperson.pdf). Its original context is public health. The narrower editorial application here is to acknowledge impact while refusing to disguise unfinished work as certainty.

Do not promise that the mistake can never recur. Explain the specific change you are making and how you will check it. A concrete, verifiable action is more useful than an unlimited reassurance.

## Draft and record a responsibility-first message

Here is an original fictional script:

“We sent two conflicting versions of the setup instructions to the same customer group. That caused some teams to repeat work they should not have needed to do. I am sorry. We were responsible for making the instructions consistent before sending them.

“We have stopped the conflicting message and verified a corrected version. Our support team is contacting the affected group through the established support route. We are still reviewing how the approval process failed, so I am not going to claim that review is complete. We will publish the next update at the checkpoint listed beside this recording.”

Use this structure only with your own verified facts. Do not borrow its completed actions if your team has not performed them.

Record in a straightforward frame with clear audio. Speak to the affected customer, not to an imagined audience evaluating your leadership. Avoid dramatic music, promotional footage, or a closing request to celebrate the team's resilience.

If exact wording matters, read from approved text. A natural delivery is valuable, but not at the expense of changing the remedy or its conditions.

## Review the recording for evasion and ambiguity

Listen once without watching. Underline where the apology occurs, where responsibility is assigned, and where the customer's next step is explained. If any of those are missing, revise before evaluating the picture.

Check the verbs. “We are improving” is less specific than “We have assigned an owner to review every version before release,” when that is true. “Resolved” should refer to a defined completed action, not the team's hope that customers will move on.

Then watch the recording with the written note. Confirm that dates, affected groups, and response routes match. Important uncertainty should not disappear from the spoken version while remaining in small print.

Ask a reviewer to identify what the company has already done and what remains open. If those categories blur together, revise the sequence or wording.

Do not remove a useful acknowledgment because it makes the founder look less polished. Equally, do not retain a theatrical pause because it appears emotional. Judge whether the message is clear, respectful, and accurate.

## Make the follow-up part of the apology

Publish the recording with a dated written explanation and the practical response route. People who cannot watch should still be able to find the remedy and any action required.

Assign ownership of replies. Some customers may need an individual conversation, a correction to their account, or an explanation that the public message cannot provide. Do not ask them to post private details publicly to receive help.

At the promised checkpoint, report what was completed, what changed, and what remains unresolved. If there is no new conclusion, explain the current state rather than disappearing until you have a more flattering update.

Keep the apology separate from a marketing success story. Customer forgiveness is not a performance metric you can infer from view counts or a few supportive comments.
