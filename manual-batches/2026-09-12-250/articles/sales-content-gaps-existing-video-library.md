Find sales content gaps by comparing real buyer questions with the answers your existing video library can actually provide. Watch the relevant passages rather than trusting titles. Mark each question as covered, hard to find, partial, outdated, or missing, then choose retrieval, repair, or new production based on the specific gap.

## Collect questions before counting assets

Start with questions buyers ask during evaluation, demonstrations, and follow-up. Use available call notes, approved recordings, sales messages, and relevant support patterns. Record the question in the buyer’s language and the situation in which it arose.

Avoid turning the exercise into a list of videos the team wishes it had. “We need more case studies” names a format, not an unanswered question. “How does a team handle a change after the monthly report is prepared?” gives you something concrete to inspect.

[HubSpot’s content-gap guide](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/content-gap-analysis) frames gaps around overlooked topics, questions, and information. Apply that idea narrowly to sales usefulness rather than assuming every competitor keyword represents a missing sales asset.

Keep private account details out of the shared audit. Preserve the business question without copying sensitive customer context into a public planning document.

Group genuinely equivalent questions, but do not merge different decisions simply because they use similar words. “Can I export a report?” and “Can another team verify the exported result?” may require different evidence even when both mention reporting.

## Inventory answers, not filenames

Search the library using the question’s language and likely product terms. Inspect candidate videos at the relevant passages. A title can suggest coverage while the recording only mentions the topic in passing.

For each useful passage, record the video, timestamp range, answer supplied, necessary context, and currentness. Note whether a salesperson can share it directly or whether it depends on a long introduction, internal details, or an outdated interface.

Look beyond dedicated sales assets when appropriate. A product workshop or support explanation may contain a clearer answer than a polished overview. [Zendesk’s guidance on choosing help articles](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/4408894140826-Getting-started-with-self-service-Part-3-Determining-what-articles-you-need-to-create) points to existing support interactions as source material. Use approved patterns as clues to questions, not as permission to publish private conversations.

Distinguish an answer from proof. A speaker saying a feature exists may not show the workflow a buyer needs to evaluate. If the question concerns how something works, note whether the relevant action is visible.

The inventory should help a colleague find a usable answer, not merely prove that the company owns many recordings.

## Classify the gap with a coverage matrix

Use this fictional matrix for a reporting product. The examples illustrate audit categories, not verified findings about any real library.

| Buyer question | Existing evidence | Coverage status | Next move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How do I export the monthly view? | Clear current demo, buried in a workshop | Hard to find | Add a labeled chapter and sharing reference |
| What happens after a report is corrected? | Explanation stops before the revised output | Partial | Record the missing result and repair the segment |
| Can another reviewer inspect the change? | No relevant passage found | Missing | Define a new focused demonstration |
| Where is the export control? | Old interface tutorial | Outdated | Replace the changed navigation |
| What does the summary include? | Two current videos answer the same question | Covered with duplicates | Choose a primary sharing asset |

Add a confidence field. “Missing after checking these candidate assets” is more useful than an absolute claim that no answer exists anywhere.

The categories prevent unnecessary production. A retrieval gap needs better navigation; a partial answer needs a repair; a genuinely absent explanation may need new footage. These are different work products and should not all become requests for another general demo.

## Prioritize by decision relevance and repair effort

Choose the gaps that block useful buyer understanding, not simply those attached to the loudest internal request. Ask what decision the unanswered question affects and whether the current sales team can explain it reliably without improvising.

Use qualitative priorities when you lack measured frequency. A question may be important because it determines whether the product fits a workflow, even if it appears rarely. Conversely, a frequently requested overview may already exist but be difficult to find.

Estimate effort in practical terms: add navigation, extract a current passage, replace an outdated scene, or record a new example. Do not invent precise production costs or turnaround times when the team has not assessed them.

Look for repairs that close several genuinely related questions without creating another oversized asset. A focused demonstration of the corrected-report process might answer both the change and review questions, provided the resulting video remains easy to navigate.

Document why each priority was chosen. “Relevant to a current evaluation and repairable with one missing result scene” is a defensible explanation. A numeric score with arbitrary weights can conceal more judgment than it clarifies.

## Turn the audit into specific reuse briefs

For a retrieval gap, specify the primary asset, relevant range, reader-facing label, and context needed before sharing. The deliverable may be a chapter link or a short internal description, not a new video.

For a partial answer, define the missing step or evidence. State the starting condition, what must be demonstrated, and what completion should look like. Give the editor the existing passage so they can preserve useful material.

For an outdated answer, name the exact mismatch and its consequence. “The control moved” leads to a bounded recapture. “The product has changed” leaves the producer guessing.

For a missing answer, write a new-production brief centered on the buyer’s decision. Avoid asking for an entire product overview when the gap concerns one scenario. Include the limitations or exceptions the explanation must preserve.

For duplicates, select a primary sharing version based on accuracy and usefulness. Do not delete assets as part of a content audit without the appropriate separate decision. The immediate job is to guide use, not erase history or reorganize the whole organization’s media system.

## Test the repaired library with real retrieval tasks

Ask a salesperson who did not conduct the audit to find an answer using the revised references. Give them the buyer’s question, not the asset title. Observe whether they locate the right passage and understand when it is appropriate to share.

Then ask them to explain what the asset does not answer. This catches an important failure mode: a video can be easy to find yet still be used too broadly. A sample workflow should not become evidence for every configuration.

Check new or repaired recordings against the original question. Do they close the identified gap, or have they expanded into another general presentation? Mark the coverage status only after inspecting the finished material.

Keep the matrix focused on the active sales questions. Review it when product changes or recurring buyer questions make the existing answers insufficient. Do not turn it into an unrelated publishing calendar.

The final output is a usable coverage map and a small set of precise actions. It should reduce guesswork about whether an answer exists and distinguish finding, fixing, and creating content before the team commits production resources.
