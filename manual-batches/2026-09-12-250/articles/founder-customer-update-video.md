Record a founder update for existing customers around one question they already care about. Show a current artifact, explain what changed or what you learned, name the remaining limitation, and give a clear next checkpoint. The video should add founder context to their working relationship, not become a list of releases or a report for investors.

## Select a customer question that needs founder context

Start with a concern you have actually heard through support, customer conversations, or an advisory discussion. Do not invent a frequently asked question because it creates a convenient opening for a product announcement.

A useful question might be: “Why did you simplify the approval step instead of adding another dashboard?” It invites a founder to explain priorities and trade-offs. A narrow question about which button to click belongs in a support answer, not necessarily a founder update.

[Wistia describes using personal videos to answer customer questions](https://wistia.com/blog/how-to-use-video-to-improve-customer-satisfaction). The application here is a periodic founder explanation, not a claim that video will improve satisfaction or reduce support volume.

Write the audience precisely. Existing administrators, daily contributors, and procurement contacts may need different information. Choose the group whose work the update addresses and avoid broad language that implies every customer is affected.

If you cannot explain why the founder is the right speaker, ask whether a product or support colleague would provide a better answer.

## Choose an artifact that makes the update concrete

An artifact is something current that the viewer can inspect: a revised instruction, a released interface element, a decision note, or a clearly labeled example. It should make the explanation more specific than a talking head alone.

Choose one artifact, not a tour of everything the company has done. Ask what it establishes. A revised instruction proves the wording changed; it does not prove that customers now understand it or that a business outcome improved.

Check its status and date. If it is a prototype, label it as such and do not narrate it as available functionality. If it contains customer information, prepare an approved example before recording.

Use a simple artifact card: name, current status, customer question it answers, point to show, and claim it cannot support. Keep that card with the recording plan.

The strongest artifact is not always visually impressive. A single before-and-after sentence can be more useful than a polished dashboard if the customer's concern is an ambiguous instruction.

## Build a five-part update rundown

Create five rows: question, artifact, interpretation, limitation, and checkpoint. Write one sentence in each row before drafting the full narration.

The question establishes relevance. The artifact makes the subject visible. The interpretation is the founder's contribution: why the team chose this response and what trade-off it accepted. The limitation prevents the update from becoming blanket reassurance. The checkpoint explains how the conversation continues.

[Buffer's account of opening its roadmap invites customers to contribute feedback](https://buffer.com/resources/transparent-product-roadmap/). That historical example supports treating communication as a conversation. It does not mean every request becomes a commitment or that you should publish a roadmap in every video.

Keep the checkpoint specific enough to be useful. “We will keep you posted” does not tell customers where to look. “We will add the next decision to the existing update page after the review” gives a route, provided your team has agreed to do it.

If the update contains a future date, verify that it refers to a communication checkpoint rather than accidentally promising product delivery.

## Record a two-scene example

Imagine a fictional founder whose company provides review software. Customers have asked why the team changed the approval wording before adding a requested reporting view.

In scene one, the founder addresses them directly: “Some workspace owners asked why we changed the approval instruction first. I want to show the ambiguity we were trying to remove and explain the choice.”

In scene two, the founder displays an original fictional before-and-after instruction. The old example says, “Finish review.” The revised example says, “Send to the workspace owner for approval.” The founder explains that the second sentence distinguishes completing a review from granting approval.

The limitation follows immediately: “This wording change does not add a new approval role or a reporting view.” The checkpoint identifies where customers can comment on whether the distinction matches their workflow.

This is an illustrative script, not a real product claim. Its job is to demonstrate how one artifact can carry the update. The founder does not claim measured improvement, universal customer agreement, or completion of the separate reporting request.

## Review for usefulness without turning it into a showcase

Watch the draft from the selected customer's perspective. Can they state what the artifact shows and why the founder made the decision? If the answer is mostly company activity, revise.

Remove funding milestones, hiring news, or internal achievements unless they directly explain a customer consequence. Existing customers do not need to congratulate the company before learning what affects their work.

Check the limitation with particular care. It should be spoken clearly and remain visible long enough to understand. Do not place an important exception only in a description below the video.

Review the artifact at ordinary viewing size. If the relevant detail is unreadable, simplify the visual or show a closer approved view. Do not rely on the viewer pausing and zooming.

Ask a colleague to write a one-sentence summary after watching. If they infer a broader release or promise than you intended, revise the pairing of image and narration rather than adding a vague disclaimer at the end.

## Close the customer loop after the recording

Publish the video beside a short written summary, a date, and the agreed response route. Customers who cannot watch should still be able to find the practical point and any required action.

Route replies to someone who understands the decision and its limits. A founder update can invite thoughtful questions; it should not create an inbox nobody monitors. Record what the replies clarify without calling a small set of responses a market-wide finding.

For the next update, refer back to the checkpoint if it remains relevant. Say what changed, what did not, and why. Do not restart the narrative as though the previous commitment never existed.

Keep the series modest enough to sustain with useful material. Frequency is an editorial choice, not a performance guarantee. Skip an empty update rather than manufacturing progress.
