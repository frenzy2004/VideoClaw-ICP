Review a video editor's classification from the actual working relationship, not the freelance label, invoice format, or location of their laptop. Gather facts about control, methods, expenses, tools, and continuity, then obtain qualified advice on federal tax, labor, and relevant state rules before extending an arrangement whose responsibilities have changed.

## Start with the relationship you actually operate

An editor may begin with a defined project and later become part of daily company operations. Perhaps the business now sets their hours, trains them in detailed methods, assigns ongoing tasks, and expects continuous availability.

Those changes are reasons to review the arrangement, not proof of a particular classification. Record what happens in practice and how it differs from the original agreement.

The [IRS common-law guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/employee-common-law-employee) says the relationship's substance matters rather than its label and groups relevant facts around behavioral control, financial control, and the parties' relationship. That guidance concerns federal tax analysis.

Do not use it as a complete answer to every employment-law question. The buyer needs a clear factual record and the appropriate separate reviews, not a convenient label that fits the preferred budget.

## Document control without rewriting the facts

Ask who decides the methods, order of work, schedule, tools, and assignment of assistance. Distinguish a requirement for a finished result from detailed direction about how the person performs the work.

[The IRS behavioral-control page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/behavioral-control) identifies instructions, evaluation systems, and training as relevant evidence. It also discusses the right to control, not only instructions actively given on a particular day.

For an editor, useful records might include required working hours, recurring training, process instructions, approval chains, and whether they may choose their own methods. Record the context and any exceptions rather than reducing each item to a yes/no score.

Do not change emails, contracts, or descriptions to make the relationship appear more independent. If the current facts create uncertainty, preserve them and ask what a compliant arrangement would require.

## Assemble the non-diagnostic facts packet

Use this original worksheet to prepare for an adviser. It does not calculate a status or rank the risk.

| Topic | Facts to record | Supporting example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work scope | Defined project or ongoing assignments | Current statement of work and task history |
| Methods and direction | Who controls how work is performed | Actual instructions and review practices |
| Schedule and location | Required hours, availability and workplace | Calendar expectations and communications |
| Tools and expenses | Who supplies, pays for and selects them | Equipment and reimbursement arrangements |
| Business independence | Other clients and ability to offer services | Information voluntarily supplied for review |
| Financial arrangement | Payment basis and relevant business costs | Actual agreement and invoices |
| Relationship duration | Expected continuity and changes over time | Renewal history and current expectations |
| Assistance | Whether others may perform or support work | Contract terms and real practice |

Add where the company and worker are located and where the work is performed. Cross-border or multistate facts should be visible early.

Separate known facts from assumptions and unanswered questions. “We think the editor has other clients” is not the same as confirmed information. Avoid unnecessary collection of private details unrelated to the review.

## Keep tax, labor, and state questions separate

Ask the adviser which rules apply to the specific relationship and for which purpose. Federal tax treatment, wage-and-hour obligations, unemployment insurance, workers' compensation, and state employment rules should not be collapsed into one generic checklist.

[California's Department of Industrial Relations](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/faq_independentcontractor.htm) discusses the ABC test, the Borello test, and exceptions with particular conditions. Its guidance illustrates why an occupation name alone should not be treated as an automatic exemption.

Do not assume California's framework applies everywhere, or that a federal tax conclusion settles California or another state's requirements. Likewise, do not assume a service platform has resolved every issue simply because it facilitates contractor payments.

Ask the reviewer to identify the current governing sources, applicable exceptions, and unresolved jurisdictional questions. Classification rules and enforcement positions can change; obtain advice current to the actual decision rather than relying on an old contract template.

## Review the changed arrangement before renewing

Prepare a short change summary: how the engagement began, what duties or control changed, when they changed, and what the business now expects. This can be more useful than sending an adviser a folder of invoices without context.

For example, an editor originally hired for one deliverable might now attend daily staff meetings, follow company-defined methods, and receive a continuous stream of assignments. Those facts deserve examination together; none is presented here as a standalone verdict.

Ask what practical options fit the business's real needs. If the business requires an employee-like role, do not seek wording that disguises it. If an independent service arrangement is appropriate under the applicable rules, ask what operational and contractual conditions must actually be maintained.

Address past periods separately where necessary. A new agreement may not answer questions about earlier work. Obtain qualified guidance rather than assuming a prospective change resolves everything that came before.

## Keep the decision and future review triggers

Document who reviewed the facts, what purposes and jurisdictions the review covered, what arrangement was selected, and what responsibilities remain. Keep sensitive advice and records in an appropriate business location.

Tell the operational manager what the reviewed arrangement permits and requires. A carefully drafted agreement can become disconnected from practice if a manager later imposes different hours, methods, or responsibilities.

Identify material changes that trigger another review: ongoing employment-like duties, new locations, expanded control, a different payment arrangement, or a substantially longer engagement. This is a practical reminder to revisit the facts, not a promise that a fixed review interval guarantees compliance.

Do not present the worksheet to the editor as a demand to agree with a predetermined status. Invite accurate corrections to the factual record and handle the relationship transparently.

The useful outcome is a production staffing decision grounded in the real work and qualified, current advice. It is not a self-issued contractor certificate or a numerical score designed to justify the label the business wanted at the outset.
