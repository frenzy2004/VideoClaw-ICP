Prepare for a founder media interview by learning the journalist’s focus, rehearsing direct answers to likely questions, and organizing the evidence behind your claims. Practice saying what you do not know as clearly as what you know. Aim to help the reporter understand the company, not deliver an uninterrupted sales presentation.

## Define what the conversation should clarify

Begin with the interview’s subject and audience. A trade journalist exploring a workflow problem needs different context from a general business reporter introducing the company. Ask about the planned topic and format, while recognizing that the journalist may ask additional questions.

Write one understanding you want the audience to leave with. For a fictional startup building maintenance software, that might be: “The product lets a facilities team share a defined repair task with an outside contractor.” It should be understandable without a funding amount or a list of investors.

Then name the obvious follow-up questions. What information is shared? Who can access it? What happens when the contractor works offline? These questions test the substance of the explanation.

[PRSA’s media-training guidance](https://prsay.prsa.org/2021/03/29/media-training-lessons-from-oprah-winfreys-interview-with-prince-harry-and-meghan-markle/) recommends researching the interviewer and practicing likely questions. Use that preparation to become responsive, rather than trying to force every answer toward a preferred slogan.

## Build an answer ladder

A useful response has a clear starting point and optional depth. Prepare it in four layers.

| Layer | Purpose | Example function |
|---|---|---|
| Direct answer | Address what was asked | State whether the capability exists today |
| Concrete example | Make the answer understandable | Describe one real task or situation |
| Limit | Prevent an overbroad impression | Name the relevant unsupported condition |
| Follow-up | Resolve a remaining factual question | Offer a document or a verified answer later |

You will not need every layer in every response. A simple factual question may need one sentence. A complicated question may need an example and a boundary.

Keep the ladder on a preparation page, not as a paragraph to memorize. Each layer should contain a fact or a useful idea. If you cannot describe the direct answer without company jargon, work on the underlying explanation.

The sequence also helps when a question arrives unexpectedly. Answer its actual subject first. Add context only when the context helps the reporter interpret that answer.

## Rehearse the uncomfortable questions

List questions that make you want to change the subject. They might concern a missing feature, a customer limitation, or why a larger company could not build something similar.

For each question, separate discomfort from confidentiality. A difficult question is not automatically a private question. You may be able to answer honestly even when the answer is less flattering than the marketing copy.

The [PRSA ethics code](https://www.prsa.org/professional-development/prsa-resources/ethics) emphasizes accurate communication and protection of confidential information. In practice, prepare the public fact and the boundary before the interview.

For example, “We do not publish individual customer contracts” states a boundary. “I can explain how access works in the product today” offers useful information without pretending to answer the private question.

Avoid rehearsing invented certainty. If you do not know how a future feature will work, practice saying so. A confident forecast does not become more supportable because you have repeated it several times.

## Work through a mock exchange

This original exchange uses a fictional company and fictional product facts.

**Reporter:** “Does this remove the need for a maintenance coordinator?”

**Weak answer:** “We are transforming the future of operational collaboration through a powerful platform.”

The response sounds prepared but leaves the question unanswered. It also encourages the reporter to guess what the founder means.

**Stronger answer:** “No. The coordinator still decides what work should happen and who should do it. Our current workflow gives an outside contractor access to selected repair tasks. It does not make staffing decisions.”

The stronger response gives a direct answer, one concrete capability, and a boundary. It does not need a long list of features.

**Reporter:** “How much time does that save?”

**Stronger answer:** “We have not established a general time-saving figure. I can show the current workflow and explain which manual sharing steps it changes. I would not turn that demonstration into a claim about every customer.”

Now add a follow-up only if it exists. Do not promise a study, customer introduction, or technical document simply to move past the question.

## Review one recording with a narrow scorecard

Ask a colleague to run a short mock interview. Have them vary the wording and interrupt with a genuine request for clarification. The point is to practice a conversation, not perform a memorized sequence.

Watch the recording once for substance. Did you answer the question? Did you use a specific example? Did any sentence imply more than you know? Mark the timestamp of a problem rather than writing a vague note such as “be more confident.”

Listen again without watching the image. This can reveal a sentence that is grammatically complete but difficult to follow aloud. Rewrite that thought into shorter spoken units.

Finally, review delivery. Look for rushing, filling every silence, or smiling automatically during a serious answer. Choose one change for the next attempt. Trying to repair voice, posture, wording, pace, and gestures simultaneously can make the rehearsal less useful.

Stop when the main facts are clear and the answers remain flexible. More takes are useful only if they address a specific remaining problem.

## Prepare the follow-up before the interview ends

Keep a small factual reference page nearby: correct names, product availability, dates that matter, and the location of supporting information. Do not keep an entire script open if it encourages reading past the journalist’s question.

During the conversation, note any answer you promised to verify. At the end, repeat the outstanding item and agree on a practical way to send it. If the interview concerns a changing situation, make clear which facts were current at the time.

Afterward, send the promised information concisely. Correct a factual mistake you made without trying to rewrite the journalist’s framing or demand approval of the article.
