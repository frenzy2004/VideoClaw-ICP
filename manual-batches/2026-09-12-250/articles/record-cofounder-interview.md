Record a cofounder interview by giving each person a distinct contribution to the same customer question. Assign first answers according to firsthand knowledge, agree on a simple interruption cue, and test both voices in the chosen shot. The result should reveal two useful perspectives, not two founders repeating the company story for the camera.

## Choose one decision worth hearing from two people

Two founders do not automatically make a stronger interview. They become useful together when each knows something the other cannot fully explain. Start with a decision customers care about, such as why you changed support hours or narrowed the first version of a feature.

Avoid a broad prompt like “Tell us about the company.” It invites both people to deliver the same polished introduction. Ask instead: “Why did we choose scheduled onboarding instead of opening access immediately?” One founder may know the customer confusion; the other may know the operational constraint.

Write the viewer's question at the top of the plan. Beneath it, write what each founder personally observed. If both entries are identical, either find a different question or consider whether a solo message would be clearer.

The purpose is not to manufacture disagreement. A useful conversation can show shared judgment while making the different evidence behind it visible.

## Build a perspective-and-turn map

Create a worksheet with five columns: customer question, first speaker, firsthand evidence, second speaker's contribution, and fact to verify. Fill it before drafting individual answers.

For a fictional scheduling startup, one row might read: “Why introduce an approval step? First speaker: customer cofounder. Evidence: specific permission questions from support conversations. Second contribution: technical cofounder explains which actions require ownership. Verify: the exact roles affected.”

A second row could ask what the team chose not to change. A third could ask what customers should do if the new process does not match their situation. These rows move the conversation forward instead of circling the rationale.

[Riverside recommends preparing interview participants on timing, topics, and the recording process](https://riverside.com/blog/record-video-interview). Use your map as that briefing. Share the questions and factual boundaries, but do not require both founders to memorize a script.

Add a “not mine to answer” note where necessary. A founder should be able to hand over a technical detail without pretending to know it firsthand.

## Set a shared shot that supports turn-taking

For an in-person conversation, begin with a simple frame that includes both speakers comfortably. Place the camera where the audience can see their expressions without either founder needing to twist toward it.

Sketch the arrangement in words: camera in front; founders seated with enough space to turn slightly toward each other; notes outside the shot; optional interviewer close enough that neither speaker looks far away. Use the sketch as a testable plan, not a studio specification.

Record a short exchange in which each person speaks at their ordinary volume. Listen to the saved file. One clear voice does not prove that the second person was captured well. Check whether turning toward the other founder changes audibility.

If you use separate microphones, confirm which recorded track contains each person. If you use a shared microphone, test the actual positions and reject a layout where one speaker sounds consistently distant.

Agree on who opens and closes the message. During the conversation, let the founders address each other naturally. Make direct audience address deliberate rather than switching targets unpredictably.

## Ask follow-ups that expose the difference in knowledge

Treat the question list as a guide to understanding. [Wistia's studio team recommends listening closely and pursuing useful follow-ups](https://wistia.com/blog/how-to-interview-podcast-guests-remotely). The original map gives you direction; the answer tells you what needs clarification.

Suppose the customer cofounder says, “People were confused about permissions.” A useful follow-up is: “What did they think would happen?” That asks for an observable misunderstanding. “How exciting was the solution?” asks for promotional emotion instead.

Then invite the technical cofounder to add a different layer: “Which part of that misunderstanding could the interface prevent, and which part still needs explanation?” The second answer should not restate the first in technical language.

Use a concrete example when the exchange becomes abstract. Ask both founders to walk through the same fictional customer situation from their respective viewpoints. Make clear that the example is illustrative if it did not happen to a real customer.

Keep genuine uncertainty visible. “We have not resolved that edge case” can be a useful boundary, provided the customer also learns where to get help.

## Rehearse an interruption recovery, not every answer

Agree on a neutral cue such as “Finish that thought” or a small hand signal. The cue should tell everyone what happens next without assigning blame.

Here is an original fictional exchange:

Interviewer: “Why did you keep approval with the workspace owner?”

Customer cofounder: “The important confusion was whether a member could change—”

Technical cofounder: “Because the permission model—”

Interviewer: “Let Maya finish the customer example, then we will come to the permission model.”

Customer cofounder: “Members thought opening the settings page would change the workspace. We needed to distinguish viewing an option from approving it.”

Technical cofounder: “That distinction helped us decide which control belonged to the owner.”

If overlapping speech makes a sentence unusable, pause and restart that complete answer. Do not pretend the original exchange was clean. Record enough context that the repaired answer still responds honestly to the question.

Also practice a factual correction: “Let me correct the role name before we continue.” It is better to repair a detail plainly than have both founders work around the error for the rest of the conversation.

## Review for contribution and accuracy before polish

After recording, review the interview against the map. Did each founder add firsthand knowledge? Did the viewer receive an answer to the original question? Did one speaker repeatedly lose their turn?

Do not use equal speaking time as the only measure of balance. Some questions belong mostly to one founder. The relevant issue is whether the second voice contributes something necessary and has room to finish it.

Check every named role, date, and customer instruction. Confirm that edits have not joined two separate answers into an apparent agreement neither founder made. Keep unresolved points explicit.

Before release, let both founders review factual meaning and any private details. This is not an invitation to remove every thoughtful pause or disagreement.
