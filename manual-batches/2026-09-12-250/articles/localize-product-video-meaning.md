Localize a product video by preserving what the viewer must understand and do, not by translating each sentence in isolation. Build a terminology and meaning brief, review narration alongside the interface, and test the complete localized viewing path. Use qualified language review before publishing, especially for product terms, conditions, and calls to action.

## Define the locale and the task

Specify the audience’s language and regional context, then name the task the recording supports. A general introduction and an exact setup demonstration require different levels of interface matching. Do not treat “translate into Spanish” as a complete brief if the product terminology, examples, or destination vary by market.

Check whether the product interface is available in the target language. If it remains in the source language, the localized narration must help viewers find the real labels they will see. Translating a button name into a word absent from the interface can make an otherwise fluent tutorial unusable.

Choose the version of the recording to localize and freeze its factual content before translation. A changing master creates avoidable rework and can leave language versions describing different workflows.

Define what stays unchanged: product capabilities, required conditions, limitations, and the demonstrated outcome. Define what may adapt: idioms, examples, sentence structure, and explanatory pacing. This boundary lets language specialists make the content natural without expanding its claims.

Start with a small, useful recording if the team is still learning how to review localized material.

## Create a terminology and meaning brief

List product names, interface labels, abbreviations, and domain terms. For each, provide the intended meaning, the visible label, and whether it should remain untranslated. Include a pronunciation reference when a spoken product name or technical term could be ambiguous.

Do not hand over a transcript alone. Add the video, screenshots of important states, and notes explaining what the viewer is supposed to notice. A translator needs to know whether “hold” means pause playback, reserve stock, or delay a transaction.

Use a meaning statement for every important claim. “The user selects which warehouse to inspect” is more robust than a phrase whose grammar is tightly tied to one language. Preserve conditions such as “in this sample account” or “after the report is generated.”

[YouTube’s automatic-dubbing guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15569972?hl=en) notes possible errors involving proper nouns, idioms, and jargon. Treat generated audio as material requiring review, not as proof that specialized terminology is correct.

Give reviewers a place to flag uncertain terms. Resolving an ambiguity early is better than recording a fluent but incorrect explanation.

## Use a meaning-preservation worksheet

Consider a fictional inventory video being adapted for another language. The product interface remains in English, while narration and supporting text will be localized.

| Source element | Meaning to preserve | Localization instruction | Review question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Warehouse selector | Choose the location being inspected | Retain the visible control name, explain it naturally | Can viewers find the real control? |
| Available stock | Quantity shown under the product’s defined status | Use the approved glossary definition | Has it been confused with total stock? |
| Sample shortage | Illustrative scenario, not customer evidence | Preserve the sample-data qualification | Does the example still sound hypothetical? |
| Export action | Open the current report export | Match the actual interface label | Does the narration point to the right action? |
| Final invitation | View the localized setup guide | Use the destination’s real title | Is that guide available in the promised language? |

This worksheet is not a translation. It gives the language reviewer the context needed to produce and assess one.

Add a timing note where a translated phrase may need more space. Keep meaning and pronunciation decisions separate from timing adjustments so an editor does not shorten away an important condition merely to fit the original gap.

## Choose audio and text treatments deliberately

Decide whether the localized version needs translated captions, a new spoken track, changed on-screen labels, or a combination. Captions alone may work for some uses; a narrated product explanation may need more extensive adaptation.

[YouTube’s multi-language feature documentation](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13338784?hl=en) distinguishes uploading prepared language tracks from automatic dubbing. Check the current feature access and file requirements for the channel before planning the publishing workflow.

Review the audio against the picture. A fluent sentence can still arrive after the action it describes. Extend a hold, adjust the narration, or recut a transition when necessary. Avoid speeding speech until the explanation becomes difficult to follow.

Do not assume translated captions are automatically correct because a localized audio track exists. Verify the relevant text and language settings for the publishing destination. Check speaker names, interface labels, and terms that may have multiple meanings.

If on-screen text cannot be changed cleanly, decide how narration or a supporting label will explain it. Be explicit about the real interface rather than covering it with a translation that suggests a product version the viewer does not have.

## Run a bilingual task-based review

Use a reviewer who understands both the language and the product task, or pair a language specialist with someone who can verify the workflow. Ask them to assess meaning and usability, not just grammatical fluency.

Have the reviewer follow the demonstration. Can they identify the starting condition, locate the control, understand the state change, and recognize the result? Ask them to flag points where the narration and screen disagree.

Run a separate listening pass without reading the source transcript. This catches unnatural pacing, confusing pronunciation, and a sentence that sounds plausible on paper but is difficult to understand aloud. Then inspect captions and on-screen text at the intended display size.

Record corrections by timestamp and issue type: meaning, terminology, pronunciation, timing, or destination. That makes the feedback actionable for different contributors.

Do not resolve a disputed product meaning through stylistic preference. Return to the verified source behavior. If the source itself is ambiguous, clarify it before publishing any language version. Localization should expose uncertainty, not hide it beneath a polished voice track.

## Localize the viewing path as well as the file

Check the title, description, thumbnail text, captions, audio selection, and destination in the actual viewer experience. A localized video with an untranslated invitation can leave the viewer stranded at the final step.

Follow every promised link. If the setup guide is only available in the source language, say so clearly or provide an appropriate alternative. Do not imply localized support, features, or documentation that the team cannot actually provide.

Keep a relationship between the master version and each localized asset. When the product workflow changes, identify which narration lines, visual states, and supporting resources need rechecking. A new master does not automatically make old language tracks accurate.

Review automatic publication settings where applicable. The team should understand whether generated tracks can appear before its intended review. Use the platform’s supported controls to match the chosen editorial process.

The completed work is a coherent localized explanation, not simply a translated audio file. The viewer should receive the same factual meaning, find the real product controls, and reach a next step they can actually use.
