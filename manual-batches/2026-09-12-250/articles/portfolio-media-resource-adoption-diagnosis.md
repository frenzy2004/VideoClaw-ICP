Diagnose unused portfolio media resources by separating awareness, relevance, usability and usefulness problems before choosing an intervention. A low view count cannot tell you which barrier matters. Build a short evidence sheet around one resource, talk to eligible users and test a specific change rather than relaunching the entire program.

## Define the resource and eligible audience

“People do not use the library” is too broad to investigate. Pick a resource or tightly related set of resources, such as an approved customer-interview pack for companies preparing their first case study. Define who could reasonably need it and what action would show useful engagement.

The eligible audience is not necessarily every company in the portfolio. A fundraising storytelling guide may be irrelevant to a company that is not fundraising. A resource can be valuable to a small group without being useful to everyone, so keep audience fit separate from popularity.

[Canto’s implementation guidance](https://www.canto.com/glossary/digital-asset-management-implementation/) includes user training alongside library setup. That is a useful reminder that making resources available is not the same as making them usable. It does not establish that training is the right solution to every adoption problem. Begin with evidence about this resource and this audience.

Write a one-sentence investigation brief: “Understand why companies currently planning customer interviews do or do not use the shared interview pack.” This keeps the work focused on a practical support decision.

## Separate four possible barriers

Use four hypotheses as a starting frame, not as an exhaustive theory of user behavior. Awareness asks whether people know the resource exists. Relevance asks whether it matches their current job. Usability asks whether they can access and apply it. Usefulness asks whether applying it helps them accomplish that job.

These barriers can coexist. A founder may have seen the announcement, opened the resource and still found its examples unsuitable for their customer type. Sending another announcement would address awareness while leaving the real obstacle untouched.

Build an evidence sheet with a column for each hypothesis and a place for disconfirming evidence. If a participant can describe the resource accurately, that weakens the awareness hypothesis for that person. If they cannot open a permissioned template, that supports an access problem but says little about the quality of the template itself.

Do not turn the framework into labels for people. “Low-adoption founder” is not a useful category. Record the situation: “Needed interview guidance, found the pack, could not identify which worksheet to start with.”

## Gather behavior and explanation together

Review available usage evidence with its limitations attached. [Atlassian’s knowledge-base reporting documentation](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-service-management-cloud/docs/see-how-your-knowledge-base-articles-are-performing/) distinguishes signals such as views, sharing and helpfulness. These are different observations; none alone proves that a resource changed a company’s work.

A view might represent curiosity, accidental navigation or repeated attempts to understand confusing instructions. A lack of views might reflect a direct download shared elsewhere. Record what the measurement actually captures before using it to select participants or judge the resource.

Pair the available signals with short conversations and a realistic application task. Ask what the person was trying to do, what they used instead, where they encountered difficulty and what happened next. Invite examples of alternative resources without implying that using something else was a mistake.

Include people who did not use the resource, not only enthusiastic participants who reply quickly. If you cannot reach non-users, state that limitation. Keep feedback private at the company level unless participants have agreed to another use; the investigation should not become a public comparison of founder engagement.

## Build an intervention card from the evidence

Consider a fictional customer-interview pack shared with several portfolio companies. The operator gathers observations and chooses a different response for each barrier.

| Observation | Working explanation | Bounded intervention | Evidence to collect next |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A company did not know the pack existed | Awareness gap | Add it to the relevant support conversation | Can the contact locate it later? |
| The pack assumes an enterprise customer | Relevance gap | Add a clearly labeled small-customer example | Can the company adapt the example? |
| Users open three worksheets before finding the first step | Usability gap | Add a task-based starting page | Can a new user begin without help? |
| Users complete the worksheet but still cannot brief an interviewer | Usefulness gap | Revise the output into an interview brief | Does the resulting brief answer the interviewer’s questions? |

Choose one intervention for the next cycle. Write its owner, intended audience, proposed change, observation method and review point. Keep the hypothesis explicit: “A task-based starting page may reduce confusion about where to begin.” Do not write a promised adoption uplift.

Also record what you are not changing. If the evidence points to unclear sequencing, the intervention does not require a new library vendor, more resource production or a portfolio-wide training campaign. This boundary makes the test easier to interpret.

## Test application, not just another click

For the starting-page intervention, ask a new participant to prepare a customer-interview brief using the revised pack. Observe whether they choose a starting worksheet, complete the relevant fields and understand the next action. A page view is only the beginning of that sequence.

Compare the result with the original obstacle. If users find the first worksheet but still cannot turn it into a brief, the awareness or navigation repair may have worked while usefulness remains unresolved. Treat that as a more precise diagnosis, not a failed program.

Avoid incentives that make the measurement meaningless. Requiring every company to download a pack may produce downloads without demonstrating relevance. A voluntary, task-specific test gives a different kind of evidence and should be described accordingly.

Keep the test small enough to review the actual outputs. With permission, inspect a completed brief or ask the participant to explain what it enabled. Do not collect confidential customer details simply to demonstrate that the resource was used. A redacted example or verbal walkthrough may answer the operational question.

## Decide whether to improve, reposition or retire

Close the investigation with a concrete decision. Improve a resource when the job is relevant and a repair appears plausible. Reposition it when the content is useful but aimed at the wrong audience or moment. Consider retiring it from everyday discovery when it no longer answers an active need and a better route exists.

Preserve the evidence behind the decision: eligible audience, observations, limitations, intervention and result. This is an adoption diagnosis for a resource, not a full evaluation of the portfolio media program. It should not claim effects on fundraising, revenue or community health.

Share the practical change with the people affected. Explain where the revised resource fits and invite correction if the diagnosed need has changed. The useful final output is a resource with a clearer job—or a deliberate decision to stop promoting it—not a larger collection of activity numbers.
