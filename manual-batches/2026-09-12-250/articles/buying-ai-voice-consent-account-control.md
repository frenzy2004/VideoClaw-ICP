Buy an AI voice only after identifying the speaker, the permission scope, the platform's verification process, and who controls future generation. Treat model access and finished recordings as separate questions. Document approved scripts, users, duration, data settings, and exit arrangements before sending recordings or relying on a supplier's reusable voice offer.

## Identify what the supplier is actually selling

A “branded AI voice” can mean access to a shared voice, a clone created from a particular speaker, a synthetic voice designed without that speaker, or finished narration files produced by the supplier. Those arrangements create different dependencies.

Ask the vendor to name the product, account structure, voice origin, and deliverables. Determine whether your company will generate new audio itself or submit scripts to the vendor. If access is promised, ask how it is granted and what happens when the vendor relationship ends.

Do not provide someone's recordings merely because the supplier says the process is standard. Confirm that the recordings were obtained with appropriate permission for the contemplated use and that the speaker understands the proposed synthetic generation.

The buyer's first work product is a plain description of the transaction. “We will receive a shared voice in a named workspace for these scripts” is more useful than “we will own an AI voice.”

## Follow the platform's actual verification route

Product rules can be more specific than a general statement that the speaker consents. [ElevenLabs' Professional Voice Clone documentation](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/product/voices/voice-cloning/can-i-create-a-professional-voice-clone-of-someone-elses-voice) says the speaker must create and verify their own clone, then can share it privately.

That is a product-specific procedure. It does not establish all permissions needed for your business use, and it should not be generalized to every voice service.

Ask the supplier to demonstrate the legitimate creation and sharing path using authorized accounts. Do not accept an arrangement that requires impersonating the speaker, sharing passwords, bypassing verification, or using unrelated recordings as a substitute.

Record who completes verification and which account retains control. If the speaker creates the voice in their own account, establish how the business receives approved access and how both parties can see or change that access. A successful demo does not by itself prove the business has a durable arrangement.

## Build the voice-permission chain

Use this proposed worksheet to connect each party to a specific permission and control. It is a fact-gathering document, not a legal contract or automated clearance test.

| Link | Record | Question to resolve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speaker | Identity and authorized contact | What synthetic uses have they agreed to? |
| Source recordings | Origin and collection purpose | May these recordings be used for this process? |
| Verification account | Account controlled by the speaker or required party | Does it follow the product's rules? |
| Sharing mechanism | Named workspace or private access route | Who can generate, invite others, or revoke access? |
| Business use | Script categories, placements and duration | Which uses require further approval? |
| Supplier | Processing role and subcontractors | What may the vendor retain or reuse? |
| Exit | End date, withdrawal and retained outputs | What stops and what happens to existing audio? |

Attach the applicable agreement and record unresolved answers. Avoid storing sensitive identity evidence unnecessarily in a broadly shared production folder; reference the authorized record location instead.

Have the speaker and business owner review the plain-language chain before the technical setup. If they understand the arrangement differently, resolve that difference before recordings enter the service.

## Set script boundaries and approval responsibility

Permission for a product tutorial should not quietly become permission for any statement the company might generate later. Ask what categories of scripts, languages, placements, and uses are contemplated.

Name the person who approves scripts before generation and the person who approves the resulting performance before publication. Synthetic narration can mispronounce a name or stress a sentence in a way that changes its apparent meaning.

If scripts may concern sensitive, regulated, personal, or reputational matters, identify those as separate approval questions. Do not assume a broad vendor setting replaces the speaker's agreement or the business's responsibility for its statements.

Keep a practical record connecting the approved script version to the selected audio file. The purpose is not to build an elaborate content system; it is to make the purchased voice arrangement accountable when someone asks what was authorized and what was actually produced.

## Inspect data settings before uploading recordings

Ask where source audio, text, generated recordings, and voice-related data are processed or retained. Request the documentation that applies to the selected plan, not a generic enterprise promise attached to a different offer.

[ElevenLabs' data-use guidance](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/legal/is-my-data-used-to-improve-eleven-labs-ai-models) describes an opt-out whose effect applies to newly submitted data. That makes the timing of the setting important. It does not justify promising retroactive removal of earlier training use.

Ask separately about retention and deletion. A model-improvement preference, deletion of an audio file, removal of a shared voice, and closure of an account may be different operations. Have the vendor explain the consequences relevant to your arrangement.

Use a minimal authorized sample for initial evaluation. Resolve the data questions before sending a large archive simply because more audio is available. For sensitive recordings or complex consent issues, involve the appropriate privacy or legal adviser.

## Define the exit before relying on the voice

Write a concrete end-of-relationship scenario. The speaker withdraws future permission, the supplier stops trading, or the business changes vendors. Who removes access, who confirms it, and what happens to the next scheduled recording?

Separate stopping new generation from the status of recordings already made and published. Ask what the agreement says about each, including retained source data, archived exports, and unfinished work. Do not invent a universal right to keep publishing or a universal obligation to erase everything.

Also confirm whether the voice itself is portable or whether continued generation depends on the original platform and account. If portability is unavailable, compare that dependency with the business's need before buying.

Approve the purchase only when the creation route, permitted uses, account controls, and exit answers are coherent. A realistic consent and access arrangement is more valuable than an impressive voice sample whose future use nobody can explain.
