Turn a private founder lesson into non-identifying notes only after confirming that the discussion's sharing terms allow that use. Extract the useful decision, remove unnecessary identifying context and test whether remaining details still point to a participant. If permission or identification risk remains unresolved, withhold the note rather than treating a pseudonym as protection.

## Confirm the allowed use before editing

Begin with the meeting's recorded terms and any specific instructions covering the proposed written output. This task starts after the conversation; it does not decide whether the meeting should have been recorded. A recording or transcript is not required to create a useful note.

[Chatham House's events code](https://www.chathamhouse.org/events/code-conduct) distinguishes on-record, non-attributable and off-record discussion. Check which arrangement actually applied to this material. Do not assume that a private meeting used the Chatham House Rule or that permission to attend included permission to publish lessons.

Identify the intended audience for the note. Sharing within a small participant group differs from posting in a portfolio-wide library or on a public blog. The same details can be more identifying to people who know the cohort than to an unfamiliar reader.

If the terms are unclear or the proposed audience exceeds the agreed use, pause and ask the responsible meeting owner for a decision. The editor should not resolve uncertainty by removing names and proceeding. This is an editorial safeguard, not a legal determination that a note is anonymous or publication is permitted.

## Extract the decision without carrying the biography

Read the authorized source material and identify the practical lesson: the problem, decision, reason and tradeoff. Most of the useful operating insight may not require the speaker's company stage, geography, customers or fundraising timeline.

For example, a lesson about collecting review feedback may be: “Separate factual approval from stylistic suggestions so the editor knows which changes block release.” That decision can be described without naming the company or recounting the circumstances that made the participant recognizable.

Do not turn a speaker's tentative observation into a proven rule. Preserve uncertainty and scope. “The participant found this useful in one workflow” differs from “This process eliminates approval delays.” Removing attribution should not strengthen the underlying claim.

Keep the source note and the proposed shareable note separate, with access appropriate to each. The source reference allows authorized reviewers to check fidelity, while the shared version contains only the material approved for that audience.

## Work through a fictional transformation sheet

The following is an invented exercise, not a disguised account of a real participant. Assume the meeting owner has confirmed that a short non-attributed process lesson may be shared with the portfolio, subject to review.

| Source detail in the fictional note | Why it matters or creates risk | Editorial action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “The only robotics company in our latest cohort” | Strong clue to company identity | Remove entirely |
| A named customer and a specific launch week | Adds identifying context without explaining the method | Remove entirely |
| Reviewers mixed factual corrections with style preferences | Central operating problem | Retain in general language |
| The editor separated blocking decisions from optional suggestions | Central decision | Retain |
| A claim that the change always makes review faster | Broader than the example supports | Do not include |
| A distinctive phrase used by the participant | May be recognizable and unnecessary | Paraphrase faithfully or omit |

A proposed shared note is: “For a media review, separate decisions that must be resolved before release from optional style suggestions. Name the owner of each blocking decision. This can clarify what the editor is waiting for, but it does not resolve missing approval authority.”

The transformation retains a usable method and an important limitation. It does not invent a different industry, customer or date to disguise the story. If a fictional scenario would teach the lesson better, write it separately and label it as fictional rather than presenting altered facts as the participant's experience.

## Test combinations of identifying clues

The [Chatham House Rule addresses direct and indirect identification](https://www.chathamhouse.org/about-us/chatham-house-rule). For an editorial review, look beyond names to combinations of details: role, sector, cohort, timing, distinctive event, quoted wording and the people who were known to attend.

Ask an authorized reviewer familiar with the intended audience to assess whether the note points to someone. They should not need wider access than the task permits. Record the concern and proposed repair without creating a new widely shared list of identifying clues.

Test the note in its planned placement. A neutral paragraph beside a photograph of the meeting or a list of attendees may reveal more than the paragraph alone. A newsletter introduction such as “Lessons from yesterday's three-founder session” can narrow the source group considerably.

Do not claim that passing this review proves anonymity. Small communities can recognize context in ways an editor misses. If the lesson depends on a distinctive detail that cannot be removed without changing its meaning, withhold it or seek a different, explicitly authorized treatment.

## Check fidelity as well as identification

A note can become less identifying and less accurate at the same time. After removing context, verify that the remaining lesson still represents what the source supports. The original decision may have depended on an unusual constraint that the generalized note now hides.

For the fictional review example, keep the limitation that separating feedback does not create missing approval authority. Without it, readers might treat a clearer worksheet as a solution to a governance problem. Useful synthesis preserves the boundary around the lesson.

Ask the appropriate source or meeting reviewer to check the proposed wording where the agreed process requires it. Keep the review question narrow: whether the authorized lesson is faithful, appropriately scoped and suitable for the intended audience. Do not treat a casual “looks fine” as permission for every future use.

Maintain a disposition record: share as revised, request further authorization, rewrite as a clearly fictional teaching example or withhold. Include the audience and version reviewed. This makes the decision reproducible without exposing the underlying private discussion.

## Publish only the reviewed note and preserve a correction route

Before release, compare the final placement with the reviewed version. Check headings, introductions, images, links and metadata for clues reintroduced during production. An editor may remove a company name from the body while leaving it in a file title or image caption.

Publish through the authorized channel and keep the source material out of the public package. The shareable lesson does not need the raw notes, transcript or participant list attached as proof. Preserve those records only within the appropriate internal process.

Give participants or the meeting owner a clear way to raise a concern about the published note. If a concern arrives, hold further reuse while the responsible owner reviews it, and follow the relevant correction or withdrawal process.

The finished work product is a reviewed transformation sheet and a bounded written lesson—or a documented decision not to share. Withholding is a valid outcome when useful meaning, agreed use and non-identifying context cannot be reconciled.
