Build a nurture sequence from existing content by identifying what a lead still needs to understand, selecting an asset for each next question, and defining clear entry and exit conditions. Write each email around one useful promise. Let explicit actions guide the path without assuming that a click proves readiness to buy.

## Define what the first action tells you

Start with the action that introduces someone to the sequence: requesting a guide, registering for a session or asking about a topic. Describe what that action establishes and what it does not. Downloading a worksheet suggests interest in the worksheet’s problem; it does not establish budget, authority or a purchase deadline.

Use that limited information to choose the first educational question. If the source offer explains how to recognize an unclear handoff, the next message might help the reader identify one in their own team. A product demonstration could become relevant later, but it should not be the automatic answer to every entry action.

[HubSpot’s description of its own nurturing](https://blog.hubspot.com/customers/hubspot-lead-nurturing) separates sequences by purpose. The practical lesson is to give this sequence a specific job rather than collect unrelated promotional messages.

Write a success condition that the team can observe, such as a request for a particular explanation or a response identifying a requirement. Keep broader commercial outcomes separate from what the sequence itself can establish.

## Map the next questions before selecting assets

Write the questions someone might ask after consuming the initial material. These should follow from the problem, not from the order in which your library was produced. A useful progression might move from recognizing a symptom to comparing approaches, seeing a relevant demonstration and checking fit.

[HubSpot’s content-mapping guide](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/31406/How-to-Map-Lead-Nurturing-Content-to-Each-Stage-in-the-Sales-Cycle.aspx) connects content choices to changing buyer questions. Treat the map as a working hypothesis about your audience, not a universal path every person will follow.

For each question, identify an existing asset and the exact section that answers it. A long recording may contain one useful example. You can link to that moment, write a concise companion explanation, or make an appropriate excerpt.

Mark gaps instead of filling them with the nearest available asset. If the sequence needs a comparison and the library contains only introductory material, repeating the introduction with a new subject line will not create the missing explanation.

## Draft a nurture path map

This original example follows a fictional lead who requested a guide to clearer team handoffs. It illustrates educational progression, not a proven conversion sequence.

| Entry or question | Existing material | Email promise | Next observable action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Requested the guide | Expert explanation of ambiguous ownership | Identify one unclear handoff | Use the short diagnostic |
| Wants a practical approach | Recorded comparison of two completion signals | Compare the signals with your process | Select the more relevant example |
| Wants to see the product | Current demonstration of assigning the next action | Watch the relevant interaction | Ask a fit question |
| Has an unresolved requirement | Appropriate technical explanation | Check the stated prerequisite | Request clarification or leave the sequence |

Add entry conditions, exclusions and exit rules beside the map. A person already speaking to sales may need an account-specific response instead of the same educational messages.

The map should also show what happens when nothing is observed. Silence is not a reason to invent a new interest or intensify the claim. Use a bounded plan and stop when continued messages no longer have a useful purpose.

## Write one promise into each email

Each message should answer why this asset is useful now. Avoid opening with the fact that your company has published another video. Connect the material to the question the reader is likely considering, then state what they will find at the destination.

For the fictional second message, a useful opening is: “Two teams can agree that a handoff matters while using different definitions of finished. This comparison shows two completion signals you can discuss with the people doing the work.” The asset’s role is clear without a claim that it will solve every coordination problem.

Keep the call to action consistent with that promise. If the email offers a comparison, link to the comparison rather than a generic product homepage. Give the reader enough context to decide whether to click; do not hide essential qualifications behind the link.

Review adjacent emails together. They should advance different questions rather than repeat the same benefit in slightly different language. If two messages would lead a reader to the same conclusion, combine them or replace one with the missing educational step.

## Use branches that reflect real information

Choose a small number of meaningful branches. An explicit request for a product demonstration is stronger evidence of that interest than a generic page visit. A reply describing a requirement may justify a tailored explanation. Avoid a complicated branching tree based on signals you cannot collect reliably.

Document the interpretation attached to each event. A video start means playback began; it does not prove the person understood the content. A link click can indicate curiosity, but it may not reveal the buyer’s role or intention. Build uncertainty into the route rather than treating every event as a definitive stage change.

Include exit conditions for a relevant human conversation, an explicit stop request, unsuitable audience membership or completion of the sequence’s useful purpose. Coordinate with the team responsible for other messages so a person does not receive contradictory requests.

Test the route with fictional records before using it. Walk through an interested reader, a reader with a different requirement and a reader who takes no action. Each path should remain understandable without silently assigning them motives.

Here is a filled routing example for the same fictional handoff sequence. Before every send, evaluate these rules from top to bottom: an explicit stop overrides everything; a relevant human conversation takes precedence over educational branches. The no-action path allows the requested guide and one diagnostic follow-up only. This is a chosen scenario boundary, not a timing benchmark.

| Observed event | Condition checked | Next asset or stop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reader replies “Please stop” or unsubscribes | Stop instruction exists, even if the reader also clicked or requested an asset earlier | Stop this sequence; cancel its queued sends. Do not let another branch restart it. |
| Account owner confirms an active conversation about this handoff problem | No stop instruction; relevant human discussion is underway | Exit the automated sequence. Let the conversation owner coordinate any appropriate asset. |
| Interested reader replies “Send the comparison” after using the diagnostic | No higher-priority exit; explicit request matches the existing comparison | Send the recorded comparison of two completion signals. Do not infer budget or purchase readiness. |
| Reader replies “We need approvals from an external partner” | No higher-priority exit; the current comparison covers internal handoffs only | Hold automated sends and route the requirement for human clarification; do not send an unsupported fit claim. |
| Guide has been delivered; no request or reply is observed at the planned follow-up check | No higher-priority exit; diagnostic follow-up has not been sent | Send the single diagnostic follow-up, then proceed only to the final sequence check. |
| Final sequence check finds no explicit request or reply after that follow-up | No higher-priority exit; the no-action allowance is exhausted | Stop the sequence. Do not recycle introductory assets or escalate to a sales message. |

A click alone selects none of the explicit-request branches. These routes govern the next communication, not a judgment about the reader’s budget, authority or readiness.

## Learn from gaps in understanding

Review the questions people ask after receiving the sequence. A repeated clarification may indicate that an asset needs a better introduction or that the sequence skipped a prerequisite. This is more specific than concluding that “email three performed badly.”

Compare the message promise with the destination. If people click but ask what the video is supposed to demonstrate, improve the wrapper or select clearer evidence. If they understand the material but need a different example, consider a purposeful branch rather than another general follow-up.

Keep observations separate from explanations. Record the actual event, the possible reason and the change you plan to test. Do not attribute revenue to one email merely because a buyer encountered it before a purchase.

Refresh the sequence when the underlying content changes. A rewritten guide, replaced demonstration or changed prerequisite can make an otherwise sensible path misleading. Maintain the relationship between entry topic, next question and current asset so the sequence continues to teach something useful rather than simply deliver messages on schedule.
