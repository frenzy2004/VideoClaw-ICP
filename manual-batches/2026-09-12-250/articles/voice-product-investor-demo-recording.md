Capture a voice-product demo as one traceable interaction from the user's spoken command through the product's response and visible result. Keep founder narration, user speech and generated output distinguishable, and preserve the real pauses between them. Use an utterance-to-response capture sheet so editing cannot accidentally imply a different command, faster response or unsupported autonomous behavior.

## Define the interaction the recording must show

Choose one task the product can actually perform in the demonstrated state. Write the starting conditions, intended user command, expected type of response and visible evidence of completion. Keep the scenario small enough that an investor can follow the causal chain.

A fictional scheduling assistant might receive “Move my planning meeting to Friday,” ask which meeting the user means and then update the selected event. The clarification is part of the behavior, not an inconvenience to remove merely because a shorter interaction looks more impressive.

Identify whose voice appears at each point. The founder may introduce the task, a user may speak the command, and the product may generate a response. If one person performs both narration and user speech, use clear transitions or labels so the audience knows when the product is being addressed.

State relevant prototype or operator conditions. If a person must confirm the proposed change behind the scenes, include that action in the capture plan. A real result can still depend on manual work.

## Capture the input and output as distinct sources

Choose a recording route that captures the user's speech, the product's actual output and the visible state needed to interpret both. Make a short test and inspect the saved files before recording the full example.

[Zoom documents computer audio as a separate sharing path](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0063608). If a remote session is part of your capture setup, do not assume that a working microphone also carries the product's generated sound. Verify what reaches the recipient and what the chosen recording method actually saves.

Avoid relying on accidental speaker-to-microphone pickup when a suitable direct route is available. If the physical acoustic interaction itself matters, record that condition honestly and keep equipment placement consistent. A digital product feed and a room recording represent different listening perspectives.

Keep sources separate where practical, with a run ID connecting them. That gives the editor a way to distinguish captured product audio from added narration. Do not label an explanatory voiceover as the product's real response.

## Fill an utterance-to-response capture sheet

The following fictional sheet plans one scheduling interaction, RUN-03. Event positions are descriptive rather than measured latency claims.

| Event | Speaker or source | Recorded content | Visible state | What must remain intact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Orientation | Founder narration | Explains that the user will move one meeting | Starting calendar with fictional entries | Clearly separate introduction from command |
| First command | User microphone | “Move my planning meeting to Friday” | Product listening or input state as actually shown | Complete utterance, including any correction |
| Clarification | Product output | Asks which planning meeting | Actual clarification prompt | Response onset and full question |
| User answer | User microphone | Identifies the intended fictional meeting | Selected context or actual input display | Answer belongs to the same run |
| Completion | Product output and screen | Confirms the recorded change | Updated meeting in the calendar | Sound and visible result correspond |
| Explanation | Founder narration | Describes what the example establishes | Final result remains inspectable | No claim that one run proves general reliability |

Add actual source filenames and time positions after recording. The plan states what to capture; the completed sheet records what happened. If the product gives a different answer, do not leave the planned wording in the evidence record as though it was observed.

For RUN-03, retain the clarification exchange. Removing it would change the apparent behavior from a multi-turn interaction into successful interpretation of an ambiguous command.

## Synchronize the same run and inspect alignment

Start all recording sources before the interaction and keep them running through the result. Establish a shared marker or another genuine event that appears in the relevant sources. A filename or device clock alone does not prove that two recordings align.

[Apple's synchronization guidance](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/guide/final-cut-pro/verc1fabc30/mac) describes aligning same-take material using audio, timecode or markers. Whatever method you use, inspect the actual command and response against the visible product state after alignment.

Check that the user's final word is not cut off and that the product's response begins where it actually began. A small edit around silence can accidentally turn overlap into a clean turn or make a delayed response appear immediate.

Preserve the original sources and the synchronization note. If one source stops or drops out, mark that run and decide whether a new capture is needed. Do not silently combine a clear command from one attempt with a successful response from another.

## Preserve pauses, interruptions and uncertainty

A voice interaction includes timing and turn-taking, not just words. Keep meaningful pauses, clarification requests, interruptions and corrections visible or audible when they affect what the investor can infer.

If the user interrupts the product, capture what the product actually does. Does it stop, continue, ask again or fail to respond? Do not edit the exchange into a capability demonstration that the source does not support.

If you shorten a long wait for presentation, make the omission explicit and preserve the uncut reference. Do not infer or advertise a response-time metric from loosely synchronized recordings. A performance measurement requires an appropriate method beyond an editorial timeline.

Disclose substituted audio or reconstructed scenes at the point needed to understand them. A clean rerecorded explanation can be useful, but it must not masquerade as the generated response from the captured interaction. When reconstruction would make the evidence ambiguous, use a new complete take instead.

## Test whether an unfamiliar viewer can follow the voices

Play the export to someone who did not attend the recording. Ask who issued the command, what the product asked, what the user clarified and what result appeared. Their account should match the actual interaction, not the story you intended to convey.

Then ask which voice was the founder's explanation and which was generated by the product. If the distinction is unclear, improve labels, spacing or the introduction without altering the underlying response. A more polished mix is not enough if source identity remains ambiguous.

Check captions against speaker identity and the actual output. A caption that silently corrects a product error can misrepresent the demo even when the audio remains unchanged. Keep explanatory notes distinct from a transcription of what was said.

The finished work product is one coherent interaction, its source sheet and an unedited reference segment. It lets investors inspect command, interpretation, response and result under the stated conditions while preserving the limits of what a single recorded example can establish.
