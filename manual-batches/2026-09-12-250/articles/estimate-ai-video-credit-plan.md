Estimate AI video credits from the shots you need, the selected model's charges, and a small authorized trial's usable output. Finished-video length alone is insufficient. Count rejected attempts and any separately metered steps, then map the estimate to billing dates and expiry rules before choosing a plan.

## Start with the shots that require generation

Separate the finished video into material already available, material that needs conventional production, and shots you intend to generate. Only the last group belongs in the initial generation-credit estimate.

Describe each generated shot's job: subject, action, framing, approximate duration, and what would make it unacceptable. A technically completed clip is not necessarily usable if the product changes appearance or the action cannot match the edit.

Specify whether the output needs sound, an extension, a higher-resolution version, or another processing step. Ask which actions consume additional credits instead of assuming that one initial charge covers the complete asset.

Keep rights, confidentiality, and brand approval as separate purchase requirements. A low credit estimate does not establish that the service is appropriate for sensitive inputs or a particular commercial use.

## Record model-specific charges before testing

Copy the current charge displayed for the exact model and settings into your worksheet, together with the inspection date. Check whether the unit is per second, per generation, or another basis.

[Runway's Max plan documentation](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/52067870667411-Max-plan-details) explains that different models consume credits differently. That supports a model-specific ledger, not treating every credit allowance as an interchangeable quantity of finished footage.

If the interface shows an estimate before submitting a request, capture it. Afterward, compare it with the actual balance change or usage record where available. Ask how failed requests, cancelled jobs, extensions, and optional processing are charged.

Do not test paid generation without spending authorization. Existing authorized trial records may be enough for an initial estimate, provided their model, settings, and acceptance criteria match the proposed work.

## Build an attempt-to-accepted-footage ledger

Use one row per attempt, including rejected outputs. Keep a link or reference to the output so another reviewer can understand the acceptance decision.

| Field | What to record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shot and model | Brief reference, model and version where shown | Prevents mixing unlike production tasks |
| Settings | Requested duration and charge-relevant options | Makes the quoted charge reproducible |
| Credit debit | Actual consumed credits or unresolved discrepancy | Captures expenditure rather than allowance |
| Result | Accepted, rejected, or awaiting review | Avoids counting unfinished review as success |
| Usable duration | Seconds actually available for the intended edit | Separates generated length from utility |
| Rework reason | Specific mismatch or requested change | Shows which assumptions need another trial |

Preserve the total generated duration separately. It can explain the charge, but it is not the denominator for estimating usable production output.

Record when an accepted clip serves several uses without requiring another generation. Conversely, do not count the same accepted seconds several times simply because they appear in multiple exports.

If nothing is accepted, report consumed credits and “no accepted output.” Dividing by zero cannot produce a meaningful credits-per-accepted-second estimate. Investigate the failure before purchasing a larger allowance.

## Work through a clearly fictional estimate

Every number in this example is invented to demonstrate the calculation. It is not Runway pricing, a vendor benchmark, a measured acceptance rate, or a prediction for your project.

Suppose a fictional model charges four credits per requested second. Each five-second attempt therefore consumes twenty credits. Six attempts use 120 credits.

Imagine that only two clips are accepted and each contributes four usable seconds after trimming. The trial yields eight accepted seconds. Its observed-within-the-fiction ratio is 120 divided by eight, or fifteen credits per accepted second.

At the same fictional yield, forty accepted seconds would require 600 credits. This is a scenario, not a recommendation to buy that amount: the next shots could be substantially easier or harder.

Keep a second scenario that changes a stated assumption. If the same six attempts yielded twelve accepted seconds, the ratio would be ten credits per accepted second and forty seconds would imply 400 credits. The difference shows why counting attempts matters.

Do not add a universal rejection percentage. Use your own authorized trial observations, explain the sample's limits, and keep unlike models or shot types in separate rows.

## Apply the billing calendar and credit rules

Allowances and demand must meet in the same billing period. Record renewal dates, expected production dates, expiry, rollover, and any separate purchased-credit balance.

[Runway's credit rollover guidance](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/23987639973267-Will-unused-credits-carry-over-after-my-billing-day) distinguishes monthly allowances from purchased credits, describes limited Max rollover, and states that monthly credits are used first. Verify the current rule for your selected plan; do not generalize it to other providers.

For a separate fictional calendar example, assume a plan supplies 400 monthly credits with no rollover. Using 350 leaves fifty unused at reset. If the next period requires 600, its 400-credit allowance leaves a 200-credit gap.

Do not treat the expired fifty as available or assume the gap can be purchased at a particular price. Ask about the actual top-up options, minimum purchases, and timing.

Map delayed approvals too. Work planned near renewal may slip into another period, changing whether an allowance is sufficient even when total production demand stays unchanged.

## Choose a plan with a spending stop

Compare candidate plans using the same shot list and trial evidence. Include the quoted subscription cost, necessary credit purchases, relevant seats, and any required processing that the estimate excluded.

Keep credit sufficiency separate from service suitability. Check permitted models, account access, security requirements, and export needs. A plan with enough credits can still fail the production brief.

Write a short purchase recommendation: proposed allowance, evidence behind it, unresolved assumptions, authorized spending limit, and the point at which the estimate will be reviewed.

Set a stop condition for repeated unusable output. The next decision may be to revise the shot, try another approved model, or commission conventional production—not automatically buy more credits.

After the first delivery, compare estimated and actual consumption using the same acceptance standard. Preserve that record for the next buying decision rather than turning one trial's yield into a permanent productivity claim.
