When a portfolio company's product changes, audit the facts that changed and trace them into existing media before deciding what to update. Separate current guidance from historical material, then choose replacement, annotation or retirement for each affected asset. A product change should trigger a focused dependency review, not an indiscriminate rewrite of the company's entire media history.

## Describe the change precisely

Ask the company what is different, when the change became effective and which public wording is approved. A new product name, a changed workflow and a narrower customer audience create different maintenance tasks.

Record the old and new facts side by side. “Updated positioning” is too vague to search reliably. “The product no longer supports the workflow shown in the second screen sequence” gives the editor something concrete to locate.

Do not assume that every change invalidates all earlier media. A founder's historical account of an early customer may remain accurate. A tutorial presented as current may become misleading. The audit should distinguish those contexts.

Keep unresolved questions visible. If the company has not approved a replacement description, you can identify affected assets and prepare decisions without inventing the new message.

Start with one confirmed change and a bounded set of owned destinations. Expand when the evidence shows more dependencies, rather than treating the audit as a general campaign redesign.

## Trace facts into assets and destinations

Search the company record, asset descriptions, transcripts, captions, thumbnails and page copy for the changed name or claim. Include old spellings and abbreviations that people may have used.

[Canto's metadata guide](https://www.canto.com/glossary/metadata-management/) identifies relationships between assets as a kind of metadata. Those relationships are useful here: a source recording, an excerpt and an article can all depend on the same factual passage.

Draw a small dependency graph in your working notes. Put the changed fact at the center, connect it to the source asset and then to each derivative and destination. The graph helps distinguish one underlying correction from several publication tasks.

For example, an outdated product name may appear in a video opening, its captions, its thumbnail and a newsletter link. Replacing only the website description leaves the rest of the chain unresolved.

Record where you searched and what remains outside the current scope. A statement about the platform's checked library should not imply that every company-owned channel has also been audited.

## Classify the effect on the reader

For each affected item, ask what a reader would believe or try to do. An old logo may create mild confusion. An obsolete setup instruction may prevent the reader completing a task. A changed claim may require the company's immediate attention.

Use a small set of editorial actions. Update means the current asset can be corrected. Replace means a different asset is needed. Annotate means the historical material remains useful with context. Retire means it should leave active discovery.

[Atlassian's knowledge-base guidance](https://www.atlassian.com/itsm/knowledge-management/self-service-success) recommends reviewing content that becomes stale or irrelevant. In a portfolio media audit, the important addition is to evaluate the specific company's change and the asset's current purpose.

Avoid a blanket “old equals bad” rule. Age is a clue, not a verdict. A three-year-old founder lesson can remain useful, while a recently published screen recording can become stale after a product change.

Prioritize actions by the consequence of the misunderstanding and the exposure you actually know, not an invented traffic estimate.

## Use a fictional change-impact worksheet

Suppose Beacon replaces its “team dashboard” with a different workflow and supplies an approved explanation. The following worksheet is an original example.

| Dependent item | What became stale | Reader consequence | Proposed action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Current directory profile | Old product description | Visitor misunderstands today's product | Update approved copy |
| Setup tutorial | Removed dashboard step | Viewer cannot follow the instruction | Replace or withdraw from current guidance |
| Founder history interview | Discussion of the earlier product | Accurate historical account | Add date context if needed |
| Tutorial captions | Old step names | Text repeats obsolete instructions | Update with the replacement recording |
| Newsletter archive | Original launch description | Historical announcement | Preserve with context rather than silently rewriting |
| Current resource card | Link to the obsolete tutorial | Sends users to unusable guidance | Redirect to a current resource or show unavailable |

The worksheet separates factual impact from the production work required. Replacing the tutorial may be a larger task, but the resource card can be corrected sooner so new users are not directed to a known problem.

The company should confirm the replacement explanation. The platform can propose editorial actions and sequence the work, but it should not infer the new product behavior.

## Sequence corrections by dependency

Correct the source of the current claim before creating new derivatives. If the approved description is still changing, avoid updating every caption and thumbnail independently. You may create repeated work and inconsistent versions.

At the same time, do not wait for a complete replacement package before addressing a misleading current link. A temporary notice or removal from active recommendations may be appropriate under the company's instructions.

Assign one correction record to each underlying change and connect destination tasks to it. That lets the coordinator see which publications are waiting on the same new recording or approved wording.

Check companions when a source changes. A revised video can still be paired with an outdated transcript. A new thumbnail can be attached to the old embed. Verify the package in its destination, not just the files in the editing folder.

Record the version and date used for the correction. Future audits need to distinguish the obsolete state from the replacement rather than repeating the same investigation.

## Close the audit without erasing history

Ask the company to review the current-facing changes and confirm that the intended explanation is now accurate. Check that visitors can reach a useful next step from updated resource cards and links.

Report historical items separately. Explain which were preserved, annotated or removed from active discovery and why. Historical context should remain honest; do not edit an old quotation to make it sound like the founder predicted the new positioning.

List unresolved destinations and their owners. If a partner page still uses the old tutorial, identify the request sent and the confirmation pending. Avoid presenting a platform-only correction as a complete company-wide audit.

Finally, add the change trigger to the maintenance process. Ask companies to tell the platform when names, key workflows or public descriptions change, and make it easy to identify affected records.

The output of the audit is a set of specific decisions backed by a visible dependency chain. That makes future changes manageable without turning every product update into a full media restart.
