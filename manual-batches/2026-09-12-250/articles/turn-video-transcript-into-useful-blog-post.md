Turn a video transcript into a blog post by choosing one reader question, rebuilding the explanation around that question, and checking every factual statement against the recording. Preserve the speaker’s meaning, supply missing visual context, and write transitions for readers who never watched the original session.

## Choose an article-sized question

A recording has a running order; an article needs a reason to keep reading. Write the reader’s question before opening an editor. “What did our webinar cover?” describes your source. “How can a manager make a handoff clear when two teams share responsibility?” describes a problem someone could bring to the article.

Underline the passages that answer that problem. Put interesting tangents in a separate parking list. They may support another asset, but forcing them into this one makes the argument harder to follow. If the selected passages contain only opinions, narrow the promise to an explanation of those opinions. Do not promise a practical method until the source supplies one or you develop and clearly present your own.

Wistia’s [transcript repurposing example](https://wistia.com/blog/ai-webinar-transcript-content) recommends finding useful ideas with timestamps and verifying the resulting draft. Use the timestamps as working references. They should let an editor return to the exact discussion without searching the entire recording whenever a sentence needs checking.

## Rebuild the explanation before polishing sentences

Create an outline from the selected ideas, then temporarily hide the transcript. Arrange the article in the order a reader needs: recognize the problem, understand why it occurs, make a decision, try a procedure, and check the result. The original speaker may have reached these points in a completely different sequence.

For each section, identify what the reader must already know. A passage about exceptions may need a definition that the speaker gave much later. Move the definition, but avoid combining remarks so that an example becomes a universal recommendation. Words such as “sometimes,” “for this customer,” and “when the data is available” often carry essential boundaries.

Write transitions yourself. A transition explains why the next point follows; it should not claim that the speaker explicitly made that connection. When the connection remains speculative, frame it as your editorial interpretation or leave the two ideas separate. An elegant transition is not worth changing the argument. The finished structure should stand without references to the event’s agenda.

## Use an argument reconstruction sheet

The following worksheet is an original method. The example describes a fictional operations interview, not a customer result.

| Proposed section | Source material | Missing bridge | Editorial action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recognize an unclear handoff | Speaker describes duplicate work | Why duplication happened | Explain the shared responsibility before discussing the symptom |
| Choose a completion signal | Demonstration shows a status field | What the field means | Define the status using the visible example |
| Handle an exception | Speaker discusses an absent reviewer | Whether the workaround is routine | Preserve the condition and label it an exception |
| Check the next handoff | No direct passage | A usable reader exercise | Add an explicitly original exercise rather than attribute it to the guest |

Fill the sheet before writing polished paragraphs. An empty source cell is useful: it tells you that the article needs another source, a narrower claim, or an original example. It does not mean the missing information should be guessed.

The exercise also reveals redundancy. If two sections rely on the same passage and reach the same conclusion, combine them unless they help the reader make different decisions.

## Translate speech without manufacturing quotations

Spoken language contains repetitions, unfinished thoughts, gestures and pronouns that make sense in the room. Written language usually needs explicit subjects and cleaner relationships. In a fictional transcript, a guest says, “We put it there, and then they knew.” A useful paraphrase might be: “The team placed the handoff status beside the task, making the expected next action visible.” That paraphrase is acceptable only if the surrounding audio and screen support those details.

Keep direct quotations short and exact. Do not silently improve a quotation into a sentence the guest never said. If you need substantial editing, paraphrase and retain attribution. Avoid attributing your own worksheet to the speaker simply because their discussion inspired it.

A transcript can also omit information carried by pictures. [W3C’s transcript guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/transcripts/) distinguishes spoken content from visual information needed for understanding. Review the recording when an explanation depends on a chart, screen interaction or physical demonstration. Describe the necessary visual fact, or choose a different passage when that fact cannot be established.

## Add an example that teaches the decision

A transcript summary tells readers what someone discussed. A useful article lets them do something with the idea. Develop a small example around the article’s question, with enough detail to expose a decision.

For the fictional handoff article, describe a task that moves from a specialist to an account manager. The specialist considers the work finished after uploading a file; the account manager considers it unfinished until a customer question is answered. Ask the reader to choose a completion signal that both people can observe. Then explain why “file uploaded” and “customer question answered” describe different states.

This example adds value without inventing a success statistic. It should be labeled fictional, and its solution should be presented as a worked recommendation rather than a reported customer practice. Use names, numbers and interface details only when they clarify the reasoning.

Finish the example with a transfer question: “Which two people define completion differently in your current process?” That helps the reader apply the method without assuming every company has the same organizational structure.

## Check the finished article against the recording

Read the draft once without the source. Mark every place that assumes knowledge of the webinar, introduces an unexplained term, or refers vaguely to “this approach.” Then make a second pass with the recording available. Check names, product behavior, qualifications and any sentences that sound more certain than the speaker.

Separate factual checking from prose editing. Combining them encourages a smooth sentence to survive because it reads well. A brief claim list is enough: draft statement, supporting timestamp, and keep, qualify or remove. Check the article’s title too; it may promise more than the body establishes.

Finally, give the draft to a colleague who did not attend. Ask them to explain the problem, describe the proposed method and name one situation where it may not apply. If they cannot, improve the explanation before adding more words.

Keep the transcript and reconstruction sheet with the working draft. They make later corrections easier and help the next editor distinguish the guest’s contribution from your original synthesis. The published article can then remain focused on the reader’s task.
