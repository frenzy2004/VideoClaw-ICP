Turn recorded Q&A into a buyer FAQ by extracting the real questions, separating answers that depend on different conditions, and checking each response against its full exchange. Merge only questions that share an answer. Keep unresolved points visible in your working notes and write concise responses that stand without the event.

## Collect questions before trying to answer them

Start with the recording and any available chat or question export. Record the question as asked, the speaker or moderator who answered it, and the relevant timestamp. Preserve the original wording in your working sheet even if the published version will be shorter.

Look beyond sentences that end in question marks. A participant may describe a concern—“Our reviewer is often unavailable”—and receive an answer that belongs in the FAQ. Write down the concern without converting it into a stronger claim about what all buyers need.

Keep an unanswered question in the collection. Its presence tells you what the session did not resolve. You can ask a subject expert for a separate answer later, but do not let an automated summary fill the gap as though the response appeared in the recording.

[Wistia’s transcript workflow](https://wistia.com/blog/ai-webinar-transcript-content) supports using timestamped source ideas and checking the resulting copy. For a FAQ, the useful unit is usually an entire question-and-answer exchange, including the qualification at its end.

## Group by the answer the buyer needs

Normalize questions only after collecting them. Two people may use different language to ask the same thing, while two almost identical questions may require different answers. “Can I share a report?” might concern an external recipient, another employee or an anonymous public link. The verb is the same; the conditions are not.

Create a short label for each question’s job. Examples include understanding a prerequisite, deciding between two options, recognizing a limitation or finding a next step. Compare the required answer before merging rows.

Use the buyer’s terminology in the visible question. [Intercom’s help-writing guidance](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/56645-how-to-write-great-help-articles) emphasizes customer goals and language. Apply that principle without inventing search behavior. The wording should be recognizable because it represents the problem, not because you assume a particular phrase has measurable demand.

Keep a narrow question narrow. A specific answer about one workflow should not become “How does your platform handle every review?” simply because the broader version sounds more substantial.

## Build a question-resolution table

This original worksheet uses a fictional expert session about team reviews. It shows how superficially similar questions can produce different editorial decisions.

| Source question | Normalized question | Answer status | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Can a teammate view the draft? | Who can view a draft? | Answered for internal teammates | Keep the audience condition |
| Can a customer view the draft? | Can external reviewers view a draft? | Not answered | Request a separate verified response |
| What if our reviewer is away? | How do we handle an absent reviewer? | A conditional workaround is described | Preserve the condition |
| Can someone else finish it? | Who can complete the review? | Refers to the same absence scenario | Merge only the overlapping portion |

Add an answer timestamp, necessary qualification and final wording column. Use a visible working status such as answered, partial or unresolved.

The table prevents a common failure: one broad answer absorbing several questions whose differences matter. It also gives the subject expert a focused review task. They can confirm the disputed condition instead of rereading a long transcript or reviewing a polished page without knowing where its answers came from.

## Write the short answer first

Begin each answer with the part the reader needs most. If the answer is conditional, state the condition early. Then add only the explanation necessary to use the answer correctly. A short paragraph can often contain the answer, its main limit and a relevant next step.

Do not force a yes-or-no opening when the source does not support one. “The session describes internal sharing; external access needs a separate check” is more useful than an unsupported “Yes, you can share it.” If the question remains unresolved, omit it from the public FAQ until it can be answered accurately or link to an appropriate route for clarification.

Avoid reproducing conversational filler. Replace vague pronouns with explicit nouns, but verify the noun you insert. Keep a distinction between the speaker’s answer and any procedure you add as an editor.

Use links selectively. A link should deepen or operationalize the answer, not hide the answer elsewhere. Readers should understand the essential response before deciding whether they need the supporting guide or full recording.

## Test whether the answer survives without the session

Read each question and answer alone, separated from the rest of the page. Mark references such as “as we showed earlier,” “that option” or “the second approach.” Replace them with enough context to make the answer independent.

Then inspect the surrounding recording. Listen before and after the selected exchange. A late correction, a moderator’s clarification or another speaker’s objection may materially change the response. If the source includes disagreement, describe the different conditions or perspectives instead of compressing them into artificial consensus.

Test the fictional absent-reviewer answer with two cases: a routine review and an urgent exception. If the source supports only the routine case, keep that limit explicit. Do not extend the workaround to emergencies because the question appears similar.

Ask a colleague to identify what action the answer permits and what remains uncertain. Their interpretation should match the intended scope. When it does not, revise the answer rather than adding a general disclaimer at the bottom of the page.

## Publish a useful collection, not a transcript dump

Arrange the questions in the order a buyer would encounter them. Start with prerequisites and basic distinctions, move to practical use, then address limits or exceptional cases. The event’s question order may reflect who raised a hand first rather than a useful reading sequence.

Remove questions that repeat the page’s main explanation without adding a decision. Keep questions that resolve a real ambiguity. A compact set of specific answers is easier to navigate than a long collection padded with generic definitions.

Maintain a source reference privately for each answer. When product behavior changes or a speaker corrects a statement, you can identify the affected response quickly. That reference need not clutter the reader’s experience.

Before publishing, compare the questions with related pages. If another guide already answers one fully, provide a concise answer and a relevant link rather than create a conflicting version. The finished FAQ should give buyers a dependable route through the uncertainty the original conversation revealed, while remaining faithful to what has actually been established.
