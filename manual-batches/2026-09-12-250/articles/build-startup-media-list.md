Build a startup media list by starting with the people affected by your news, finding reporting about their problems, and checking the journalists behind those articles. Keep only entries whose relevance you can explain with evidence. Record current coverage, public contact preferences, and a specific reason each reporter belongs on the shortlist.

## Define the reader before searching for reporters

A media list should answer where a useful company development fits into an existing editorial conversation. Begin with the development you might discuss and the people whose work it affects.

Write a reader description that includes a responsibility and a situation. “Operations leaders coordinating repair contractors across several buildings” is more useful than “business decision-makers.” The narrower description creates a practical search boundary without assuming that only one publication could care.

Then write the reader’s question. Perhaps they need to understand why maintenance instructions get lost between internal teams and external contractors. A reporter might approach that question through workplace technology, property operations, procurement, or labor practices.

Keep those possible angles separate. You are looking for evidence of interest, not collecting everyone who has ever used the word startup.

For a newly funded founder, this step also prevents the funding label from dominating every search. The company’s financing is one fact; the reporter’s audience may care primarily about the underlying work.

## Find articles that reveal an editorial interest

Search for the customer problem, an established alternative, or a recent industry change. Read articles rather than relying on headlines or search previews.

In [YC’s guidance on finding journalists](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/a-guide-to-pitching-funding-and-launch-stories/), the relevant test is what a journalist has actually covered. Apply that principle to your current subject rather than copying a historical list of outlets.

Notice the article’s center of gravity. Does it explain a practical workflow, investigate a claim, describe a funding event, or evaluate a market trend? The presence of your industry’s name does not establish that the reporter is interested in your proposed story type.

Save the URL and date alongside a short note about the article’s question. “Discusses access boundaries between contractors and facilities teams” is a useful note. “Good publication” is not.

Read enough of the piece to understand why your expertise might help. If the only connection is that a competitor appears in a sentence, keep searching.

## Evaluate the person behind the byline

Open the journalist’s current author page and review recent work. Check whether the byline is a staff reporter, an occasional contributor, a sponsored-content author, or a quoted expert. These roles imply different routes for a company story.

Confirm that the person still writes for the outlet. An old article can be an excellent starting point but a poor current contact record. Use published professional information and any stated pitching preferences.

Look for recurring questions rather than trying to infer personal beliefs. A journalist who investigates claims critically may be highly relevant if your evidence is strong. Favorable coverage of another startup is not a requirement for inclusion.

[Qwoted’s pitching guidance](https://www.qwoted.com/best-practices-for-pitching-journalists/) emphasizes connecting your expertise to the reporter’s work. Make that connection in the list itself so it survives the move from research to outreach.

Do not guess private addresses or collect unrelated personal information. A public contact page, a professional profile, or an introduction route is enough to record while you decide whether the person fits.

## Use a worksheet that explains every entry

The worksheet below is an original research structure. Fill it with actual reporting evidence when working on your company.

| Field | What to record | Why it changes the decision |
|---|---|---|
| Journalist and outlet | Current byline and publication | Identifies the intended recipient |
| Relevant article | Exact URL and date | Makes the fit inspectable |
| Editorial question | What the article tries to explain | Reveals the kind of help needed |
| Reader overlap | Who would care about your development | Connects the story to an audience |
| Contribution you can make | Fact, experience, artifact, or access | Establishes a useful reason to contact |
| Contact preference | Public route or known introduction | Respects how the journalist works |
| Remaining uncertainty | Beat change, weak evidence, or unclear timing | Stops a tentative entry becoming assumed fact |

Avoid a complicated numerical score unless the criteria genuinely help. A carefully written explanation often makes a better decision than assigning arbitrary points to publication size.

For each entry, finish the sentence: “This person belongs because…” If the sentence could apply unchanged to everyone on the list, the evidence is too weak.

## Work through a fictional shortlist

Suppose fictional startup Fieldnote wants to discuss cross-company repair coordination. Its founder identifies three fictional journalists.

Mara Chen writes about property operations and recently examined incomplete contractor instructions. The founder can explain a specific access-design tradeoff relevant to that article. This is a strong subject connection, subject to checking current contact preferences.

Leo Grant covers public technology-company earnings. One recent piece mentioned facilities software, but the focus was financial performance. Fieldnote’s early workflow development offers little connection to that reporting task. The founder removes the entry from this shortlist.

Sam Ortiz writes a trade newsletter about field-service workflows. A recent article asks how smaller contractors handle customer portals. The founder has relevant product knowledge but no evidence about the whole market. Sam stays on the list with a narrow contribution: one implementation example and its limitations.

These people and articles are invented to demonstrate the decision. A real shortlist must use real bylines and URLs. The exercise shows why familiarity with an industry name is weaker than a specific editorial match.

## Keep the list small enough to maintain

Choose the entries with the clearest combination of reader relevance, contribution, and current activity. Record why you excluded near matches so someone else does not immediately add them back.

Before contacting a shortlisted journalist, reopen the relevant article and check their latest work. Refresh the entry when the story changes, a reporter moves, or a previous reply gives you better information about their interests.

Maintain a separate contact-history field. A courteous decline, a request for more evidence, and silence are different observations. Avoid turning any of them into an unsupported judgment about the reporter.

The list is ready when each entry can support a specific outreach decision. Use the [startup story pitch guide](/blog/pitch-startup-story-to-journalists) to turn one match into a message.
