Merge portfolio media duplicates by proving which files represent the same asset, choosing a surviving record, and preserving useful metadata, access boundaries and live references. Similar thumbnails are not sufficient evidence. Use a duplicate adjudication sheet that distinguishes identical files from meaningful versions, company-specific variants and unrelated assets that merely look alike.

## Establish what a duplicate means

A portfolio library can contain an identical logo uploaded by three people, two exports of the same interview, and a shared event photograph associated with several companies. These situations require different decisions. Duplicate detection identifies candidates; the operator still decides whether consolidation would erase something useful.

[Canto’s duplicate-check documentation](https://support.canto.com/hc/en-us/articles/23001624894993-Duplicate-Check-Options) distinguishes filename matching from an MD5-based comparison and explains that metadata changes can affect hash matching. Treat either signal as evidence about file identity, not a complete answer about editorial identity. Two identical files can still have different access histories or approval references.

Define a duplicate as an unnecessary competing record for the same intended use. A captioned export and a clean master are not duplicates merely because they show the same interview. A founder portrait cropped for a directory may remain a purposeful derivative. Write this definition at the top of the cleanup sheet so reviewers use the same test.

## Compare content and context before choosing

Gather candidate records without changing them. Capture their record IDs, filenames, company associations, file characteristics, approval references, visibility, download links and current uses. Mark missing information as unknown. Do not infer approval from the folder name or infer permission from the fact that somebody uploaded the file.

Compare the visible or audible content as well as the technical signals. Watch the opening and closing of videos, inspect captions, and compare a meaningful point in the middle. A corrected job title can be easy to miss in otherwise identical footage. For photographs, inspect crops, embedded credits and descriptive captions rather than relying on thumbnails.

Next ask whether both records are needed to express a real relationship. A single photograph may depict people from several companies, but combining records must not broaden access to a restricted event collection. If the system cannot preserve the required separation, keep separate controlled records and document why. Operational simplicity is not a reason to remove a company boundary.

## Use a duplicate adjudication sheet

Consider a fictional portfolio operator reviewing four groups of files from a founder showcase. The work product records a decision for each group, not a blanket instruction to keep the newest upload.

| Candidate group | Important difference | Proposed decision | Evidence to retain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Two identical Alder logos | One record has the current company approval link | Keep the approved reference record | Approval and affected page links |
| Two Beacon interview exports | One corrects the speaker’s title | Keep both as related versions; current export is active | Correction note and version relationship |
| Three copies of a group photograph | Captions name different participants | Consolidate only after checking the combined caption and audience | Reviewed caption and company associations |
| Two matching workshop slides | One copy belongs to a restricted session | Keep separate controlled records | Access reason and session context |

Add columns for decision owner, planned survivor, affected references, reversible action and verification date. The operator proposes a decision; the relevant company or collection owner resolves uncertain identity or use restrictions. A reviewer should be able to disagree with one row without reopening the entire cleanup.

For the group photograph, the proposed survivor may initially have the poorest caption. That does not mean it should automatically lose. The team can choose the record with stable references and transfer reviewed descriptive information into it, provided the library supports that operation. Record what was transferred and where it came from.

## Preserve metadata without mixing permissions

Combine complementary factual metadata only after checking it. If one portrait record says “chief executive” and another says “founder,” do not join them into a longer title. Ask the company which description is current and retain the historical context separately if needed. Conflicting information is a review task, not a merge opportunity.

Treat approval, attribution and access information as controlled context. Copying a credit field may be appropriate; copying an approval label without its supporting reference is not. A record approved for a private newsletter should not become approved for public promotion because another copy sits in a public collection.

Version history also needs deliberate handling. [Canto documents downloading previous asset versions](https://support.canto.com/hc/en-us/articles/23002260134801-Download-previous-Version-of-an-Asset), which illustrates why a current file and an earlier file can belong to one history rather than competing as unrelated assets. Confirm what your own library preserves before restructuring records. The practical goal is an understandable relationship, not use of a particular vendor feature.

Write a short survivor note: “Record A replaces duplicate record B; B’s reviewed participant names were added; the approved audience remains unchanged.” This creates a traceable editorial explanation without claiming that every metadata field was interchangeable.

## Repair references before retiring copies

Find where candidate records are used. Include directory entries, workshop resource pages, newsletter drafts, company folders and links shared with external collaborators. A file may be redundant inside the library while its URL remains important outside it. Record these dependencies before taking any action.

Prepare replacement links and check them from the intended user’s perspective. A working administrator link is not proof that a founder can open the survivor. Test both permitted and excluded audiences using the organization’s normal access-checking process. Keep a reference unresolved if nobody can establish who relies on it.

Use a recoverable holding stage where available instead of immediately deleting competing copies. Label the retired record with its replacement and reason, while respecting the library’s visibility rules. Set a review point for unresolved references. Do not promise that external downloads or copied links will disappear when the library record changes.

For the Alder logo example, completion means the directory and press folder point to the survivor, the company contact can retrieve it, and the old record clearly points to the replacement or is safely held. Merely reducing the file count does not complete the job.

## Close the cleanup with a checked decision log

Review a small sample across different asset types before expanding the cleanup. Include a true duplicate, a corrected version, a shared photograph and a restricted record. Ask a colleague to reconstruct the decision from the sheet. If the reasoning depends on an undocumented conversation, improve the record before continuing.

The final log should show what survived, what changed, which references were repaired, what remains unresolved and who accepted the outcome. Keep exceptions visible: purposeful duplicate records may be the right answer when ownership or access cannot be represented safely in one record.

Use the exercise to prevent recurrence without turning it into a full library redesign. Add a submission question asking whether an asset already exists, or require a link to the existing record when sending a corrected export. The next cleanup should begin with better evidence, not merely fewer files.
