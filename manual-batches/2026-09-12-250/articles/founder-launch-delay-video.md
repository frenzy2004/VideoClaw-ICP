Explain a launch delay in a founder video by naming the missed expectation, giving a factual reason, and showing what has changed without claiming the product is ready. Separate the next progress update from the release date. Leave viewers with a reliable place to check and a practical choice about waiting.

## Establish exactly which promise changed

Read the original announcement before drafting the recording. Identify the availability date, the audience it covered, and any conditions that accompanied it. A waitlist invitation, a paid order, and an existing customer’s scheduled rollout are different commitments. Do not speak as if one generic message resolves all three.

Write a factual opening that acknowledges the gap: “We said invited teams would receive access on this date. That will not happen.” Avoid replacing the delay with a vague celebration of how carefully the company builds.

The founder should know who approved the original date and what the team can now confirm. This is an internal preparation step, not an invitation to blame an employee on camera.

People whose individual arrangements are affected may need direct communication before or alongside the public recording. Route those cases to the responsible team. Keep private terms and account details out of the video.

## Separate the explanation from the defense

Use one understandable reason for the delay, then explain its consequence for the viewer. “Our validation pipeline encountered edge cases” may describe internal work but leave the customer wondering whether their information would be reliable.

In a fictional example, Fieldnote planned to open an import workflow to invited facilities teams. Testing found that repeated uploads could create duplicate repair assignments. The useful explanation is that the team is correcting duplicate handling before widening access.

Do not add unsupported reassurance such as “nothing could go wrong” or “the issue is completely isolated” unless those claims have been verified and approved. State unresolved questions plainly.

[CDC’s spokesperson guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Spokesperson.pdf) cautions against overreassurance and addresses uncertainty. Its context is public-health emergencies; the narrow communication principle is useful here, but this storyboard is an original startup application, not a crisis protocol.

Avoid turning the explanation into a tour of technical complexity. The viewer needs enough information to understand the decision, not a demonstration that the team has been working hard.

## Build a storyboard around inspectable progress

The following completed storyboard is fictional. All dates, testing conditions, and product behavior illustrate the method rather than describe a real launch.

| Beat | Spoken information | Visual choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Missed expectation | Access promised for October 7 will not open that day | Founder directly addresses the viewer; date appears plainly |
| Reason | Repeated imports can create duplicate assignments in testing | Simple illustration of one intended assignment appearing twice |
| Current progress | The revised test detects a repeated upload in one synthetic example | Current test environment, clearly labeled with synthetic data |
| Remaining work | Other import variations and recovery behavior still need review | Short list of unresolved checks, not a completion percentage |
| Next update | A progress note will appear October 14, whether or not access is ready | Written update location and communication date |
| Viewer option | Invited teams can keep their present process or contact their existing account contact about timing | Founder returns on screen; options remain visible |

The progress shot must be understandable. Narrate what changes on screen and keep labels large enough to read. “This test identifies a repeated upload” is a narrower claim than “the import problem is fixed.”

[Buttondown’s pre-launch guidance](https://buttondown.com/blog/pre-launch-newsletter-guide) recommends honest behind-the-scenes communication while people wait. The artifact-first video structure here is an original adaptation of that general idea, not a prescribed email sequence.

## Record accountability without staging certainty

Keep the founder visible for the opening acknowledgment and final commitment. Use the artifact where it answers a question the face alone cannot answer. A screen full of internal tickets may look active while explaining almost nothing.

Read the factual sentences from an approved version if necessary. Delivery can remain warm without improvising new dates or claims. Pause between the reason, the demonstration, and the remaining uncertainty so those distinctions survive a casual viewing.

A draft passage for the fictional storyboard might read:

> We said invited teams would receive access on October 7. We are delaying that opening because repeated uploads can create duplicate assignments in our testing. The example on screen shows our revised check identifying one repeat upload using synthetic data. That does not establish that every import condition is ready. We will post another progress note on October 14, even if we cannot announce access then.

This passage deliberately limits what the successful test proves. Replace every example detail with approved facts before recording. If a reviewer removes a qualifying sentence, check whether the resulting clip overstates readiness.

For the physical presentation, use [founder talking-head framing](/blog/founder-talking-head-video-framing). This message needs clarity and audibility more than a dramatic set, music, or a montage of late-night work.

## Make the next communication a promise you can keep

Assign an owner to the promised update before the founder says it on camera. Confirm the destination is accessible to the intended audience and that someone will publish a factual status even if little changes.

An update date is not a substitute release date. Say so directly when viewers could confuse them. Avoid “we hope to launch by our next update” if that phrasing would undermine the distinction you just established.

Write a fallback update now: the condition remains unresolved, the team is still working on it, and the next communication will identify what has been verified. A fallback is not pessimism; it prevents silence when the optimistic scenario fails.

Give viewers an option appropriate to their actual relationship with the company. A waitlist member can choose to keep waiting or stop receiving launch notices. An existing customer may need a private planning conversation. Do not invent refund terms, alternative access, or compensatory offers.

For continuing communication after the delay, [public-roadmap expectation setting](/blog/startup-public-roadmap-expectations) can help distinguish planned work from commitments. Keep this video focused on the specific missed launch expectation.

## Review the video from the waiting customer's position

Have a colleague watch without the internal project context. Ask them to state which date changed, why it changed, what the artifact proves, and what will happen next. If they think the product is now available, the recording has failed even if every individual sentence is technically accurate.

Check the title, preview text, on-screen dates, captions, and spoken wording together. “Launch update” may be accurate but vague; “Access is delayed; next progress update October 14” is more explicit in the fictional scenario.

Provide the core information in text alongside the recording so watching is optional. Keep the original context findable and mark later updates clearly. Do not silently replace a missed promise with an apparently uninterrupted success story.
