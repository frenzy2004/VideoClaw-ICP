Invite customers to an advisory board by explaining why their experience matters, what participation requires, and what they will receive in return. Make the first discussion concrete and the commitment optional. A useful invitation starts an ongoing exchange of judgment, not a disguised sales meeting or an unpaid product-testing obligation.

## Define the conversation before naming the board

Write the unresolved question that makes a continuing group worthwhile. “We want customer feedback” is too broad to help someone decide whether to join. “We need to understand how facilities teams divide responsibility when outside contractors enter a repair workflow” gives prospective members something recognizable to discuss.

A board should not exist only because a funded company thinks it needs one. Identify a founder who will attend, a person who will return findings, and enough recurring uncertainty to justify another meeting. If you need one reaction to one screen, ask for that smaller conversation instead.

Peter Kazanjy describes extending customer-development relationships into an ongoing advisory group in [First Round’s founder interview](https://review.firstround.com/start-up-on-the-right-foot-build-a-customer-advisory-board/). The invitation package below is an original planning example, not a reproduction of his program.

Separate advisory participation from governance, investment, and formal product development. Describe the actual role without suggesting members control company decisions. If the arrangement includes contractual obligations, establish those separately before anyone agrees.

## Choose participants for useful differences

Create a participant profile around experience rather than seniority alone. A manager who approves spending may understand different constraints from the person who handles every interrupted repair request. Both may matter; neither automatically represents the other.

[HubSpot’s 2026 advisory board announcement](https://www.hubspot.com/company-news/hubspot-introduces-its-2026-customer-advisory-board) describes a mix of functional and business perspectives. That is a useful example of deliberate variety, not a recommended board size or a model your startup must copy.

For the fictional company Fieldnote, the recruiting sheet contains these three perspectives:

- A facilities coordinator who assigns work across several buildings and sees handoff failures directly.
- An operations manager who decides which information outside contractors can receive.
- A customer administrator who has questioned whether the current workflow creates extra work.

The third person is not a consolation choice. Their criticism may reveal a condition the enthusiastic users overlook. Invite them for that experience without describing them as a difficult customer.

Check whether invitees can discuss examples without exposing employer information. Offer anonymized discussion and a way to pass on a question. Do not ask one member to speak for an entire industry.

## Fill the invitation package before recording anything

Here is a completed fictional package. All names, activities, and timings are illustrative choices, not researched demand or program benchmarks.

**Purpose:** Understand how facilities teams balance accountability and limited contractor access.

**Initial term:** Three months, followed by an explicit decision about continuing.

**Request:** One forty-five-minute online discussion each month, with an optional ten-minute scenario review beforehand. No required feature testing, referrals, or marketing appearances.

**Member value:** Compare approaches with peers, explain constraints directly to the founder, and receive a short account of how the team interpreted the advice.

**First question:** When a repair changes hands, what information must move with it, and what should remain internal?

**Participation conditions:** Members can decline a topic or leave the group. No recording by default. Any recording, attribution, or external quotation needs separate agreement.

These fields reveal whether the program is credible before the founder makes a warm invitation. If you cannot deliver the return note or protect discussion boundaries, reduce the promise.

Compensation, expenses, and travel should be explicit if they apply. Check the participant’s employer requirements instead of assuming a gift is welcome. Do not present access to the founder as payment for unlimited availability.

## Make the invitation personal and easy to decline

A short founder video can introduce the reason for the invitation, while a written note carries the exact commitment. Do not make the recipient watch a recording to discover the meeting schedule or obligations.

In this fictional example, coordinator Maya previously explained how repair assignments lose their location context. The founder could say:

> Maya, your explanation of what happens when a repair changes hands helped us see a gap in our thinking. I’m inviting a small customer group to examine that problem with us over three monthly conversations. I’d value your operational perspective, especially when our assumptions do not match the work. I’ve included the time request and first agenda below. It is completely fine if the timing does not work.

This acknowledges an actual contribution established in the example. It does not claim Maya endorsed the product or promise to build her preferred feature.

Read the message aloud once, checking the person’s name and the recalled detail. Keep the delivery conversational; a polished company-history introduction adds little here. For preparation, use [founder camera-confidence practice](/blog/founder-camera-confidence-practice).

End with a small next step: whether they would like the full details or a brief discussion. Do not enroll them, announce their membership, or add recurring calendar events before they accept.

## Give the first meeting a real job

Use the opening meeting to test whether the invitation’s promise holds. Fieldnote’s fictional forty-five-minute agenda is deliberately narrow:

- Five minutes: restate purpose, participation boundaries, and the question.
- Ten minutes: members describe one handoff from their own work, without customer-identifying details.
- Fifteen minutes: compare what must travel with an assignment and what should not.
- Ten minutes: examine a neutral scenario and identify missing information.
- Five minutes: summarize disagreements and explain the return note.

The scenario could involve a contractor receiving “check the leak” without a building location or an internal contact. Ask which missing detail prevents action first. Do not show a preferred product solution immediately; that would turn the conversation toward evaluating your answer.

Let members disagree. Record the condition behind each position: a small site may route every problem through one coordinator, while a distributed team needs a named local contact. Combining both into “customers want more fields” would erase the useful distinction.

## Return the advice without pretending everything became a feature

Acknowledge advice in a note with three categories: what the team understood, what it will examine, and what it is not committing to do.

For Fieldnote, the completed return note reads:

> We heard that a repair location and a responsible contact solve different handoff problems. We will compare how those details appear in our current workflow before proposing changes. We are not committing to a new permission model from this discussion. At the next meeting, we will show the alternatives we considered and ask where our interpretation is wrong.

Send the note through the agreed channel and invite corrections to the summary. Credit individual contributions only with permission. If the founder cannot follow through, acknowledge that before asking for another hour.
