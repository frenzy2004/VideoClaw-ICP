Explain product limits by connecting each constraint to a customer task: what works, under which conditions, what does not, and what a customer should do instead. Use verified capability language and a practical fit-boundary table. Keep future plans separate so prospects can judge the product they can use today rather than the one you hope to build.

## Begin with the task a customer is evaluating

A limitation is useful information when the reader understands its effect on their work. “Limited permissions” is vague. “Outside collaborators can view the assigned task but cannot approve workspace changes” describes a boundary, if that is the product's actual behavior.

Start from the customer's question rather than your feature list. What are they trying to complete? What conditions matter? What would make the product unsuitable? Ask the responsible product or support owner to verify the answers.

[PostHog's founder argues for concrete product details that help technical readers evaluate fit](https://newsletter.posthog.com/p/why-we-sell-features-not-benefits). That is a practitioner viewpoint, not a universal claim that benefits language is useless. Here, specificity matters because the customer is testing a dependency.

Do not hide the constraint behind a broad benefit such as “collaborate without limits.” If an important limit exists, that phrase may create the wrong expectation before the customer reaches the details.

Write one plain task sentence for every capability you plan to discuss publicly.

## Build a fit-boundary table from checked facts

Use five columns: customer task, supported conditions, known limit, possible alternative, and who should wait. Add a private owner and verification date to each row.

Here is an original fictional example for a review application called Reviewlane. The task is collecting feedback from an external reviewer. The supported condition is access to a specifically shared review item. The known limit is that the reviewer cannot manage the whole workspace. The alternative is to ask a workspace owner to perform administrative changes. Customers who require outside reviewers to administer multiple workspaces should not assume this arrangement fits.

A second fictional row concerns exporting a summary. The supported output is a plain text summary; the limit is that annotations are not included. An alternative could be a separately approved record of annotations, if that process actually exists. Customers who require a complete archival package should wait or choose another supported approach.

The table is not a list of VideoClaw capabilities. Its invented facts demonstrate the communication method. Replace them with verified facts before using the structure for your own product.

## Learn from a bounded first-party example

[Notion's offline help page identifies required apps and specific actions that remain unavailable offline](https://www.notion.com/en-gb/help/use-pages-offline). The useful editorial pattern is that a broad capability is accompanied by conditions and exclusions in the same explanation.

You do not need to reproduce another product's list. Apply the pattern to the dependency your customer is evaluating. A capability headline should lead naturally to its operating conditions rather than requiring a customer to discover them through failure.

Keep conditions close to the relevant claim. If a feature works only for an account role, environment, or file type, name that boundary where you describe the feature.

Avoid presenting every small technical detail at the same level. Prioritize limitations that change suitability, require extra work, or create a likely misunderstanding. Link to current detailed documentation for the rest.

Check those linked details before publication. A founder video can remain discoverable long after a constraint changes, so give the viewer a current written reference.

## Rewrite vague claims into useful choices

Use these three original fictional rewrites as a practice exercise.

“Works for your whole team” becomes “Workspace members can comment on shared review items; only workspace owners can change approval settings.” The replacement identifies who can do what instead of making teamwork sound unlimited.

“Export everything you need” becomes “The export includes the written summary. It does not include annotations, so this version is not a complete archive of the review.” The replacement names the missing element and its practical consequence.

“Set it up in minutes” becomes “Setup requires a workspace owner to configure the approval roles. If your organization needs a separate approval policy for each department, confirm that requirement with us before proceeding.” The replacement avoids an unsupported time claim and surfaces a suitability question.

These are not merely more cautious sentences. They help readers decide. A customer can compare their actual workflow with the conditions and recognize when further investigation is needed.

After rewriting, ask whether the sentence still implies more than the verified facts. Words such as automatic, complete, unlimited, and seamless deserve particular scrutiny.

## Explain workarounds without disguising their cost

A workaround is not the missing feature under another name. Describe what the customer must do, who owns the extra step, and what remains unsupported.

For the fictional export example, manually preserving annotations might require additional review and a separate record. If that is not a supported process, do not recommend it simply to keep a prospect interested. A founder should be willing to say that the product is not currently suitable for the requirement.

Separate “we can help you assess this” from “we support this.” A conversation is not evidence that a capability exists. Likewise, a roadmap item does not solve a current dependency.

If the alternative involves security, privacy, legal, or operational risk, involve the responsible specialists rather than improvising a public workaround.

In a founder recording, show the limit clearly and say it aloud. Do not rely on a small caption beneath a confident broad claim. The viewer should understand the boundary even if they only listen.

## Maintain one current explanation across customer conversations

Save the verified table as the internal reference for public language. When the product changes, update the relevant row and then review the places that use it: founder videos, sales answers, help material, and announcements.

Keep dated recordings understandable. If a material limitation is removed or changes, add a clear current reference rather than leaving viewers to guess whether the old statement still applies.

Ask support which phrases repeatedly create surprise. Those questions can reveal that a technically accurate description is still being interpreted too broadly. Repair the phrasing around the task and condition.

A useful final test is to ask a colleague: “Who should not use this for their current requirement?” If the answer is impossible to find, the message may still be too promotional.
