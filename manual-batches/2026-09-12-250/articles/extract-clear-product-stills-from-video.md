Extract useful product stills by finding a frame that shows a complete, current product state, exporting it from the original recording, and checking it at its intended display size. Reject blurred transitions and misleading crops. Keep the source timestamp so another editor can trace the image back to the demonstration.

## Decide what the still must prove

Begin with the destination and the reader’s question. A sales slide explaining an approval step needs a visible decision and its context. A website image introducing a dashboard may need the overall layout. These are different selection jobs even when both images come from the same recording.

Write a short image brief: “Show that the reviewer can see the requested change before choosing an action.” This is more useful than “Find a good screenshot.” It tells you which interface elements must survive the crop and which can disappear. If the footage cannot show the requested fact, change the brief or obtain another source. A caption cannot supply visual evidence that is missing.

Choose the display context early. A frame may look clear on a large editing monitor and become unreadable inside a narrow email column. Place a rough candidate in a mockup before spending time on detailed cleanup. The real question is whether someone can recognize the intended product state where the image will actually appear.

## Search around the action, not just on it

Watch the relevant interaction once at normal speed. Identify three moments: the state before the action, the action itself, and the settled result. The most informative still is often before or after the click rather than during the moving transition.

Step through nearby frames. Look for cursor overlap, motion blur, partially opened menus, loading indicators and notifications. A dramatic frame can be a poor explanatory image if it shows a temporary state the viewer might mistake for the normal interface. Also notice whether the presenter’s face or captions obscure a control you need.

Adobe’s [still-frame export instructions](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/render-and-export/export-files/export-a-still-frame.html) show how to select an exact frame and export it as an image. The underlying editorial choice remains yours.

Keep several candidates until you see them together. Label them by meaning—before review, review open, decision recorded—alongside the timestamp. That makes comparison easier than filenames such as screenshot-final-two, especially when another teammate must choose an image without reopening the entire recording.

## Build a frame-selection contact sheet

Use this original worksheet to compare candidates. The example is a fictional review application, and the observations illustrate the method rather than a real product.

| Candidate | What is visible | Problem | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Review panel opening | Part of the change request | Panel is still moving and text is soft | Reject |
| Review panel settled | Request, reviewer options and task name | Cursor covers a small label | Inspect adjacent frames |
| Decision confirmation | Result and responsible person | Original request is no longer visible | Use only when explaining completion |
| Wide dashboard | Several projects and navigation | Individual decisions are too small | Reserve for orientation |

Add columns for intended display width, proposed crop and caption. Do not choose a frame solely because it looks attractive in the contact sheet; verify the destination mockup too.

When two images communicate different stages, keep both rather than forcing one to do every job. A before-and-after pair can explain a change more honestly than a composite that appears to show a single real screen. Label a composite if you deliberately construct one.

## Export from the best available source

Use the original recording when it is available and appropriate to use. Repeatedly capturing a browser player or taking screenshots of a compressed derivative can leave you judging artifacts introduced after the original capture. An exported frame still contains only the detail present in its source; saving a larger image does not recreate unreadable interface text.

Check the export’s dimensions, color appearance and file format against the destination. Adobe’s [still-image export guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/render-and-export/export-files/export-a-still-image.html) describes selecting an image format and exporting a single frame. Its choices are specific to Premiere; use the equivalent controls in your editor.

Open the exported file outside the editing application. Compare it with the original frame and the destination mockup. If colors or brightness differ unexpectedly, investigate the export path before compensating by eye. Keep an untouched export beside any cropped derivative so a later change does not require another capture. Record the source filename and timestamp in your working notes or asset naming convention.

## Crop to explain without changing the claim

A useful crop removes distraction while preserving the relationship the image is meant to show. If the claim concerns a reviewer’s decision, keep the request and the available action together. Cropping away the request can turn a meaningful workflow into a mysterious button.

Check labels, units, filters and status indicators before trimming an edge. A dashboard filtered to one project should not be presented as an organization-wide view. A demonstration using sample data should remain understandable as a demonstration. Exclude unrelated private information rather than relying on a vague caption to make its appearance harmless.

Add restrained annotations only when they improve comprehension. A numbered callout can identify a control; an arrow should point precisely and avoid covering text. Write the caption as an explanation of the visible state: “The reviewer sees the requested change beside the decision controls.” Avoid outcome claims such as “This eliminates review delays” unless separately supported.

If the necessary crop is too small to read, reconsider the layout. A larger detail image or two separate views may solve the problem without inventing visual detail.

## Test the image without the video

Show the candidate in its actual page or slide layout to someone who has not watched the recording. Ask what product state they think it shows and what they would do next. Do not explain the image first; that removes the uncertainty you need to test.

Compare their interpretation with the image brief. If they identify the wrong action, revise the crop or choose a different moment. If they understand the action but cannot read supporting text, change the display treatment. These are separate problems with different fixes.

Review the final caption and alternative text alongside the image. The caption can explain why the state matters; alternative text should communicate the useful visual information in the surrounding context. Avoid repeating a nearby paragraph word for word.

Save the selected frame, its editable annotation version if relevant, and a short source reference. Note where the image is used so a product change can be checked later. A small set of clear, traceable stills is more useful than a folder of attractive screenshots whose meaning nobody can reconstruct.
