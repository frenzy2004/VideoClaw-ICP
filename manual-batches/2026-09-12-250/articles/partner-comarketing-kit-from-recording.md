Build a partner co-marketing kit from one recording by agreeing on the shared audience problem, selecting useful excerpts, and giving each partner an introduction suited to its own readers. Keep the joint offer and factual claims consistent. Package clear destinations and editable copy so both teams can distribute the material confidently.

## Agree on the shared offer in plain language

Start with what the two partners are asking the audience to explore. A joint recording may discuss several subjects, but the campaign needs a clear shared purpose. Write a sentence that both teams would be comfortable using without adding a different promise.

[HubSpot’s co-marketing guide](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/34188/what-in-the-heck-is-co-marketing.aspx?gad=1) describes partners promoting a shared offer. Use that distinction to keep the kit centered on a genuine joint contribution rather than two unrelated sales pitches placed beside each other.

Identify the overlap between the audiences. A partner may reach practitioners while the other reaches team leaders. The common problem can remain the same even when the examples and introductions differ. Avoid assuming that one partner’s audience knows the other company or the terminology used in the recording.

Write down what each partner contributes to the explanation. This makes it easier to choose excerpts that represent both perspectives fairly and prevents the finished kit from treating one participant as decorative endorsement for the other.

## Choose excerpts that support complementary introductions

Review the recording for complete ideas related to the shared offer. Keep enough of each exchange to preserve the speaker’s meaning and the other participant’s contribution. A useful excerpt may include both a question and an answer.

Give each candidate a short description of the audience job it supports. One might explain the problem, another compare approaches, and another demonstrate a relevant workflow. Do not create several near-identical clips just to provide each partner with the same number of files.

Check whether an excerpt depends on a claim from the other partner. If so, keep that context in the clip or its introduction. Avoid editing the discussion so that one company appears to make or validate a statement it did not.

Select the intended destination before writing promotional copy. An excerpt leading to the full discussion needs a different invitation from a clip leading to a joint worksheet. The viewer should receive what the introduction promises, regardless of which partner’s channel brought them there.

## Build a two-partner content kit

This original example describes a fictional joint session between a workflow software company and an operations consultancy. It illustrates a kit structure, not a real partnership.

| Kit item | Shared element | Partner-specific element |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Campaign summary | The problem of unclear exception ownership | Why the audience encounters it |
| Expert excerpt | The same complete discussion and attribution | A practitioner or management introduction |
| Demonstration excerpt | The visible workflow and its limits | The question the audience should consider |
| Email section | The joint offer and destination | Opening context in the sender’s voice |
| Social post | Accurate speaker names and topic | A relevant discussion prompt |
| Follow-up note | Useful next resource | The partner’s appropriate contact route |

Include a short explanation of which elements are fixed and which are editable. The goal is practical clarity, not a large approval manual.

Keep the kit small enough for the receiving team to use. A folder containing dozens of unlabeled variants creates more decisions than it resolves. Provide a recommended starting asset and explain when the alternatives are more appropriate.

## Write two introductions without creating two stories

Adapt the opening to the reader’s context while keeping the underlying explanation consistent. For the fictional session, the software company might introduce the visible handoff, while the consultancy might begin with the organizational question of who owns an exception.

A practitioner introduction could say: “This exchange examines what a reviewer needs to see before taking over a request.” A management introduction could say: “The discussion asks how two teams can agree on the signal that a handoff is complete.” Both can lead to the same recording when those ideas are actually present.

Do not add claims during adaptation. A partner’s copy should not turn a conditional example into a guaranteed benefit or imply a product integration that the session did not establish. Check the introductions against the same shared statement and source material.

Leave room for the sender’s own voice. A kit should provide useful material and boundaries, not force identical language onto audiences with different needs. Encourage meaningful context rather than superficial changes to adjectives or company names.

## Package destinations and email assets carefully

Use descriptive filenames and link labels so the partner can identify the right asset without reopening every file. Include the final viewing destination, a short explanation of its purpose and any relevant access condition.

For email, plan the viewing path explicitly. [Mailchimp’s video-email guidance](https://mailchimp.com/help/add-video-to-an-email/) explains linked thumbnails as an alternative to embedded playback. Give the partner a suitable image, clear link text and the destination URL, rather than assuming their email system will play the video inside the message.

Check the joint page from a visitor’s perspective. Does it explain both partners’ roles? Does it deliver the resource promised in the email? Are names, speaker titles and the next action consistent across the assets?

If tracking links differ between partners, preserve the same destination experience and document the naming convention privately. Do not change the offer accidentally while changing the source label. The campaign should remain understandable to someone who encounters it through either partner or receives a forwarded message.

## Test the kit as the receiving partner

Ask someone who did not assemble the kit to prepare a sample email or post from it. Observe which decisions they cannot make without asking the author. Improve the labels, instructions or recommended starting point where needed.

Have each partner review the factual statements attributed to its participants and the description of its contribution. Focus the review on concrete content: the selected excerpt, introduction, offer and destination. Broad approval of a folder is less useful than agreement on the actual materials people will publish.

After the campaign, compare the questions the two audiences raised. Different responses may reveal different information needs rather than a winning or losing partner. Keep observed distribution results separate from explanations about why they occurred.

Retain the source references and final kit so future reuse begins with known material. If the offer or product changes, reassess the relevant excerpts before redistributing them. The useful outcome is a coherent joint explanation that each partner can present naturally to its own audience.
