Treat a video editing turnaround claim as incomplete until the vendor defines its start event, working calendar, promised output, exclusions, and missed-deadline response. A first draft is not necessarily a final usable file. Map intake, review, corrections, export, and delivery before relying on an advertised speed for a fixed business deadline.

## Identify what the advertised clock produces

Copy the supplier's exact description into your comparison notes and underline the output. Is it an initial response, an editor assignment, a first draft, a revision, or an approved final export?

[Video Husky's service page](https://www.videohusky.com/) distinguishes first-draft turnaround from later revisions. That is evidence of how one provider describes its offer, not a tested guarantee or a delivery norm for the category.

Ask the vendor to show where the quoted plan defines the promise. If the sales conversation uses “delivery” while the terms say “first draft,” resolve the difference before purchasing.

Specify what you need at the end: an approved video in named formats, with required captions or other files, available to the person who will publish it. A review link arriving on time may not satisfy that requirement.

## Define accepted intake and working time

Uploading footage does not necessarily start the clock. The vendor may need a complete brief, accessible files, approved script, asset permissions, or confirmation that the project fits the purchased service.

Ask for a clear intake acknowledgment. It should state whether the job is accepted, what is missing, and when the relevant delivery period begins.

Record business hours, holidays, cutoffs, and time zone. A duration expressed in business days behaves differently from elapsed hours, especially when intake occurs near a weekend or holiday.

Ask how the vendor handles a later discovery that an input is missing. Does the clock pause, restart, or continue for the unaffected work? The answer should be part of the agreed workflow, not an improvised explanation after the deadline has passed.

## Draw the promised-clock worksheet

This original worksheet separates supplier work from dependencies. Fill durations and commitments from the actual offer; the table does not prescribe standard turnaround times.

| Event | Who controls it? | Evidence | Clock question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Files submitted | Buyer | Upload and access confirmation | Is submission alone sufficient? |
| Intake accepted | Supplier | Written readiness acknowledgment | Does the committed period begin here? |
| First draft delivered | Supplier | Named review version | Is this the advertised output? |
| Buyer review | Buyer | Consolidated, version-specific notes | Does waiting pause the schedule? |
| Correction round | Supplier | Revised version and issue responses | What response commitment applies? |
| Final approval | Authorized buyer | Explicit approval of exact cut | What remains before export? |
| Final delivery | Supplier and recipient | Complete files received and checked | Is download or only upload included? |

Include a recipient check at the finish. A file may be uploaded on time but inaccessible because the link requires an unavailable account, the export is incomplete, or the wrong version was delivered. Agree which verification the supplier performs and which the buyer must complete before calling the job finished.

Add the target final deadline and work backward through the events. Identify any step whose duration has not been agreed.

For each unknown, ask a specific question rather than adding an arbitrary safety margin. A buffer cannot reliably compensate for an undefined review process or an excluded kind of editing work.

## Test one realistic deadline scenario

Give the shortlisted provider a representative brief and a calendar date when the final files must be usable. Ask it to walk through the complete path, including your review availability.

For example, suppose intake is ready late in the supplier's workweek, the first draft arrives before your reviewer returns, and a correction requires a new product screenshot. The constraint may be buyer decision time rather than editing speed.

Use illustrative dates only as a rehearsal, and label them as such. Do not describe the rehearsal as a service benchmark or a promise the provider has not accepted.

Ask what happens if the project is more complex than the standard offer. Multicamera footage, motion graphics, poor audio, or unusually long source material may alter the quoted scope. Have the vendor identify those conditions before confirming that its advertised clock applies.

## Agree the deadline, exclusions, and escalation

[Upwork's milestone guidance](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/44564821903763-Understanding-milestones-on-fixed-price-contracts) links scope, dates, feedback, and acceptance. The general purchasing lesson is to connect the timing promise to a defined deliverable, while respecting the actual platform or contract terms.

Record who can approve the deadline and who handles an emerging delay. Ask when the supplier will warn you that a commitment is at risk and what information that notice will contain.

If a guarantee includes a remedy, obtain its exact terms. A service credit, refund eligibility, or rework promise is not the same as assurance that the business will have a usable video on time. Do not invent a standard remedy or advise that every delay creates a particular legal entitlement.

Identify your own obligations too. A vendor cannot reliably commit to final delivery if the buyer reserves unlimited time to approve each cut or can change the brief without discussing the schedule.

## Purchase against the usable final result

Compare vendors using the same deadline scenario and final package. A slower advertised first draft may still lead to a more credible final delivery if review and corrections are clearly staffed.

Ask for relevant historical examples or references, but distinguish those from proof that your specific project will finish on time. If the date is critical, consider a bounded paid trial with the same intake and review pattern before making a larger commitment.

The order should record the accepted intake conditions, promised output, working calendar, review responsibilities, final deadline, exclusions, and escalation path. Keep the exact version of the offer that informed the purchase.

After the job, compare actual events with that clock. Record whether delay came from supplier work, buyer waiting, missing inputs, or approved scope change. That evidence will make the next purchase more accurate.

A credible turnaround commitment is not the shortest number on a landing page. It is a complete sequence that both parties can follow to the final files the business actually needs.
