Build a sales-demo follow-up around the buyer’s stated priorities, using only the relevant moments from the recording and the next steps actually agreed. Separate answered questions from open ones. Add a short introduction so the recipient can understand why each excerpt matters without replaying the entire meeting.

## Start with the buyer’s notes, not the demo agenda

Immediately after the meeting, list the questions the buyer wanted answered. Compare that list with the demonstration’s planned agenda. The useful follow-up usually follows the buyer’s concerns, even if the presenter spent more time on other features.

Distinguish three kinds of note: an explicit buyer priority, your interpretation, and an agreed next step. Do not convert “we might involve our operations lead” into a commitment that the buyer made. Uncertainty belongs in the recap when it affects what happens next.

[Vidyard’s discussion of mid-funnel video](https://www.vidyard.com/video-in-motion-benchmark-report/) describes adding relevant context for people entering a deal after a meeting. Use that as a practical direction, without importing the report’s performance claims.

Write a one-sentence purpose for the follow-up: “Help the operations lead understand the exception workflow before deciding whether to join the next conversation.” That purpose determines what to include and gives you a reason to leave unrelated demonstration footage out of the package.

## Choose the smallest complete proof moment

Find the segment that answers each priority. Include enough setup to show what is happening, the meaningful interaction, and the result. A clip that begins with a cursor already moving may save seconds while removing the context that makes the action understandable.

Listen to the narration around the selected boundaries. Keep qualifications about availability, configuration or the example environment. If the presenter corrected a statement later, use the corrected explanation or add a clearly separate clarification. Do not preserve a convenient but inaccurate claim because it appeared in the original meeting.

Consider whether a timestamped link would serve the buyer better than a new edit. [Wistia’s embed-link documentation](https://docs.wistia.com/docs/embed-links) describes links that control playback position in a supported embed. The exact mechanism depends on the host, so verify the recipient’s actual viewing path.

If the relevant demonstration is already concise, a direct link with one useful sentence may be enough. Create an excerpt when it removes genuine navigation work, not merely to increase the number of assets produced.

## Use a recap assembly page

This original worksheet uses a fictional evaluation of a request-management product. It does not describe an actual customer conversation.

| Buyer priority | Recording evidence | Status | Follow-up treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| See how an exception reaches a reviewer | Current demo shows the handoff | Answered in the meeting | Link the complete interaction |
| Understand external access | Presenter promised to check | Open | State that confirmation is still needed |
| Compare the process with the buyer’s workflow | No buyer workflow was shown | Not evaluated | Invite one concrete example |
| Decide who joins the next session | Buyer mentioned an operations colleague | Tentative | Ask rather than assume attendance |

Add exact timestamps and destination URLs after selecting the footage. The status column prevents the recap from presenting all topics as resolved.

Write the email introduction from the table, not from memory. A useful recap might say that the first excerpt shows the handoff discussed, while external access remains an open question. That wording respects what the meeting established and makes it easier for the buyer to forward the right information.

## Make a forwarded recap understandable

Assume the message may reach someone who did not attend. Give that person a short account of the problem being evaluated, the purpose of each clip, and what remains undecided. Avoid internal shorthand, unexplained feature names and references to “the thing we discussed.”

Use descriptive link text. “Watch the exception reach the reviewer” tells a recipient what to expect. “Video one” does not. Keep the surrounding sentence factual: the clip shows a workflow; it does not prove that the buyer will achieve a particular result.

For the fictional example, an introduction could read: “We looked at how an exception moves to a reviewer. The linked segment shows that handoff. We have not yet confirmed the external-access requirement, so that remains separate from what the demonstration establishes.”

Personalization should come from relevant context, not from inserting the recipient’s name repeatedly. A short explanation that reflects the buyer’s actual question is more useful than a lengthy message containing generic compliments and a broad product pitch.

## Check the recipient’s complete viewing path

Open every link as a recipient would, using an appropriate non-owner view when possible. Confirm that access, playback position and visible context behave as intended. A link that works in the presenter’s account may not work for a buyer or a forwarded recipient.

Check the beginning and end of each excerpt. Remove unrelated private material, but ensure the remaining content is still understandable. Inspect the actual player on a small screen, especially when the proof depends on interface details. A technically playable clip can still fail its job if the relevant label is unreadable.

Keep the email and the video destination aligned. If the email promises an answer about exception routing, the link should not open a generic library page. If a form or sign-in appears, consider whether that barrier is appropriate for this specific follow-up.

Finally, confirm that the selected recording is suitable for the intended external use. This is a practical source-selection check; it does not require turning the recap into a public policy notice. Choose a different approved demonstration when the original call contains material that should not be shared.

## End with an accurate, useful next step

Use the next step the meeting supports. If the buyer agreed to review a workflow with a colleague, make that action easy. If no next step was agreed, ask a focused question rather than present a new meeting as settled.

For example: “Would your operations lead need to see a routine request or an exception first?” invites a concrete response. “Let us know your thoughts” asks the buyer to invent the task. Keep the request proportional to the uncertainty that remains.

Maintain a small private record of what was sent: buyer question, excerpt, destination and unresolved issue. This helps the next seller avoid sending the same proof again as though it were new. It also makes a correction easier if product behavior changes.

Do not treat a view or click as proof of purchase readiness. The follow-up’s first job is to make the conversation clearer and the evidence easier to retrieve. Use any observed response to decide what clarification is useful next, while keeping unsupported conclusions out of the account record.
