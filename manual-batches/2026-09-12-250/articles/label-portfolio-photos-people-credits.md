Label portfolio photos by confirming who appears, what is happening and which credit the image supplier requires. Keep those facts attached to the asset, then write a caption suited to the publication. Do not identify people from appearance or assume that an event guest list proves who is visible in a particular photograph.

## Separate identification from caption writing

A caption is a reader-facing description. The identification record is the evidence behind it. Build the record first so a polished sentence does not conceal uncertainty about a person's name, company or role.

Start with the asset identifier, capture date if supplied, event context and photographer or supplier. Ask the photographer or event coordinator for an annotated contact sheet when names are missing. Where necessary, ask the relevant company contact to confirm the people and affiliations in a particular image.

Use the image itself to describe visible action, but avoid guessing what an expression means. “A founder demonstrates a device” may be supportable from the scene and confirmed context. “Investors are impressed by the breakthrough” adds an interpretation that the photograph cannot establish.

The [IPTC Photo Metadata Standard](https://www.iptc.org/std/photometadata/specification/IPTC-PhotoMetadata-2025.1.html) separates descriptive information from credit and creation-date fields. That separation is useful for portfolio work: knowing what an image shows does not tell you how its supplier should be credited.

## Create a reliable contact-sheet record

Give every image a stable reference before collecting corrections. A reviewer saying “the third photo” is ambiguous once files are sorted differently. Use the asset ID in the contact sheet and in the form where people confirm names.

For a group image, record positions such as left to right or front row and back row. Do not make the public caption longer than necessary, but preserve enough detail privately that another editor can match names to people correctly.

Record the source of each identification. That could be the company contact's confirmation or the event organizer's verified annotation. A guest list can provide candidates to check, but it should not be treated as proof that every listed person appears.

[Canto's metadata-management guide](https://www.canto.com/glossary/metadata-management/) distinguishes information describing content from information about asset administration and relationships. Apply that distinction by keeping names and scene descriptions separate from release instructions and links to related files.

Use a visible unresolved state when a name is uncertain. It is better to select a different image or use an accurate general description than publish a confident misidentification.

## Work through three fictional scenes

The following examples describe invented photographs. They illustrate a method rather than actual portfolio events.

| PHOTO-101 | Alder founder demonstrates a prototype at a company table | Lena Ortiz, founder; company contact confirmed against PHOTO-101 on 2026-09-10 | Mira Chen Photography | Write a named caption |
| PHOTO-102 | Beacon and Cobalt teams discuss a display | Two people confirmed; one unresolved | North Hall Events | Hold named group caption |
| PHOTO-103 | Wide view of an operator roundtable | Event and date confirmed; individual names unnecessary | Platform event team | Use a general scene description |

For PHOTO-101, the final fictional placement reads: “Alder founder Lena Ortiz demonstrates the company's prototype during the portfolio showcase.” Immediately beneath it, the credit line reads exactly “Mira Chen Photography,” following this example's supplied placement instruction. The record separately holds the supplied capture date and company confirmation; a review date must not be mistaken for the capture date.

PHOTO-102's fictional positional record is: left, Ravi Sen, Beacon marketing lead, confirmed by Beacon's contact; center, Mei Tan, Cobalt product lead, confirmed by Cobalt's contact; right, identity unresolved. Both confirmations reference PHOTO-102 and are dated 2026-09-10. No final named group caption is released while the right-hand subject remains unresolved. If an approved alternative crop shows only the confirmed pair, check their new left-to-right order and display the exact credit “North Hall Events” in the required credit position.

For PHOTO-102, do not assign the remaining guest-list name by elimination. Ask for confirmation or choose a frame where the unresolved person is not part of the intended subject. Cropping still requires checking whether the resulting image accurately represents the scene.

For PHOTO-103, a general caption may be sufficient. Naming everyone in a wide scene creates work and possible errors without necessarily helping the reader.

## Keep credits exact and context current

Copy the supplier's confirmed credit wording into a dedicated field. A photographer's personal name, studio name and agency credit are not interchangeable. If the supplier has not provided wording, ask for it rather than constructing a credit from a filename.

Do not treat a credit line as evidence that publication is allowed. The media package should separately contain the company's or rights owner's instructions about use. Your caption workflow records those instructions; it does not decide legal rights.

Check time-sensitive affiliations. A photograph may show a person who has since changed roles. Decide whether the caption describes the person at the time of capture or in their current role, and make that distinction clear where relevant. Do not rewrite historical context silently.

When an image accompanies a new story, verify that the caption still fits. A photograph from an earlier gathering should not imply that its participants attended a different event or endorsed a later announcement. Adding the correct date or choosing a different photo may solve the problem.

Preserve the original identification record even when the publication uses a shorter caption.

## Review the final crop and placement

Caption review should happen against the exact image being published. A crop can remove one of the named people or change the apparent focus of a scene. A collage can bring together people who were not photographed together.

Ask the reviewer to compare the final crop with the identification record. Confirm that names remain in the correct order, the visible action matches the description and the supplied credit is included where required by the applicable instructions.

Also inspect the surrounding article. A neutral image caption can become misleading when placed under a headline that implies a partnership or endorsement. The portfolio editor should check the combined meaning of image, caption and adjacent text.

Write alternative text for the image's purpose in that placement rather than duplicating a long caption mechanically. If the caption already identifies the people, the alternative text can focus on the visible information needed to understand the content. Avoid adding speculative identity or sensitive descriptions.

Keep the final crop reference with the caption record so later reuse does not attach the wrong names to a different version.

## Correct mistakes at the asset and destination

When a company reports a naming error, confirm the correction and identify every destination using that image and caption. Fixing the library field alone may not change captions already copied into a newsletter, article or social post.

Record the corrected value, its source and the affected publication references. Use the appropriate correction process for each destination. Do not promise that an edited source record will update external copies automatically.

Tell the company what was corrected and what remains outstanding. A copied partner post may need a separate request, while an internal resource can be updated immediately by its owner.

Use the mistake to improve the contact-sheet process. Perhaps the image lacked a stable reference, a reviewer checked the uncropped original or a caption was copied from a neighboring image. Address the specific cause rather than adding a general approval step to every photograph.

A dependable photo library lets the next editor reuse confirmed facts with confidence while still checking the new context. That is the practical purpose of keeping identification, caption, credit and version information connected.
