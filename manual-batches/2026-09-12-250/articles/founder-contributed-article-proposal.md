Pitch a contributed article by proposing one useful argument grounded in experience you can explain and evidence you can support. Show the editor who the article helps, what those readers will learn, and why you are equipped to write it. Build the proposal around a decision, not your company’s publicity needs.

## Find a decision you understand unusually well

Begin with a situation in which you had to choose between plausible alternatives. Perhaps your startup changed how it handled pilot requests, decided to stop promising a feature, or discovered that a familiar customer question meant something different than expected.

The experience becomes useful when you can explain the choice, the information available, and what you would do differently. A success announcement alone does not provide that reasoning.

Make a list of decisions you can discuss without revealing private customer information. For each, ask what another founder, manager, or practitioner could learn. Choose one where your experience contains a specific tension.

For example, a fictional founder might write about why the team replaced open-ended pilot commitments with a defined learning question. The article could help readers decide what a pilot is supposed to establish. That is a more useful starting point than “Lessons from our exciting growth journey.”

## Turn the experience into an argument

An experience describes what happened. An argument explains what the reader should understand or reconsider because of it.

Try this original drafting frame: “When a team faces [situation], it should consider [decision], because [reason], provided [boundary].” The sentence is a thinking aid, not a headline you must publish.

For the fictional pilot example, the argument might be: “When a pilot keeps expanding, agree on the question it must answer before adding more requested work.” The boundary could be that an exploratory partnership intentionally has a different purpose.

Make the relationship between your experience and your argument visible: identify the decision you owned, the records you can inspect, and the limits of the lesson. This is the original proposal method used here.

Avoid turning one company’s experience into a universal claim. You can offer a reasoned method without claiming it works for every team. Acknowledging the conditions under which it helped often makes the article more informative.

## Build an evidence and counterargument map

List the claims required for the argument to hold. Beside each, write what supports it and what remains uncertain.

| Proposed claim | Support to assemble | Boundary to explain |
|---|---|---|
| The pilot had no agreed ending | The original plan and participant recollections | Other pilots may deliberately be exploratory |
| Requests expanded beyond the initial task | A dated request history | Added work may sometimes be valuable |
| A clear learning question improved the next discussion | A specific meeting example | One discussion does not prove a general outcome |
| Other teams can use the question-setting method | An original worked exercise | Readers must adapt it to their situation |

This is a fictional example of the map. Do not fill the evidence column with invented company documents or customer quotations.

Include the strongest reasonable objection. A reader may ask whether a narrow learning question would prevent discovery. Answer by explaining how a team can preserve an exploration lane while keeping a decision lane clear.

That objection gives the article substance. It prevents the proposal from sounding like a rule invented after one favorable experience.

## Give the editor a concrete article to imagine

Build a compact proposal with five parts: the reader situation, the argument, the supporting material, the structure, and your relevant experience.

Here is an original fictional proposal:

> Working title: The Pilot Needs a Question Before It Needs More Features
>
> This article is for early product teams whose customer pilots keep expanding without a clear decision point. I will explain a method for separating work that answers the pilot’s central question from work that belongs in a later project.
>
> The piece will use a fictionalized exercise, supported by my own documented operating experience, and address when an exploratory partnership should remain broader. Readers will leave with a one-page pilot question map they can adapt.

To complete that fictional proposal, add a concrete outline: first reconstruct the original pilot question; then classify three incoming requests; work the decision map; examine an intentionally exploratory partnership; close with a customer-conversation prompt. The fictional author qualification is: “Maya Rao led Fieldnote's pilot planning and the request-review conversations used to develop this exercise.” This is an invented qualification for the example, not a real credential.

In a real proposal, distinguish actual operating experience from the fictional exercise used to teach it. Do not imply that a composite example is a reported customer case.

The structure should show progression: the problem, the decision, the method, the counterexample, and the application. An editor should be able to picture the finished article without guessing what fills the middle.

## Make the work useful beyond your company

Remove the company name from the central lesson and read it again. Does the advice still help the intended reader? If not, you may have a product announcement rather than a contributed article.

Google’s [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks whether material adds original value and reflects firsthand expertise. For this proposal, the practical test is whether you bring something beyond a summary of familiar advice.

Add a useful original work product. It might be a decision map, an annotated example, a comparison of two approaches, or a short exercise. The work product should make the reasoning easier to apply, not merely decorate the article.

Also check whether every section advances the same argument. A paragraph about fundraising may be true but irrelevant. A product screenshot may be attractive but unnecessary. Keep supporting material only when it helps readers make the decision the proposal promises.

The editor’s audience is the destination. Your biography explains why you can help them; it does not need to dominate the piece.

## Submit to the publication’s actual requirements

Read the publication’s current instructions before preparing the final submission. [Fast Company's Work Life instructions](https://fastcompany.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000291906-How-can-I-submit-an-editorial-contribution) request complete unpublished articles, not pitches or outlines. For that destination, use this proposal canvas internally to develop the required draft; do not send it as a pitch. Other publications may request a proposal. Check the requested format, audience, topic scope, and any rules about prior publication or simultaneous submissions. Do not assume that one outlet’s process applies elsewhere.

Tailor the proposal to material the outlet actually publishes. Identify a useful connection to its coverage without claiming that the publication has ignored your topic unless you have examined that claim carefully.

Keep a private submission record with the version, date, outlet, and any response. If an editor asks for a revision, clarify which part of the argument needs work. A request to narrow the scope is different from a request for more supporting evidence.
