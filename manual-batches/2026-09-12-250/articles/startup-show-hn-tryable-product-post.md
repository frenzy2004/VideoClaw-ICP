Prepare a Show HN post by making the work usable, rehearsing a stranger’s path to one meaningful result, and being ready to explain how you built it. Check the current community rules before submitting. A waitlist and a polished announcement are not substitutes for something people can actually try.

## Check whether you have something to show

[The official Show HN guidelines](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html) call for personally made work people can try, with the maker available to discuss it. They exclude landing pages and fundraisers. Check the complete rules for exceptions and the current scope before deciding your work fits.

Funding is not the qualifying event. A newly funded founder may have an interesting working tool, or only a plan for one. Decide from the visitor’s experience rather than from the company’s announcement calendar.

Write a one-line description of the usable result. “Join our early-access list” describes a request you make of the visitor. “Inspect a sample file and identify duplicate entries” describes something the visitor can do.

If access is not ready, do not disguise a waitlist as an interactive product. Finish the trial path or postpone the Show HN. A regular submission has its own relevance requirements; it is not a loophole for an unsuitable promotional page.

This guide prepares the founder privately. It does not submit anything, imply launch approval, or supply a guaranteed route to attention.

## Rehearse the visitor path without founder privileges

Open the intended destination in a session that does not inherit your account or internal permissions. Work through the exact task you expect a new visitor to perform. Do not begin from the development screen already loaded with helpful data.

Use this original fictional rehearsal for a small file-inspection tool called Tablecheck. It is an illustrative product, not a claim about an existing service.

| Step | Visitor action | Useful result | Failure to fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arrival | Opens the submitted link | Sees what the tool inspects | Only a company story is visible |
| First choice | Selects a supplied synthetic sample | Can begin without uploading private data | Sample control requires an invitation |
| Inspection | Runs the duplicate check | Receives a readable list of repeated rows | Results show an unexplained internal identifier |
| Understanding | Opens one flagged example | Sees which fields caused the match | The visitor cannot distinguish exact from partial matches |
| Exit | Downloads a sample report or closes the session | Understands the result without contacting sales | A useful result is hidden behind a sales form |

Record each observed result during the actual rehearsal. Do not mark a row passed because the developer says it should work.

If the product legitimately needs an account, explain the requirement and reduce unnecessary friction where feasible. Do not remove important safeguards merely to simplify a launch. The rehearsal should expose tradeoffs, not override them.

## Provide a safe example and honest limits

A synthetic example lets someone understand a workflow without volunteering real customer information. Label it as synthetic and make it representative enough to reveal what the tool does.

For Tablecheck, the sample contains repeated maintenance-record rows with fictional locations. It deliberately includes both an exact duplicate and two similar descriptions that should remain separate. This makes the matching boundary visible.

Document the current limitation beside the result: the fictional tool compares selected fields and does not determine whether two differently worded descriptions refer to the same real repair. A visitor can then judge the method rather than infer capabilities from a clean-looking report.

Check what happens to uploaded material in the actual implementation before describing it. Do not claim files stay on-device, disappear after a session, or remain private merely because that would make the trial more appealing. If you cannot verify the handling, use a controlled sample and resolve the claim before launch.

Also test an ordinary failure: an empty file, an unsupported format, or a missing expected column. The message should explain what happened without exposing internal details. A tryable product includes understandable boundaries, not only a successful path.

## Prepare the maker story without outsourcing its authorship

[Hacker News’s guidelines](https://news.ycombinator.com/newsguidelines.html) prohibit generated or AI-edited text and discourage primarily promotional participation. Write the actual submission and replies yourself. Do not paste this article’s examples into HN; they are private preparation material.

Build an ingredient sheet instead of a polished announcement. For the fictional Tablecheck example, it contains:

- The triggering problem: repeated rows obscured a small maintenance dataset.
- The work personally built: a comparison routine and a results view.
- The technical choice: explicit field matching instead of inferred semantic similarity.
- The reason for that choice: a visitor can inspect why rows matched.
- The limitation: related descriptions may remain separate when the fields differ.
- The useful discussion: where explicit matching becomes too restrictive for real workflows.

These ingredients support a substantive explanation without declaring the tool revolutionary. The founder must verify that each statement describes their actual contribution and then express it personally.

A concise product description is not a substitute for a real technical tradeoff. If you cannot explain one, spend more time understanding the work before inviting a discussion about it. For a broader messaging exercise, see [explaining product limitations](/blog/startup-product-limitations-message).

## Reserve time for an actual conversation

Prepare operationally for questions, not for a promised number of comments. Have the project available, know where relevant documentation lives, and identify which implementation details you can explain publicly.

Do not organize friends or colleagues to create favorable engagement. The useful preparation is your ability to respond, not a coordinated audience reaction.

Sort incoming feedback by what it needs. A reproducible failure needs details about the visitor’s environment and action. A design disagreement needs an explanation of the tradeoff. A request for an unsupported feature needs an accurate statement of current scope.

Keep private information out of the discussion. If troubleshooting would expose a real file or account detail, offer an appropriate private route without pressuring the person to share it.

When you do not know an answer, say so. Do not promise a fix before investigating or imply that one person’s preference represents the whole audience. A clear acknowledgment of a limit can make the technical discussion more useful than a defensive argument.

## Decide readiness from evidence, not launch momentum

Before submitting, review three gates. Can a new visitor reach the result? Can they understand its limitations? Can the maker discuss the work personally?

If the first gate fails, repair the path. If the second fails, improve the explanation and example. If the third fails, schedule the launch when the actual maker can participate. None of these decisions requires predicting traffic.

Preserve a small readiness note with the tested destination, environment, sample, result, and unresolved issue. This is an internal work product, not a fabricated public endorsement or a demand estimate.

After the discussion, separate observed problems from suggested features and unsupported interpretations. A [founder customer-update video](/blog/founder-customer-update-video) may later explain a meaningful change to customers, but it is a different job from the Show HN conversation.
