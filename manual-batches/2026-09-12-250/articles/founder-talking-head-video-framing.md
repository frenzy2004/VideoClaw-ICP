Frame a founder video around what viewers need to see: your expression, any meaningful gesture, and enough context to understand the setting. Test a seated update, standing statement, or interview layout as appropriate. Check the saved frame while you move, and remove background distractions before recording the full message for customers or the press.

## Let the communication job determine the crop

A talking-head frame is not simply the widest picture your camera can capture. [Wistia describes the format as focusing on the speaker, often from the chest upward](https://wistia.com/blog/talking-head-video). That is a useful starting point, but the right crop depends on what your message asks the viewer to notice.

For a customer update, your face may carry most of the communication. For an explanation involving a physical sample, the object and your hands need room. For a cofounder interview, the viewer should understand the relationship between the speakers.

Write a frame brief in one sentence: “The viewer should see my expression and the notebook when I explain how we made the decision.” Anything else in the picture must justify its place.

Do not solve framing by asking the founder to remain unnaturally still. If the shot only works when you freeze, it is not ready for ordinary delivery. Build the composition around how you actually speak.

## Sketch three useful layouts before choosing one

Draw a rectangle for each relevant layout. Label where the face, hands, and background elements sit. These are original planning sketches, not mandatory camera rules.

For a seated update, place the face as the clear focal point and leave enough lower-frame space for the gestures you normally make. Mark the chair back and desk edge if they appear. Ask whether either creates an awkward visual interruption.

For a standing statement, allow room for your normal shift in posture. If your hands matter to the explanation, include their resting position and expected movement. Mark a stable standing spot so you do not gradually leave the usable area.

For an interview, draw the speaker's gaze direction and leave a comfortable relationship between the face and the conversational space. If both founders share one frame, check their relative size and whether each has room to turn toward the other.

Choose the simplest layout that serves the message. A second camera angle is not required merely because your sketch has open space.

## Balance headroom with the founder's actual movement

Headroom is the visible space above your head. [Wistia's framing guidance warns against both a cramped top edge and excessive empty space](https://wistia.com/blog/look-good-on-camera). Instead of treating a fixed distance as a guarantee, test the whole delivery.

Record yourself sitting upright, relaxing into your normal posture, and making the gesture you use when explaining a contrast. Check whether the top of your head approaches the frame edge or whether you sink noticeably lower as you speak.

If your face becomes too small, decide whether to move the camera, change your position, or simplify what must fit in the frame. Make one change at a time and save another sample. Avoid adjusting only the preview window's size; that may not change the recorded composition.

Test with the actual recording orientation. A horizontal preview does not tell you whether the same arrangement will work for an original vertical recording.

The goal is visual breathing room that survives a real sentence, not an aesthetically precise still that fails as soon as you lean forward.

## Inspect the background as if you did not work there

Familiar objects become invisible to the person who sees them every day. Review the frame from corner to corner and ask what a new customer would notice first.

Check whiteboards, calendars, package labels, reflective surfaces, and open screens for information that should not be public. Move or cover private material before recording. Do not assume a later blur will reliably remove everything from a moving shot.

Look for objects that appear to emerge from your head or cut through your shoulders. A small shift in the chair or camera may solve the problem more cleanly than redesigning the room.

Then check implied meaning. A prominent customer logo can look like an endorsement. A trophy can suggest an award relevant to the message even when it is unrelated. Keep background context truthful and avoid relying on viewers to infer the correct explanation.

Finally, consider movement behind you. A doorway, shared corridor, or bright monitor may attract attention halfway through an otherwise usable take. Record long enough to observe the ordinary room, not only its quietest instant.

## Use a frame audit with explicit pass conditions

Here is a fictional example for a founder named Priya explaining why her team changed a support process. She wants viewers to see her face and a paper diagram, but her first sample crops the diagram whenever she raises it.

Her audit has four rows: face, hands and object, edges, and background. The face row passes because her expression remains clear. The object row fails because the label disappears. The edges row notes that she leans outside the crop when reaching for the paper. The background row flags an internal planning board.

Priya moves the paper within easy reach, tests a slightly wider composition, and removes the planning board from view. She does not add a decorative object to fill the newly visible space.

The second audit includes an important trade-off: her face is smaller, but the diagram remains readable at the intended viewing size. If the diagram still cannot be read, she will separate that explanation into a deliberate visual rather than hold it closer and block her face.

## Preserve the accepted frame through the recording

Save a reference still and note the camera position, chair or standing mark, recording orientation, and visible objects. If you record a pickup, compare it with that still before speaking.

Do one final movement test after changing clothing, adding notes, or adjusting the microphone. Each can change how you occupy the frame. A microphone that looks unobtrusive at rest may obscure a gesture; notes may make you lean toward an edge.

Review the actual saved video at an ordinary viewing size. Ask a colleague what drew their attention before explaining your intended focal point. If they mention a background object first, reconsider whether it belongs there.
