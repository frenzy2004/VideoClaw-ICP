Balance an investor demo's sound by deciding what the listener must hear at each moment, then lowering or removing competing audio around that priority. Narration is not always the foreground: a product response or alert may be the evidence. Use a sound-priority timeline and review the finished export on both headphones and ordinary speakers.

## Identify the sound that carries meaning

Start with the finished recording and list its audio sources. Common layers include founder narration, captured product sound, room noise and optional music. Separate them where the source project allows so you can change one without unnecessarily changing the others.

Classify each meaningful moment. Is the listener learning what to expect, hearing the product behave, or listening to an explanation of the result? The foreground can move between narration and product audio as the demonstration progresses.

Do not label every non-voice sound as background. A confirmation tone, spoken response or warning may be essential to understanding what happened. Lowering it automatically under a continuous voiceover could remove the evidence the investor came to inspect.

Also distinguish audible evidence from proof of a broader claim. A confirmation sound can show that the product emitted that sound; the accompanying screen or other evidence must establish what completed. Mixing should clarify the demonstrated event, not make a decorative cue stand in for an unobserved result.

## Create space before applying processing

Listen for collisions between narration and important product audio. The simplest repair may be to shorten an explanatory sentence or move it before the event, leaving the recorded product moment intact. More processing cannot always make two simultaneous messages easy to understand.

Do not slide a product sound away from its visible cause merely to create a convenient gap. If narration is an added explanatory layer, adjust that layer first. Preserve the relationship between the captured action, sound and result.

[Apple's iMovie guidance](https://support.apple.com/en-ae/guide/imovie/mov84788882d/mac) describes lowering other clips relative to a selected foreground clip. Use that as a mixing option, not a rule that the voiceover should always dominate. Select the foreground according to the demonstration's meaning.

Remove optional music during a decisive product event if it contributes nothing useful. A quieter mix can communicate more clearly than a dense one. Keep enough natural context that the recording does not suggest a different environment or product behavior.

## Mark a sound-priority timeline

The following fictional timeline covers an edited demonstration of a task submission and its confirmation. Times are illustrative edit positions, not product performance measurements.

| Edit interval | Foreground | Competing sound | Mixing decision | Listener must identify |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 00:00–00:06 | Founder introduces the task | Optional music | Keep music subordinate or omit it | The action about to be shown |
| 00:06–00:10 | Recorded interaction and confirmation tone | Narration tail | End the explanatory sentence before the meaningful tone | The tone and its visible event |
| 00:10–00:15 | Product's spoken confirmation | Music and room noise | Remove music; reduce avoidable noise without changing the response | The actual confirmation words |
| 00:15–00:22 | Founder explains the result | Residual product ambience | Restore narration priority after the response ends | The observed result and its limitation |

Add source clip references, planned level changes and review notes to the working timeline. The table tells an editor where priorities change; it does not prescribe one loudness value for every source.

For the confirmation interval, compare the product track alone with the full mix. If the important word is clear alone but obscured in the mix, address the competing layer. If it is unclear in the source, a different mix cannot honestly reconstruct missing speech.

## Use ducking and level changes selectively

Automatic ducking can create a starting point when one sound should yield to another. [Adobe's ducking documentation](https://helpx.adobe.com/uk/premiere/desktop/add-audio-effects/adjust-volume-and-levels/automatically-duck-audio.html) uses audio-type assignments and generated level keyframes that can be adjusted. Review those changes around the actual product events rather than accepting them as editorial decisions.

Check which track drives the ducking and which track is lowered. If a product response is mistakenly treated as disposable ambience, the automation may suppress it exactly when it matters. A technically working effect can still produce the wrong demonstration.

Listen to the transitions. Abrupt level changes may distract from the task, while a slow return can hide the beginning of the next explanation. Adjust the relevant points in the editor and compare the result with the intended priority timeline.

Watch for clipping and distortion in the recorded sources and final output. Lowering an already distorted recording does not restore the lost information. If repair would require replacing evidentiary sound, consider a new capture or an explicit explanatory treatment rather than presenting reconstructed audio as the original event.

## Review with specific listening tasks

Use headphones to inspect details and ordinary speakers to check the intended viewing experience. Keep playback at a comfortable level; do not compensate for a poor balance by continually increasing the listener's volume.

Ask a fresh reviewer to repeat the important product phrase, identify the confirmation tone and explain where narration resumes. “Sounds good” is not enough to tell whether the required information survived. Give the reviewer the task without telling them the words they should hear.

Compare the full mix with a version without optional music. If the music-free version is clearer and loses no meaningful context, keep the simpler treatment. The goal is understandable product evidence, not a soundtrack that demonstrates how many layers the editor can manage.

Check more than one point in the clip. A strong opening does not guarantee a quiet qualification near the end remains audible. Review the loudest interaction, the softest essential phrase and each transition between narrator and product.

## Preserve the meaning through the final export

Export a candidate and listen to the actual file from beginning to end. Do not approve the mix solely from the editing timeline, where track monitoring or temporary solo states may differ from the delivered output.

Confirm that the intended channels are present and that no test mute remains active. Recheck the audio alongside the picture so the product sound still aligns with its visible event. If the venue has a stated delivery specification, verify it separately without substituting an invented universal target.

Keep the sound-priority sheet with the approved edit. It explains why certain moments are quiet and which sounds must not be removed during a later shorter export. A future editor should not have to guess which background-looking track carried the product evidence.

This job ends with a clear prerecorded mix, not a live meeting audio setup. The useful result lets investors distinguish explanation from actual product sound, hear each at the right moment and inspect the same behavior the picture shows.
