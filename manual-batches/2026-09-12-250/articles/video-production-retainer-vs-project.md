Choose a video retainer when you can use its specific allocation regularly and value the capacity it actually reserves. Choose project buying when work is intermittent, uncertain, or unusually varied. Compare both against the same realistic workload, including quiet months, rollover restrictions, and any tasks excluded from the monthly agreement.

## Find out what the monthly payment purchases

The word retainer does not tell you enough about the commercial arrangement. One offer may reserve hours. Another may include a list of deliverables. Another may provide a budget that is consumed by separately estimated jobs. Start by naming the purchased unit.

Ask whether payment reserves a person, a place in a queue, particular shoot dates, or simply the right to submit requests. Those are different forms of access. If availability is the main reason you want a retainer, a discount on future work will not necessarily solve your problem.

Vidico describes a monthly arrangement that includes production services and allows unused budget to roll forward. That is one provider's stated structure, not a definition that applies to every retainer. [Vidico's retainer description](https://vidico.com/news/video-retainer-packages/).

Write the unit and its boundaries at the top of the proposal comparison. Include who estimates its consumption, who approves that estimate, and what happens when a job exceeds the remaining allocation. Without those answers, you cannot tell what an apparently predictable monthly payment means.

## Build the workload from committed inputs

List the next several months of work using projects that have an owner, available source material, and a plausible review date. Keep aspirational ideas in a separate column. A long list of desired videos does not establish that production can begin.

For each job, record the expected type of work and the inputs you must supply. An editor cannot spend reserved capacity productively on an interview that has not been recorded. A studio cannot make a planned shoot happen if the participant is unavailable.

Mark the difference between recurring base work and exceptional commissions. Regular interview edits may fit a stable package while a complex location film requires separate staffing and preparation. You do not need the same buying structure for both.

Ask the proposed partner to translate this workload into its purchased unit. If the offer uses hours, request estimates against the listed jobs. If it uses deliverables, ask which jobs qualify. If it uses a monthly budget, request how estimates, approvals, and balances will be shown. Keep this translation attached to the proposal.

## Run the six-month commitment sheet

Use a worksheet that exposes timing as well as quantity. Enter the actual contract terms; do not assume unused allocation survives or that an accumulated balance can be spent immediately.

| Month | Ready work | Included allocation used | Unused balance | Work outside scope | Decision needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | Confirmed source-ready jobs | Vendor estimate | Apply actual rollover rule | Separate estimate | Start or defer |
| Second | Confirmed and probable jobs | Vendor estimate | Check any cap | Specialist needs | Reserve extra capacity |
| Third | Quiet-period scenario | Vendor estimate | Check expiry | None unless known | Reduce or pause if allowed |
| Fourth | Busy-period scenario | Vendor estimate | Check usable balance | Overflow jobs | Confirm simultaneous slots |
| Fifth | Updated planned work | Vendor estimate | Reconcile statement | New requirements | Amend scope |
| Sixth | Renewal-period work | Vendor estimate | Check final treatment | Exit needs | Renew or conclude |

The month labels are planning positions, not prescribed contract periods. Extend or shorten the sheet to match the agreement.

Look for a pattern: repeated unused allocation, recurring overages, or a large balance that expires before the team can supply work. Those conditions suggest the proposed package needs adjustment. They do not automatically mean retainers are unsuitable; the allocation or scope may simply be wrong.

## Stress-test quiet months and urgent peaks

Create three versions of the sheet: expected workload, a quiet period caused by missing inputs, and a busy period with overlapping deadlines. Hold the contract terms constant so you can see how the arrangement behaves.

In a fictional example, a company expects four routine edits in one month and none in the next because its subject-matter expert is unavailable. A plan with non-expiring budget might preserve spending power. It still needs enough available editing time when work resumes. Ask both questions.

Video Husky's public offer distinguishes unlimited submissions from an editor working on one video at a time. That illustrates why request allowance and simultaneous capacity need separate examination. [Video Husky's description of its queue](https://www.videohusky.com/).

For urgent peaks, identify what the agreement actually promises. Does the vendor hold extra capacity? Can you buy an additional slot? Will a specialist join, or will your existing queue simply be reprioritized? Ask what happens to already scheduled work when urgency increases. A useful answer names the tradeoff and the approving person.

## Compare cash exposure without invented savings

Place the actual retainer proposal beside actual project estimates for the same eligible work. Compare committed cash over the period, likely additional purchases, and any recoverable or expiring balance. Keep internal preparation and review effort visible as a separate estimate.

If you use an illustrative calculation internally, label it as fictional and replace its assumptions before deciding. The useful relationship is straightforward: compare the commitment you cannot avoid with the work you can realistically get completed under that commitment.

Do not divide the monthly fee by a vendor's maximum possible output unless your workload and schedule support that output. A theoretical unit price can look attractive while concealing unused capacity. Equally, project buying can carry coordination or availability costs that are not obvious in the production fee.

Include the cost of changing course. Record minimum term, notice period, pause rights, treatment of unfinished jobs, and what happens to unused balances. Ask for unclear points in writing. You are comparing the flexibility of two purchases, not trying to establish a universal market price for video.

## Choose the smallest useful commitment

A retainer is a reasonable choice when recurring work fits the scope, inputs arrive reliably, and the agreement gives you useful access or continuity. Project buying is reasonable when work changes materially, arrives irregularly, or requires different specialists each time.

A mixed arrangement can be practical. Reserve a stable editing workload and commission unusual production separately. Define which contract covers each job before work begins, especially when a project includes both ordinary edits and custom animation or filming.

If the relationship is new, consider a bounded first period or a representative project that tests the assumptions behind the larger commitment. Agree the decision point in advance. The evidence you need is whether the allocated service can complete your actual work with an acceptable amount of internal effort.

Finish with a purchase note naming the chosen structure, the workload it covers, the assumptions that would change the decision, and the next review date. That gives both sides a clear basis for adjusting the arrangement when the business changes.
