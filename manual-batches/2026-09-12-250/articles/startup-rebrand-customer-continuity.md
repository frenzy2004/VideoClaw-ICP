Announce a startup rebrand by connecting the old and new names immediately, then explaining what customers will notice and what has been verified to continue. Give people a trusted way to recognize the company and find current instructions. The announcement should reduce uncertainty about an existing relationship before asking anyone to admire the new identity.

## Treat recognition as the first customer job

Your team has watched the new identity develop for weeks or months. Customers have not. A new logo, unfamiliar sender name, or changed login page can look like a different company, even when the relationship continues.

Open with a direct bridge: “Old Name is becoming New Name.” Follow with a plain explanation of the relationship. Do not make the reader infer continuity from a founder's face, a familiar color, or a celebratory headline.

[Kit's rebrand series visibly connects its current identity with its former ConvertKit name](https://kit.com/rebranding-in-public). That is a first-party communication example, not evidence that a particular announcement format guarantees recognition.

Write down the places where customers encounter your identity: product interface, email sender, support replies, invoices, documents, and public profiles. The announcement must prepare people for the changes they will actually see.

If the change also involves ownership, contracts, or a different service, do not describe it as a cosmetic rebrand. Explain those separate facts with appropriate review.

## Build a recognition bridge before drafting the story

Create a worksheet with six fields: old name, new name, reason, customer-visible changes, verified continuity, and trusted destinations. Each field should have an owner who can confirm it.

The reason field should be understandable without your internal brand strategy. “The new name reflects the review workflow we now focus on” is clearer than “We are unlocking our next identity.” Keep the reason proportionate to the actual change.

The visible-changes field might include a product header, sender display name, or documentation title. The continuity field might include existing account access or current support arrangements, but only after those have been checked.

[Kit's written rebrand explanation separates what changes from what does not](https://kit.com/resources/blog/rebranding-in-public-why-we-are-becoming-kit). Use that distinction as a prompt for your own verification, not as permission to copy another company's assurances.

Add a final question beneath the worksheet: “What might a customer wrongly assume from this announcement?” That answer often reveals the missing sentence.

## Verify continuity instead of using blanket reassurance

“Nothing changes for you” is a broad claim. It can be false even when the underlying service remains the same. A customer may need to recognize a new invoice label or update an approved vendor record.

Review continuity by customer task. Can they sign in through their usual route? Will existing documents remain valid? Will support messages come from the same address? Does the change affect an integration or a bookmarked help page? Record the answer and the evidence owner.

If you cannot confirm a point, do not bury it under reassurance. Resolve it before announcing or state that specific instructions will follow. Customers should not be forced to discover the exception during their normal work.

Keep operational and legal questions distinct from the founder's narrative. A communications worksheet does not replace checking agreements, payment records, or domain transitions with the responsible specialists.

The objective is not to list every internal implementation detail. It is to identify the details that change how a customer recognizes or uses the relationship.

## Write an announcement around likely confusion

Here is an original fictional example for a company changing from Draftline to Reviewlane:

“Draftline is becoming Reviewlane. The new name reflects our focus on helping teams review work before approval. You will begin seeing Reviewlane in the product header and our public materials. Your current workspace remains available through the route described in the account notice. Please read that notice for the exact timing and any organization-specific instructions.”

This example intentionally avoids claiming that pricing, agreements, or every access route remain unchanged. Those facts would need separate verification in a real announcement.

Annotate each sentence with its job. The first establishes recognition. The second explains the reason. The third previews visible change. The fourth directs customers to verified instructions. The fifth preserves the boundary between a general announcement and account-specific detail.

Then add the precise continuity statements your team has actually confirmed. Replace vague language such as “business as usual” with the relevant fact.

Read the announcement as a customer who sees only the first paragraph. They should know which familiar company is speaking and why the message matters.

## Use a founder video to explain, not to replace instructions

A short founder recording can make the relationship between the identities explicit. Begin with both names spoken aloud and shown clearly. Explain the reason in ordinary language, then address the most important customer concern.

Keep the visual transition simple. If the old mark disappears before the new one appears, the viewer may miss the bridge. Show enough overlap for recognition without turning the message into a long design reveal.

Do not force exact operational instructions into fleeting on-screen text. Put verified destinations, dates, and account-specific directions in a written note beside the video. The founder can explain where to find it.

Record sensitive continuity wording from approved text. An improvised “everything is the same” can undo the care taken in the written announcement.

Before release, ask someone outside the rebrand project to watch. Ask what company they think sent the message, what they expect to change, and whether they need to do anything. Their answers are more useful than asking whether they like the new name.

## Coordinate the recognition period and follow-up

Assign someone to check the customer-facing surfaces after the announcement. A support page using the old name may be acceptable during a transition if it explains the relationship; an unexplained mixture can create confusion.

Keep a dated source of truth for the rebrand and link to it from familiar channels. Do not ask customers to trust an unfamiliar destination solely because a new email says so. Give them a way to verify the announcement through an established route.

Collect recurring questions and improve the explanation. A question about invoices is not merely resistance to change; it may reveal a practical gap. Respond with a verified fact, not more brand rationale.

When the transition settles, review whether the old-name bridge is still needed in important discovery points. Make that decision based on actual customer confusion and context, not an arbitrary desire to erase the old identity.
