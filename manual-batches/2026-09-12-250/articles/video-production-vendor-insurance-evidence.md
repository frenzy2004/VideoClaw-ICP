Verify a production vendor's insurance by matching the actual location and equipment requirements to the insured entity, filming dates, activities, and requested endorsements. Do not diagnose coverage from a certificate alone. Ask the broker or insurer to resolve discrepancies, and obtain the requesting party's acceptance before relying on the paperwork for access.

## Collect the actual requirements before asking for a certificate

Request the written insurance requirements from the location, permit office, equipment rental company, and any other party making a specific demand. Record who issued each request and which booking or activity it concerns.

A generic “please send your insurance” email is not enough when a venue expects particular entities, dates, or supporting documents. Ask for the current requirement sheet and the person authorized to review the evidence.

The [California Film Commission's state-property requirements](https://film.ca.gov/state-permits/insurance-requirements/) distinguish a certificate from a separate additional-insured endorsement and add requirements for certain activities. That is a jurisdiction-specific example of why a single generic document may not complete a filming application.

Do not copy California's limits or wording into an unrelated production as a national rule. Use the actual recipient's requirements and ask an appropriately qualified insurance professional to help reconcile them.

## Match the vendor, project, and activities

Identify the legal entity contracting to provide the production service and compare it with the insured name on the evidence. If the supplier uses a trading name or another entity, ask for the relationship to be explained.

Record the filming location, preparation and shoot dates, equipment use, vehicles, aerial work, and other relevant activities. Ask the broker which details are needed to assess the proposed arrangement.

Do not assume that a camera operator's policy automatically addresses the production company's obligations or the client's separate responsibilities. Ask who is expected to provide each category of evidence.

If the shoot changes, update the facts. A new location, added drone operation, extended rental period, or additional filming day may require another review. The administrative task is to keep the evidence aligned with the actual work, not to preserve an outdated certificate because it was previously accepted.

## Use a requirement-to-evidence reconciliation sheet

This original worksheet organizes questions for the vendor, broker, and requesting party. It is not a coverage opinion.

| Requirement | Evidence received | Check to perform | Unresolved owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Named production entity | Insured name and related explanation | Does it match the contracted arrangement? | Vendor and broker |
| Location or rental recipient | Certificate-holder details | Are the requested details accurate? | Requesting party |
| Filming period | Effective and expiry dates | Does the relevant period appear addressed? | Broker |
| Required activities | Description and policy information | Are vehicles, drones or special operations raised? | Broker or insurer |
| Additional-insured request | Relevant endorsement if required | Does the recipient accept the supplied document? | Broker and recipient |
| Equipment requirements | Requested evidence or confirmation | Are rented items and responsibilities addressed? | Rental company and broker |
| Remaining gaps | Written questions and answers | What must be resolved before access? | Named decision-maker |

Use “received,” “question outstanding,” and “recipient accepted” as distinct statuses. A document being present in the folder is not the same as its meaning being confirmed.

Avoid collecting unnecessary personal details. Keep insurance records in the agreed business location with access appropriate to the transaction.

## Understand what a certificate can and cannot establish

A certificate is evidence to review, not a substitute for the underlying coverage terms. [Washington's certificate rule](https://app.leg.wa.gov/Wac/default.aspx?cite=284-30-355), within its stated scope, says a certificate does not confer additional rights beyond the referenced policy.

That official example supports a practical caution: do not ask someone to make a certificate “say we are covered” if the policy or endorsement does not support it. Ask the broker or insurer what documentation or change is actually needed.

Keep certificate holder and additional-insured requests separate in the conversation. If a recipient requires an endorsement, ask for the relevant evidence rather than assuming a name typed into a certificate resolves the requirement.

Do not provide your own conclusion about exclusions, limits, deductibles, or whether a particular loss would be paid. Those questions need the policy, facts, and appropriate professional review. This guide helps the buyer avoid missing questions before the shoot.

## Resolve gaps before nonrecoverable commitments grow

Send discrepancies to the supplier and broker early enough for a response. Identify the exact request, supplied document, and mismatch rather than forwarding a vague “insurance issue.”

Confirm who is authorized to communicate with the broker and whether the business may verify documents directly through an established contact. If authenticity is uncertain, use a contact route independently confirmed with the insurer or broker rather than relying solely on details in an unexpected attachment.

Ask the location or rental company whether the revised evidence satisfies its requirement. Keep the written acceptance with the booking record, while recognizing that recipient acceptance is not a guarantee about every possible claim.

If the required evidence cannot be obtained, discuss alternatives: a different vendor arrangement, revised activity, another location, or postponement. Obtain actual quotes and contractual consequences before changing the production plan.

Do not assume insurance will reimburse cancellation, lost footage, business disappointment, or every equipment problem. Ask those questions separately if they matter to the purchase. Avoid making a buying decision based on the broad phrase “fully insured.”

## Keep a concise production-ready evidence packet

The final packet should contain the written requirements, relevant certificate and endorsements, broker or insurer responses, recipient acceptance, and a summary of unresolved issues. Name the person responsible for keeping it current.

Before filming, confirm that the schedule, entity, location, and activities still match the reviewed facts. If a policy period changes or a supplier is replaced, ask for updated evidence rather than silently carrying forward the old packet.

Make the relevant contact information available to the production lead without distributing sensitive documents more broadly than necessary. If a problem occurs, follow the actual reporting process and seek professional guidance.

The buying decision is ready when the requesting parties have the evidence they require and material uncertainties have been addressed by the right people. That is more defensible than relying on a reassuring certificate filename or a vendor's unsupported promise that every aspect of the shoot is covered.
