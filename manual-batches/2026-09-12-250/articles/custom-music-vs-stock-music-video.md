Hire a composer when a tested stock track cannot satisfy important timing, identity, or structural needs within the available edit. First identify the exact mismatch. Then compare a custom treatment against the usable library option, specifying musical cues, dialogue space, alternate mixes, delivery files, and rights questions before agreeing to the commission.

## Prove that the library option has a real gap

Custom music should solve a problem you can hear and describe. “We want something premium” does not tell a composer what to create or help a buyer compare the result with a library track.

Test one or two plausible stock candidates beneath the actual dialogue and picture. Note where a vocal competes with narration, a build arrives too early, an ending feels abrupt, or the instrumentation changes the meaning of the scene.

Do not assume stock music is an unchangeable stereo file. [Epidemic Sound's agency offering](https://www.epidemicsound.com/business/agencies/) advertises stems, instrumentals, and tools for adjusting structure. Those are provider-described options to investigate, not a promise that every track or plan meets your requirements.

Ask the editor what can reasonably be accomplished with the candidate and permitted materials. If a straightforward alternate mix solves the issue, commissioning a new score may add work without adding useful control. If the mismatch survives a fair test, document it as the reason to request a composer treatment.

## Translate mood words into timed musical jobs

A composer needs more than a playlist of references. Describe what the audience should feel at specific points and how the music should interact with speech and action.

For example, the opening may need restrained motion while a problem is explained. A product reveal may need a lift without sounding triumphant. The final spoken sentence may require an unobtrusive bed followed by a clear, brief resolution.

Use references to explain qualities such as texture, density, pace, or warmth, not to request a close copy of another artist's composition. Tell the composer which characteristic matters and which does not.

Mark where the picture is locked and where timing may still change. A tightly synchronized score ordered against an unstable cut creates a different revision burden from music designed to remain flexible. Let the composer price that uncertainty explicitly rather than discovering it after recording.

## Prepare the composer commission sheet

This original example is a proposed briefing tool. Its times and creative directions are illustrative, not a universal scoring format.

| Time or section | Musical job | Constraint | Requested material |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening 0–12 seconds | Establish quiet forward movement | Narration begins immediately | Sparse version without lead melody |
| Transition 12–24 seconds | Build anticipation | No sudden accent over product name | Adjustable transition or alternate cue |
| Reveal near 24 seconds | Add energy without overpowering speech | Picture timing may move slightly | Treatment showing a flexible arrival |
| Closing sentence | Leave space for the final claim | Voice must remain intelligible | Reduced instrumentation mix |
| End card | Resolve cleanly at agreed duration | No abrupt fade from a long track | Exact ending plus agreed shorter ending |

Add intended duration, delivery date, picture version, reference notes, and the person who can approve musical direction. List the stems you actually need rather than requesting every possible file by habit.

Ask for a short description or demo showing the proposed direction before authorizing the full arrangement. Define whether that stage is paid, what you may do with the demo, and what decision it is supposed to unlock.

## Compare creative fit, not just custom versus stock

Listen to the library candidate and composer treatment in the same context and at comparable perceived levels. A louder demo can seem more impressive without improving the film.

Use a short ballot with observable prompts: Is the dialogue clear? Does the transition arrive at the required moment? Does the ending feel complete? Does the music convey the intended tone without implying something the business does not want to say?

Record the timecode and reason for each concern. “Too corporate” is less useful than “the brass accent makes this modest process improvement feel like a victory announcement.” The second comment gives the composer a solvable problem.

Limit decision-makers and identify one person who consolidates feedback. If stakeholders disagree about the emotional direction, resolve that disagreement before asking for another complete version. Otherwise a supplier may be charged with fixing an undecided brief rather than improving the work.

## Ask about rights and contributors separately

Newly composed music is not automatically an unrestricted asset owned by the buyer. The [U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/engage/musicians/) distinguishes the underlying musical work from its sound recording, which may have different ownership and licensing arrangements.

Ask the composer to explain the proposed rights for both, including intended placements, future edits, duration, territories, exclusivity, and who else contributes to the recording. These are fact-gathering questions for the agreement, not a substitute for qualified legal advice.

If the buyer wants exclusive use, define what that means in the actual transaction. Ask whether the composer may reuse elements, present the piece in a portfolio, or license the recording elsewhere. Do not assume that the word “bespoke” resolves those issues.

Also ask about any pre-existing material, samples, performers, or third-party components. Request a clear statement of what the supplier can grant and what remains subject to another permission. Resolve uncertainty before treating the finished music as a reusable company asset.

## Order an acceptance-ready delivery package

The purchase order should identify the approved composition stage, full mix, requested stems, alternate versions, file formats, and correction process. Have the editor confirm the technical requirements before the composer prepares final exports.

Define the distinction between correcting a delivery problem and changing the approved creative direction. A missing agreed stem is different from replacing the instrumentation after final approval. Request the supplier's actual revision terms and quote; there is no universal number of included revisions or standard custom-music premium.

Check the final package in the real video. Play the ending, every dialogue transition, and any alternate mix you ordered. Confirm that filenames distinguish versions and that the rights record corresponds to the delivered recording.

Choose stock when the tested material already serves the film under an appropriate license. Choose custom when the commission offers valuable control over a demonstrated need and the schedule, deliverables, and agreement support that control. The label matters less than whether the purchased music works precisely where the film needs it.
