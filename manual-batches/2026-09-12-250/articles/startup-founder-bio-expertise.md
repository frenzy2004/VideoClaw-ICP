Write a founder bio by naming your current role, explaining the relevant experience behind your work, and selecting only the details the audience needs. Use concrete facts instead of broad prestige claims. Keep several lengths based on the same factual record so journalists, hosts, and organizers can introduce you accurately.

## Decide what the biography needs to establish

A biography introduces the person behind an idea. It does not need to explain the company’s entire history or reproduce the funding announcement.

Start with the context in which it will appear. A conference audience may need to know why you can teach a particular lesson. A journalist may need a concise description of your role and expertise. A podcast host may want enough background to introduce the conversation naturally.

Write the question the biography should answer: “Why is this person useful to hear from on this subject?” That question provides a practical filter for every credential and anecdote.

[Sessionize’s speaker guidance](https://sessionize.com/playbook/submit-your-session-for-an-event) recommends connecting knowledge and experience to the event. Apply the same idea to the specific editorial setting, rather than treating one biography as equally appropriate everywhere.

For a founder video, the spoken introduction can be shorter still. The viewer usually needs a name, a role, and a reason the speaker understands the problem.

## Make a factual inventory before writing

List the facts you could include without deciding yet which belong in the finished version.

Useful categories include current role, previous relevant work, areas of direct responsibility, published work, and experience with the problem being discussed. Keep a source or internal verification note for facts that might otherwise drift between versions.

Separate company facts from personal expertise. Raising funding is a company event. Designing a particular workflow, conducting customer research, or managing an operational process may explain the founder’s relevant experience.

[Qwoted’s guidance on expertise](https://www.qwoted.com/am-i-an-expert-how-to-know-if-you-should-pitch-an-opportunity/) emphasizes relevant, verifiable knowledge and honest limits. A biography should help the audience understand that scope.

Avoid vague phrases such as visionary leader, serial innovator, or renowned expert unless a precise and supportable meaning follows. Often the stronger sentence simply names the work.

If a credential is sensitive or easy to misread, confirm its exact wording. Participation in a program, advising a project, and employment by an organization are different relationships.

## Use a credential-selection sieve

Apply this original worksheet to each possible detail.

| Possible detail | Does it explain relevant expertise? | Can it be verified? | Does this audience need it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current founder role | Usually, as context | Confirm current title | Usually |
| Previous operational work | Yes, if connected to the topic | Confirm role and responsibilities | Often |
| An unrelated award | Possibly little | Check exact award | Usually not |
| Funding round | Company context, not personal expertise | Confirm approved facts | Sometimes |
| A specific method developed | Potentially strong | Explain the actual contribution | Often |
| A personal interest | Human context | Usually straightforward | Only when it adds something useful |

The worksheet does not rank people by prestige. It selects information for a particular introduction.

A founder speaking about contractor coordination might include years spent managing repair work. The same experience could be less relevant to a discussion of subscription billing. Change the selection, not the truth.

Keep a small reserve of optional details. If an organizer requests a longer version, expand with relevant context rather than padding the short biography with adjectives.

## Build three versions from one fictional fact set

Use this complete fictional input inventory for Maya Rao: she founded and leads Fieldnote; she previously coordinated maintenance projects for a property operator and worked on contractor handoffs; that work sparked her interest in repair coordination; incomplete handoffs frequently raised responsibility and access questions in that fictional workplace. Her current team designs software for sharing a defined repair task without opening the entire internal workspace. Her proposed session teaches how to distinguish a handoff problem from a broader process issue. These are all invented inputs for the exercise, not real credentials or product claims. The biographies below select from this inventory without adding new facts.

**Short introduction**

> Maya Rao is the founder of Fieldnote, where she works on software for coordinating repair tasks between facilities teams and outside contractors.

**Program biography**

> Maya Rao is the founder of Fieldnote. Before starting the company, she coordinated maintenance projects for a property operator and worked with teams managing handoffs to outside contractors. Her current work focuses on how software can make those handoffs clearer while keeping access to internal information appropriately bounded.

**Extended speaker biography**

> Maya Rao is the founder of Fieldnote, a startup focused on repair coordination between facilities teams and outside contractors. Her interest in the problem began while coordinating maintenance projects for a property operator, where incomplete handoffs frequently created questions about responsibility and access. She now works with her team on the practical design choices behind sharing a defined task without opening an entire internal workspace. In this session, she will explain how to identify a handoff problem and distinguish it from a broader process issue.

The versions share a factual spine but serve different contexts. None claims that Maya is an industry leader or that the product produces a quantified outcome.

## Adapt the biography without changing its meaning

Read the biography aloud if someone will use it as an introduction. A sentence that looks concise on a page may be difficult to speak because it contains several unfamiliar terms or long job titles.

Choose a consistent point of view. Third person usually works for an organizer’s introduction. First person may suit a founder’s own author page or direct video message. Follow the destination’s requirements.

If you shorten the biography, preserve the words that limit the claim. Removing “worked on” from “worked on a research project” could falsely imply sole authorship. Removing a time qualifier could make a past role sound current.

Also distinguish speaking topics from qualifications. “Speaks about product strategy” describes an activity. “Led the customer research for a product change” describes relevant experience.

For video, let the introduction establish context quickly, then move to the viewer’s question. The biography should support the message rather than become a speech about the founder.

## Keep the submitted versions accurate

Maintain one factual reference and save the versions actually sent to organizers or editors. When a role changes, check the destinations that may still display the old wording.

Do not assume updating your main profile updates every event submission. Confirm the destination’s behavior and revise the specific submitted copy when needed.

Before sending a biography, verify the name, role, company description, and any dates or credentials. Ask a colleague whether the wording could imply a broader relationship or accomplishment than intended.

A useful final test is to remove every adjective. If the biography still explains why the audience should listen, the factual core is probably doing its job.
