Decide whether to record a private founder roundtable before inviting participants, based on the conversation's purpose and the sharing everyone expects. Agree separately on recording, attribution and later use. If recording would undermine the intended discussion, choose another way to capture actions rather than assuming a private meeting needs a reusable media asset.

## Choose the purpose before the capture method

A peer roundtable may exist to help founders discuss unresolved problems candidly. A teaching session may exist to create a reusable explanation. Both can be valuable, but they call for different capture choices.

Write the primary purpose in the invitation. “Compare approaches to hiring a first marketing lead” describes a conversation. “Record a lesson for the portfolio resource library” describes a content project. If you want both, explain how the session will separate them rather than revealing the recording plan after people arrive.

Ask what would be lost without a recording. If the essential output is a list of next actions or a shared reading recommendation, a recording may add little. If the purpose is an approved demonstration, a recording may be central. Choose the method that serves the actual outcome.

Do not treat the availability of an automatic recording button as a reason to use it. The platform's ability to capture a session says nothing about the participants' expectations.

## Distinguish three different decisions

The first decision is whether the session will be recorded. The second is whether information may be attributed to people or companies. The third is who may receive or publish the resulting material. These decisions should be explicit even when they lead to the same practical choice.

The [Chatham House Rule explanation](https://www.chathamhouse.org/about-us/chatham-house-rule) distinguishes non-attribution from off-the-record discussion. It also addresses indirect identification. Calling a meeting “under the Rule” therefore does not, by itself, establish a recording plan or permission to publish identifiable video.

For a roundtable, the organizer should state the intended capture and sharing arrangements in ordinary language. Participants should not need to interpret a slogan to understand whether their voices, faces or company examples will leave the room.

If the organization's policies or circumstances require specialist review, resolve that before the event. The facilitator's role is to communicate the agreed arrangement, not improvise legal consent requirements during the opening minute.

Keep the arrangement practical enough that the host can actually enforce the chosen mode.

## Use a facilitation card

The following is an original planning card. It describes operating choices, not legal categories or universal consent rules.

| Session mode | Intended discussion | Capture choice to agree | Sharing choice to agree |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| On-record teaching | Prepared explanation intended for reuse | Defined recording of the teaching segment | Named audience and review before release |
| Non-attributed peer discussion | Candid exchange without identifying contributors | Prefer a capture method consistent with that purpose | Any notes must respect the agreed non-attribution arrangement |
| Off-record problem discussion | Information not intended for publication | No reusable recording planned | Keep only the actions or records the participants have agreed |

A useful opening might say: “This is a private peer discussion. We are not creating a recording for the resource library. We will capture agreed action items, and anyone can ask us to pause before discussing a sensitive example.” Adapt the statement to what has actually been arranged.

For an on-record teaching segment, name what will be captured and the intended destination. Explain whether participant questions are included or whether the recording stops before open discussion.

Avoid a vague promise that everything is confidential while also planning to distribute the full recording to people who did not attend. Resolve that contradiction before the invitation is sent.

## Configure the meeting to match the agreement

Check the host's recording settings and any connected note-taking or transcription tools. A session can generate records through more than one application. The organizer should know which tools are active and who receives their outputs.

[Zoom's recording-consent documentation](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0059819) describes prompts presented when recording starts or a participant joins a recorded meeting. Those prompts are part of the meeting experience; they do not replace the organizer's explanation of intended later use.

Assign one person to operate recording and another, where feasible, to watch for questions about the session's mode. A facilitator absorbed in the discussion can miss an unexpected recording indicator or a late participant who has not heard the opening explanation.

Prepare a short message for late arrivals. Repeat the practical arrangement rather than assuming the original invitation was read. If the platform provides a visible session notice, keep it consistent with the spoken statement.

Do a brief rehearsal with harmless material. Confirm that stopping recording stops the intended capture and that outputs go to the correct controlled location.

## Handle a change of mode without ambiguity

A founder may raise a sensitive example during a recorded teaching session. Pause the discussion before the details are shared. Stop the relevant capture, confirm the new mode and make clear when the group is returning to the recorded portion.

Do not rely on “we can edit that out later” as the default response. Editing may be possible, but the material has already been captured and may exist in transcripts, notifications or other copies. Preventing an unintended record is a different task from correcting one.

In a fictional session, the first portion is a prepared explanation of an interview-planning method. During questions, a founder wants to discuss an unreleased customer dispute. The host stops the teaching recording, confirms that the discussion will not become library content and continues only within the agreed private-session arrangement.

If the host discovers unexpected capture, pause and explain what happened. Use the organization's established handling process and tell participants what is known and what still needs checking. Avoid declaring the material erased until the relevant copies and systems have been addressed.

## Close the session according to its chosen purpose

At the end, remind participants what will happen next. A teaching recording may go to its speaker for the agreed review. A private roundtable may produce only a list of volunteered actions or recommended resources. Do not create a public recap simply because the conversation was interesting.

For a non-recorded session, capture operational follow-up without reconstructing attributed remarks. “The platform will arrange a separate session on interview preparation” can be useful without naming who raised a sensitive concern.

Keep an event record of the agreed mode, capture operator, intended outputs and any changes during the session. That record helps the organizer answer later questions about what was promised. It should not become an unnecessary transcript of private content.

If someone later proposes turning a lesson into a shareable note, treat that as a separate editorial decision with its own authorization and identification review. It is not automatically covered by the original invitation.

The best recording decision preserves the value the participants came for. Sometimes that produces a useful media asset. Sometimes the successful outcome is a candid conversation that was never turned into content.
