Record a conference presentation by designing for a viewer who cannot interrupt or ask the room for help. Choose one task they should perform afterward, teach it through a concrete example, and include an exercise with a worked answer. Plan spoken transitions, accessible materials, and a complete test viewing before delivery.

## Turn the accepted promise into a learner task

Start with the session description the organizer accepted. Your recording should fulfill that promise, not become a funding update or a product pitch because the founder now has a camera and slides.

Replace “understand better decision-making” with an observable task. For the fictional talk in this guide, the task is: assign one accountable decision owner while distinguishing people who provide input from people who must approve a change.

The audience is small operations teams that repeatedly delay routine changes because responsibility is unclear. The founder has been accepted to teach a practical session about that problem, not to demonstrate software.

Write what the session will not cover: legal authority, employment policy, or a universal management framework. Those boundaries keep a useful example from becoming an overconfident prescription.

Confirm the organizer’s length, file format, slide, accessibility, and delivery requirements before recording. Do not assume requirements from a previous conference remain valid.

## Build a teaching timeline with an actual example

Here is an original twelve-minute teaching plan. The duration and scenario are illustrative planning choices, not performance benchmarks.

| Time | Teaching beat | What appears on screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–1:00 | Name the recurring decision problem and the learner task | A change request waiting without an owner |
| 1:00–3:00 | Distinguish input, approval, and accountability | Three roles with plain-language definitions |
| 3:00–5:30 | Work through one decision | A completed responsibility map |
| 5:30–7:30 | Ask the viewer to solve a related case | An incomplete map and a pause instruction |
| 7:30–10:00 | Explain a defensible answer and its limits | The filled map, with the changed condition highlighted |
| 10:00–12:00 | Apply the method to the viewer’s next decision | A short blank worksheet and a closing summary |

The example concerns changing a repair-notification schedule. The coordinator knows when the messages arrive; the operations manager owns the workflow; a system administrator can implement the change. The decision is who approves the new schedule, not who is most senior in the organization.

Teach the distinction before filling the map. Otherwise viewers may copy labels without understanding why the roles differ. Remove any slide that neither supports the task nor explains a necessary exception.

## Give the exercise enough information to solve

A recorded exercise needs a clear prompt, relevant facts, and an answer the viewer can inspect. “Discuss this with your table” does not work for someone watching alone after the event.

Use this fictional exercise:

> A facilities team wants to stop routine repair notifications after working hours. The operations manager is accountable for the repair workflow. The coordinator knows which messages interrupt the team. The system administrator can change the schedule but does not decide operational policy. Assign the decision owner, the input role, and the implementation role. Identify one condition you would check before approving the change.

Ask the viewer to pause and fill three rows. Keep the prompt visible long enough to read; do not immediately advance while saying “take a moment.”

The worked answer assigns the operations manager as decision owner, the coordinator as an input provider, and the administrator as implementer. Before approval, the team must check whether urgent repair notifications need different handling. That question follows from the example rather than importing an unsupported rule.

Explain that another organization may allocate authority differently. The transferable method is to identify the actual responsibility and constraint, not memorize these job titles. Show why “everyone approves” would leave the original coordination problem unresolved.

## Record transitions that replace the missing room

[Wistia’s presentation-video guidance](https://wistia.com/blog/presentation-videos) covers combining a speaker recording with presentation visuals. Choose the layout around what the viewer needs to inspect, rather than leaving your face over important slide content.

Use a brief spoken map at each transition: “We have assigned the owner; now we will test the exception.” This helps viewers reconnect after pausing or looking away. Avoid references such as “over here” when the destination is not named.

In the fictional talk, the founder appears at the opening and when explaining the judgment behind the answer. The responsibility map fills the screen while its labels are being read. The speaker returns for the closing application.

Record a short section and check the actual saved file before proceeding. Confirm that slide changes, pointer movements, and narration agree. If the pointer highlights the implementer while the founder says “decision owner,” fix the mismatch even when the audio sounds fluent.

For delivery preparation, [teleprompter versus outline choices](/blog/founder-video-teleprompter-vs-outline) can help separate exact definitions from more conversational explanations. Do not repeatedly restart a good section merely to remove every natural pause.

## Plan access to the spoken and visual teaching

The [W3C media-planning guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/planning/) addresses captions, transcripts, and access to essential visual information. Plan these elements before delivery rather than treating them as optional decoration after the talk is finished.

Read meaningful labels aloud when introducing the responsibility map. Explain the relationship that a diagram shows; a listener should not need to infer meaning from color alone. “The operations manager owns the decision” conveys more than “the green box goes here.”

Keep slide text readable in the actual playback layout. A detailed internal spreadsheet may need a simplified teaching version with only the relevant rows. Use fictional information consistently and remove private names, comments, and hidden notes.

Check captions against the final recording, including role names and negatives. “The administrator does not approve policy” becomes a materially different lesson if “not” disappears. Align the transcript and exercise with any last-minute changes to the answer.

Ask the organizer how accessible materials will accompany the recording. A usable file on your computer does not establish that attendees can find it in the conference player.

## Test the completed talk without coaching

Give the finished recording to someone unfamiliar with the example. Ask them to watch independently and complete the exercise. Do not explain the slide first or rescue them during a confusing transition.

Review their actual answer. If they name the right roles but cannot explain the urgent-notification exception, the teaching may need another connection between the rule and the case. If they mistake implementation for approval, revisit the definitions and the worked map.

Make a short repair list tied to observable confusion: enlarge the exercise prompt, name the highlighted role aloud, or add a sentence explaining the exception. “Be more engaging” is less actionable than a specific missing teaching step.

Confirm the final file plays from beginning to end and matches the promised session. Keep a copy of the accepted description and the submitted materials.
