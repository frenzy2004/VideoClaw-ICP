Resolve portfolio launch clashes by separating each company's fixed commitments from the fund's shared distribution choices. Put dates, audiences, dependencies and channel limits in one calendar, then negotiate movable items explicitly. A portfolio calendar coordinates support across companies; it should not imply that the fund controls when an independent company can announce its news.

## Distinguish a date conflict from a resource conflict

Two companies launching on the same day are not necessarily in conflict. Their audiences may differ, their channels may be independent and neither may require the same staff. Conversely, launches on different days can collide if both need the same editor or newsletter slot beforehand.

Record the shared element that creates the constraint. It might be the fund's weekly newsletter, an event host, a final-review window or a partner's social account. “Busy week” is not a sufficient reason to ask a company to move.

[Asana's marketing-calendar template](https://asana.com/templates/marketing-calendar) connects activities with dates, owners and channels. A portfolio version adds the distinction between company-owned commitments and fund-controlled support. That extra boundary makes the calendar a coordination tool instead of an accidental approval system.

Begin with the next set of confirmed launches and accepted support requests. Do not fill distant weeks with speculative plans and treat them as reservations. Mark tentative entries as tentative and identify what would make them firm.

## Ask what can move and what cannot

For each launch, identify the event that anchors its timing. A customer's approved announcement date, a conference presentation or a product becoming publicly available may create a genuine constraint. A founder's preferred Tuesday may be movable. Ask for the reason without demanding confidential detail that is unnecessary for scheduling.

Separate the main announcement from supporting activities. The company's news can go live on its agreed date while a fund interview appears later. A newsletter feature may remain useful after the initial announcement if it answers a durable audience question. Do not assume every supporting asset must publish simultaneously.

[a16z's account of its media work](https://a16z.com/what-is-new-media-in-2026/) distinguishes company support from the firm's owned distribution channels. That distinction is useful here: helping with a launch and allocating a fund channel are related decisions, but they are not identical.

Record each item's earliest acceptable date, latest useful date and prerequisites. If the company does not know, leave the uncertainty visible. A guessed deadline can become a commitment when copied into another team's calendar.

## Build a collision calendar

Use one row per shared-channel activity or support dependency, rather than one row per company. A company can have several rows, each with different flexibility.

The example below is fictional. It illustrates a fund with one featured newsletter position in a particular issue, not a recommended publishing frequency.

| Company activity | Desired timing | Fixed element | Movable element | Shared constraint |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alder customer announcement | Monday | Customer-approved news time | Fund interview publication | Editorial review |
| Beacon product release | Tuesday | Product availability | Newsletter feature date | Featured newsletter position |
| Cobalt conference appearance | Wednesday | Organizer schedule | Fund social recap | Event host and post review |
| Alder founder interview | Tuesday | None confirmed | Publication and promotion | Same newsletter position |

The collision is between Beacon's release feature and Alder's interview in the fund newsletter. Moving Cobalt's conference appearance would solve nothing. Asking Alder to change its customer's announcement would impose a large change on the wrong dependency.

A workable revision could retain all three company announcement dates, feature Beacon in the current newsletter and place Alder's durable interview in the following issue. Cobalt's recap can run when the approved event material arrives. This is one defensible example, not an automatic rule that product launches always win.

## Make the tradeoff visible to affected companies

Present the constraint and a small set of feasible alternatives. Explain what each option preserves and what it changes. Avoid asking a founder to accept a vague “later” slot without knowing whether the story will still serve its purpose.

For Alder, the options might be a short current-week mention plus a later interview, or a later standalone feature with more room for context. For Beacon, the fund could publish a timely short feature while linking to the company's full announcement. The companies decide whether those forms of support remain useful.

Do not promise extra reach to compensate for a changed date. Offer a different deliverable only when the team controls it and can perform it. “We can reserve this later issue” is a concrete proposal; “the later slot will perform better” is an unsupported prediction.

Record the agreed revision, the company contact who accepted it and the assets affected. If the decision changes an editor's work sequence, update that dependency too. A calendar change that never reaches the production queue will produce a second collision.

## Protect flexibility without hiding uncertainty

Use a small set of date states, such as tentative, company-confirmed, fund-confirmed and published. A company-confirmed launch date does not automatically mean the fund has accepted every requested placement. Keep the two confirmations distinguishable.

Review the calendar when a dependency changes. A missing customer quote, delayed product release or altered event program can affect several supporting items. Ask which dates genuinely need to move rather than shifting every row mechanically.

Set a clear point for revisiting tentative reservations. If a company cannot confirm its materials by the agreed checkpoint, release the shared slot with an explanation. The company should understand how to re-enter the schedule when ready.

Leave some room for unexpected corrections or genuinely time-sensitive opportunities if the team's workload allows it. Do not label all unused space as waste. A calendar with no capacity for change often forces the platform to break an existing commitment when something important happens.

Keep sensitive details in the appropriate company record. The shared calendar needs enough context to coordinate; it does not need confidential product specifics or the content of an embargoed announcement.

## Review collisions as operating problems

After the publishing window, examine whether the conflict was predictable. Did both companies request the same slot without knowing its limits? Did the platform accept two incompatible commitments? Did one late change propagate slowly?

Each cause suggests a different adjustment. Visible channel availability may prevent duplicate reservations. A clearer distinction between requesting and accepting a feature may prevent overpromising. A change-notification route may keep the calendar and production board aligned.

Record the outcome of the scheduling decision without claiming the calendar caused business results. Useful observations include whether the agreed assets were ready, whether the revised feature remained relevant and whether the companies understood the tradeoff.

Use a brief retrospective with the people affected. Ask what information would have made the negotiation easier. If every clash requires senior intervention, the shared-channel rules probably need clarification.

The final calendar should show a set of agreements the platform can honor. Its value lies in exposing dependencies early enough to negotiate them, while leaving independent companies in control of their own announcements.
