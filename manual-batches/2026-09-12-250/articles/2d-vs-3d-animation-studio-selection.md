Choose 2D or 3D animation according to what each scene must explain, not a general belief that one looks more professional. Use 3D when depth or spatial relationships materially help understanding. Compare the assets, specialist work, and future changes each treatment requires before commissioning a studio or approving its production schedule.

## Describe the information before the style

Write one sentence for each scene stating what the viewer should understand afterward. A scene may explain a sequence, compare options, reveal an internal mechanism, or show how physical parts fit together. These are communication jobs, not visual styles.

Then sketch the simplest possible representation. Boxes and arrows may explain a relationship clearly. A rotating object may be necessary to reveal a hidden connection. A character may need expressive performance that matters more than whether the image is flat or dimensional.

Avoid beginning with a reference film and asking a studio to reproduce its look without understanding its function. The reference can inform taste, but your subject may have different informational needs and production constraints.

Identify where accuracy matters. If you are showing a real product or process, provide the technical references the studio can use and name the person who can answer questions. An attractive invented mechanism is not a useful explanation of how your actual product works.

## Test whether depth earns its place

Ask what the viewer gains from a third dimension in each proposed scene. Does rotation reveal something hidden? Does a cutaway explain internal structure? Does movement through a space establish a relationship that a flat diagram would struggle to show?

Adobe describes 3D production as involving stages such as modeling, rigging, animation, lighting, and rendering. Those stages create capabilities and dependencies that belong in the buying discussion. [Adobe's overview of 3D animation production](https://www.adobe.com/uk/express/learn/blog/how-is-3d-animation-made).

Do not assume those stages make every 3D treatment more expensive or every 2D treatment simple. A detailed illustrated performance can demand substantial work, while an existing usable 3D asset may reduce preparation. Ask about the actual treatment.

For scenes that do not need depth, consider whether 2D can deliver the information more directly. This is not a rule against visual richness. It is a way to reserve complexity for places where it contributes to the explanation or to a deliberately chosen creative effect.

## Build a scene-to-medium routing board

Use a board that compares treatments at the scene level. It can lead to a fully 2D film, a fully 3D film, or a mixed commission.

| Scene job | Simplest useful representation | Reason to consider 3D | Supplier question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Show a sequence | Steps and transitions | Movement through a real space matters | What does depth make clearer? |
| Explain a connection | Diagram or highlighted relationship | The connection is hidden behind other parts | Is a cutaway or rotation necessary? |
| Compare choices | Side-by-side graphic | Physical dimensions are central | Which measurements must be accurate? |
| Show product appearance | Image or controlled render | Multiple angles are required | Is an approved model available? |
| Explain a character's reaction | Illustrated or dimensional performance | The treatment requires a particular spatial style | Who handles performance and revisions? |
| Summarize the result | Clear composition and labels | Depth adds a defined storytelling benefit | Can a simpler ending communicate as well? |

Mark the preferred treatment and the reason, then ask the studio to challenge it. A good proposal can identify a more efficient or clearer approach than your first sketch.

Do not decide only from still frames. Ask for a rough motion demonstration of the most important scene, because timing and movement are central to the purchase.

## Map the assets and people behind the treatment

Request a list of what must be created, adapted, or supplied. For a 3D proposal, that may include models, materials, rigs, lighting, and rendered elements. For 2D, it may include illustration, character elements, compositing, and animation setup.

Ask whether your existing files are genuinely usable. A product design file may need preparation for animation. A brand illustration may not contain the separated elements needed for movement. Let the supplier inspect representative assets before treating them as a production saving.

BLS describes animation work as involving specialized artists, teamwork, research, and revisions. That supports asking how the proposed studio will cover the actual disciplines in your treatment. [BLS animator duties](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/arts-and-design/multimedia-artists-and-animators.htm).

Identify who coordinates those contributions. If modeling and animation are handled by different people, ask where the handoff is checked. If a freelancer covers several disciplines, ask which parts are strongest and where additional support may be required. Buy the necessary coverage rather than assuming a software name establishes capability.

## Compare the cost of the next change

Choose a realistic future update and ask each supplier how it would be handled. Change a label, alter a product component, replace a character action, or add a new viewpoint. The answers reveal dependencies that an initial quote can hide.

In a fictional example, a company expects its device casing to change while the internal mechanism remains stable. A 3D model may support multiple views, but a geometry change could affect materials, animation, and rendering. A flat illustration may be easier for one fixed view while needing separate work for additional angles.

Ask which editable assets you receive and what rights or third-party dependencies apply. Owning a final video does not tell you whether another supplier can revise the underlying animation. Include the desired update path in the commission.

Use actual estimates for the initial treatment and the sample change. Avoid universal claims about 2D being faster or 3D being more engaging. The useful comparison is the specific explanation, quality level, asset base, and revision pattern your business expects.

## Approve a representative scene before scaling up

Select the scene that carries the hardest communication problem, not the easiest decorative moment. Ask for an agreed development step that establishes the visual treatment and motion before the studio produces the entire film.

Evaluate whether the scene makes the intended information clear. Check product accuracy, label readability, pacing, and the relationship between movement and narration. Separate factual corrections from stylistic preferences so the studio knows which decisions are essential.

Confirm the production sequence and the points at which changes become more involved. Ask what needs approval before further asset work or rendering proceeds. The supplier should explain the consequences in terms of the actual commission rather than presenting a generic prohibition on revisions.

Finish with a treatment decision that names the medium for each important scene, the assets to be created, the people responsible, and the expected update path. The right animation purchase is the one whose visual method and production requirements fit the explanation you need to deliver.
