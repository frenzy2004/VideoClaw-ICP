Write your first in-house editor's job description from the work that already needs doing, then separate core editing from filming, design, strategy, and publishing responsibilities. Name the manager and support available. Define observable first-month outcomes and screen for those tasks instead of advertising an unlimited creative role that one person cannot reliably perform.

## Inventory a real week of production demand

Review recent or planned commissions and list what the editor would receive, change, and deliver. Include source-footage volume, number of cameras, recurring graphics, typical review rounds, and the time needed to organize files.

Do not count only finished minutes. A short video assembled from disorganized recordings can involve substantial review and clarification, while a longer straightforward recording may require a different effort pattern.

The [Bureau of Labor Statistics occupation description](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/media-and-communication/film-and-video-editors-and-camera-operators.htm) distinguishes editing and organizing footage from camera work while describing collaboration among production roles. Use that distinction to examine your task list, not as a rigid limit on what any individual can learn.

Ask who currently performs each task and what would change after the hire. If nobody supplies a clear brief today, adding an editor does not automatically create that missing decision-maker.

## Separate core, occasional, and supported duties

Choose a small set of core responsibilities that explain why the job exists. These might include organizing supplied footage, assembling clear edits, maintaining audio intelligibility, applying established graphics, and preparing agreed exports.

List occasional responsibilities separately. If the editor will sometimes film a simple interview, state the expected conditions, available equipment, and support. Do not let “occasional” conceal a second full production role.

Use this original workload map before writing the vacancy.

| Work item | Frequency or trigger | Role treatment | Support needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Edit supplied interviews | Recurring | Core | Approved brief and usable recordings |
| Apply established titles | Recurring | Core if within skill scope | Maintained template and brand guidance |
| Create a new animated identity | Project-specific | Specialist support | Motion designer or separate commission |
| Record an office interview | Occasional if agreed | Explicit additional duty | Space, equipment and participant scheduling |
| Decide campaign strategy | Business planning | Separate owner | Marketing or founder direction |
| Publish and manage channels | Separate operating task | Include only if genuinely scoped | Access, approval and clear priorities |

Use actual workload observations where available. If the team has no history, label estimates as planning assumptions and revisit them after a representative assignment.

## Draft a vacancy package with clear boundaries

The following specimen is original and illustrative. Adapt it to the actual employment arrangement, local requirements, and approved hiring budget.

Role purpose: Turn supplied business footage into clear, accurate videos that match an agreed brief and remain easy for the team to revise.

Core work: Organize source material, assemble edits, improve routine picture and sound presentation, apply established titles, incorporate consolidated feedback, and deliver the specified files with a usable project handoff.

Collaboration: Work with a named manager who supplies priorities, confirms factual claims, and resolves conflicting stakeholder feedback. Coordinate with outside specialists when the brief requires new animation, complex sound repair, or filming beyond the role's agreed scope.

Boundaries: This opening does not automatically own campaign strategy, every shoot, original music, advanced animation, channel management, or sales performance. Any included additional responsibility must be named and prioritized.

Operating context: State the location or remote arrangement, expected collaboration hours, tools provided, reporting line, and the actual process for estimating work. Have the appropriate people review compensation disclosures and employment terms before publication.

A candidate should understand what success requires and what help is available, not merely recognize a list of fashionable software names.

## Define first-month outcomes the hire can control

Choose a few observable outcomes tied to onboarding and representative work. Examples include opening the existing project package, completing one agreed edit, documenting missing inputs, and delivering a revision that preserves the approved story.

Specify the manager's contribution to each outcome. The first edit cannot be assessed fairly if the hire never receives approved source material or the reviewer changes direction without explanation.

Avoid promising a universal number of videos per week before understanding the workload. Likewise, do not make views, leads, or revenue the editor's sole success criteria when distribution, offer, audience, and strategy sit elsewhere.

Use the first month to learn where the role is constrained. The result may reveal a need for better recording inputs, specialist support, or a different review cadence rather than a problem with the hire's editing ability.

## Screen for the actual assignment

Ask candidates to explain their contribution to relevant samples. A polished portfolio film may involve a large team, so identify which decisions and craft work the applicant performed.

[Upwork's editor-hiring guide](https://www.upwork.com/hire/video-editors/how-to-hire/) emphasizes relevant portfolio fit and communication. Those are useful screening dimensions even when the eventual arrangement is an in-house role rather than a marketplace project.

Use a short screening form: describe a similar edit, explain how you handle unclear feedback, identify a dependency in this sample brief, and state which listed duties require outside support. Honest boundaries can be more informative than a claim to do everything.

If a practical test is warranted, make it bounded and paid. Use authorized material, explain the decision being evaluated, and do not request a complete publishable production as speculative free work.

## Check the vacancy against the manager's capacity

Before posting, ask the manager to walk through a busy week. Who prioritizes competing requests? Who supplies missing assets? Who resolves factual questions? Who approves final delivery?

If every answer is “the editor,” revisit the scope. Production judgment can be part of the role, but it does not replace business authority or unlimited specialist capacity.

Compare the job description with the approved staffing decision and budget. Add actual compensation and benefits through the appropriate hiring process rather than importing unsupported salary benchmarks into the description.

After hiring, review the role when responsibilities materially change. If filming, strategy, or channel operations become recurring requirements, discuss workload, support, and the employment arrangement rather than allowing the job to expand silently.

A realistic vacancy is a useful agreement about work. It attracts candidates who can succeed in the actual environment and gives the first editor a clear path to producing reliable results with the support the business has promised.
