Write a founder conference proposal by choosing one audience problem, defining what attendees should learn, and showing how your experience supports the lesson. Give organizers a clear abstract, concrete takeaways, and a session structure. Treat the proposal as an offer to teach, rather than a request for time to promote the company.

## Choose a lesson before choosing a title

Start with a decision your audience encounters. A founder may know a great deal about building a product, but a conference session cannot teach everything learned along the way.

Pick one situation with a recognizable beginning and a useful ending. For example, an audience of early product leaders might need to decide when a pilot request changes the purpose of the engagement. The lesson could help them separate essential learning from unrelated expansion.

Write down what attendees are likely to know already. If they have run several pilots, basic definitions may waste their time. If they are new to the task, an advanced discussion of contract exceptions may leave them behind.

Also identify what the session will leave out. This gives the proposal a realistic shape. You can teach a decision method without promising a complete guide to sales, product management, and customer success.

The title comes later, once the actual lesson is clear.

## Match the proposal to the event

Read the current call for speakers and look at the event’s audience, themes, formats, and submission requirements. Do not treat a broad industry match as sufficient.

[Sessionize’s submission guidance](https://sessionize.com/playbook/submit-your-session-for-an-event) emphasizes a relevant session description and speaker experience. For a founder, the connection should be visible: what have you done that equips you to explain this particular problem?

Distinguish format from topic. A workshop needs an activity and enough time for participants to use it. A short talk needs a narrower explanation. A panel needs multiple perspectives worth hearing together.

The [SXSW EDU 2025 guide](https://explore.sxsw.com/hubfs/SXSWEDU2025PanelPickerGuide-c.pdf) illustrates how organizers separate descriptions, takeaways, and formats. Use that structural distinction, while checking the current rules of the event you are approaching.

Avoid submitting investor pitch material to an educational program. A market-size slide and company traction story do not automatically establish a useful lesson for attendees.

## Write outcomes that can be demonstrated

A learning outcome describes what someone can do after the session. “Understand our journey” is difficult to evaluate. “Identify when a pilot request changes the original learning goal” is more concrete.

Use an action verb and an object. Then check whether your proposed content actually teaches that action.

For the fictional pilot session, three outcomes might be:

- Identify the question a pilot is supposed to answer.
- Separate work that supports that question from work that changes it.
- Prepare a customer conversation about the boundary.

These outcomes form a progression. The audience first recognizes the task, then makes a distinction, then acts on it.

Avoid promising an outcome that depends on circumstances outside the session. Attendees cannot leave with guaranteed customer agreement or improved revenue simply because they heard your talk.

Test the outcomes with a colleague unfamiliar with the proposal. Ask what they expect the session to contain. If their expectation differs from your plan, revise the wording before the organizer has to interpret it.

## Design an original exercise

An exercise makes the proposal tangible. It also reveals whether the lesson is teachable or merely a collection of opinions.

Here is a fictional activity for the pilot session. The pilot brief is: “Can a facilities coordinator assign one repair to an outside contractor, and can that contractor return a completion note without access to the full workspace?” Attendees already understand basic customer interviews; this exercise teaches scope decisions, not product setup.

| Customer request | Supports the current learning question? | Changes the engagement? | Conversation needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add a missing field needed for the agreed task | Possibly | Not necessarily | Confirm why the field is needed |
| Support a different department’s workflow | Unclear | Possibly | Clarify whether the audience has changed |
| Produce a management report unrelated to the task | Probably not | Likely | Discuss a separate objective |

Worked first row: suppose the missing field is the repair location. Without it, the contractor cannot identify the agreed task, so adding it supports this pilot question. A report comparing every department does not test the same question; discuss it separately. If the requested field were unrelated to the repair, the classification could change.

The table is an original teaching example, not a report of real customers. During the session, attendees would fill in their reasoning rather than being told that every row has a universal answer.

The exercise should lead back to the outcomes. If participants can complete it but still cannot identify the pilot’s purpose, the session needs a stronger explanation before the activity.

For a prerecorded talk, build a pause into the exercise and then show a worked interpretation. For a live workshop, plan how people will compare answers without turning the discussion into unrelated consulting.

## Assemble the abstract and teaching sequence

Write the abstract after the lesson and exercise are designed. Describe the audience situation, the tension, the method, and the practical result.

Here is an original fictional abstract:

> Early customer pilots can accumulate useful requests while losing the question they were meant to answer. This session shows product leaders how to identify that question, classify new requests, and prepare a clear conversation about scope. Using a worked scenario and a short decision exercise, attendees will practice separating essential pilot learning from a different engagement.

Now outline the session in teaching order: establish the situation, explain the distinction, work an example, invite application, address an exception, and close with the next action.

Include the exception deliberately. Perhaps the customer relationship is intended to remain exploratory. That case helps attendees understand where the method fits instead of treating it as a rigid rule.

Your biography should explain why you can teach this lesson. Relevant experience matters more than a long list of unrelated achievements.

## Test the proposal before submitting

Read the title, abstract, outcomes, and exercise together. They should describe the same session. If the title promises a broad transformation but the exercise teaches one narrow decision, narrow the title.

Check whether the proposal depends on your product being the answer. Replace unnecessary product references with a neutral explanation of the underlying work. Keep company examples only where their details support the lesson.

Ask a prospective attendee what they would hope to leave with. Ask a colleague whether the teaching sequence seems feasible in the available time. These are different checks: audience usefulness and delivery practicality.

Follow the event’s actual submission process and record the version you sent. If the session is accepted, clarify its final format and recording requirements.
