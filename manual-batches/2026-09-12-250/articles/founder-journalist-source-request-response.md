Respond to a journalist’s source request by answering the exact question with relevant firsthand knowledge, supporting the important facts, and identifying your role and availability. Keep the response concise enough to assess quickly. State the limits of your expertise and distinguish real experience from hypothetical examples or broader interpretation.

## Read the request as an assignment

A source request already contains an editorial task. Your job is to determine whether you can help with that task, not replace it with the story you would prefer to tell.

Read the entire request before drafting. Identify the question, intended audience, deadline, requested qualifications, and any format requirements. A reporter may want a brief written explanation, a phone conversation, or a person with direct experience of a particular event.

Underline the part you can answer. If the request asks how a workflow changes in practice, a general statement about your company’s mission will not help.

[Qwoted’s pitching advice](https://www.qwoted.com/best-practices-for-pitching-journalists/) emphasizes relevance and a readable response. Apply that by making the requested answer easy to find in your first paragraph.

For a newly funded founder, the financing may explain your current role but rarely answers the reporter’s substantive question. Include it only when the request actually concerns that context.

## Check whether your expertise fits the question

Write down how you know what you plan to say. You may have built the relevant feature, managed the process, observed a specific customer interaction with permission to discuss it, or conducted research you can describe accurately.

These are different evidence sources. A product builder can explain design choices without claiming to represent every customer in the market. A founder’s experience with one deployment does not establish a population-wide pattern.

[Qwoted’s expertise guidance](https://www.qwoted.com/am-i-an-expert-how-to-know-if-you-should-pitch-an-opportunity/) recommends verifiable knowledge and transparency about limits. Use that boundary to decide whether to respond, narrow the answer, or decline.

If the reporter asks a legal, medical, or other specialist question outside your qualifications, do not convert operational experience into professional advice. You can offer the portion you understand and make its scope explicit.

A narrow useful answer is better than a broad answer built on assumptions.

## Build a request-to-answer grid

Use this original worksheet to prepare a response.

| Field | Working question |
|---|---|
| Exact request | What does the journalist want to understand? |
| Relevant experience | What have I directly done or observed? |
| Direct answer | What is the clearest supported response? |
| Concrete example | What situation makes the explanation understandable? |
| Evidence | What record or source supports the important fact? |
| Limit | What should the reader not infer? |
| Availability | How and when can I answer follow-up questions? |

Keep the grid private. The journalist usually needs the answer, not the entire preparation document.

Suppose a fictional reporter asks what goes wrong when maintenance teams share tasks with external contractors. A founder might have direct knowledge of access-design decisions but no basis for estimating how often every company experiences the problem.

The answer can therefore explain one mechanism: sharing a whole workspace may reveal more information than a contractor needs. It should not invent an industry-wide frequency or claim that the founder’s product eliminates every coordination issue.

## Write a usable response

Here is an original fictional response to that request:

> One problem is that the task and the surrounding workspace often contain different kinds of information. A contractor may need the repair description and access instructions without needing the company’s unrelated internal notes.
>
> In my work designing task-sharing software, that distinction has shaped how we define access. It is a design consideration, not evidence that every team currently shares too much information.
>
> I am [name], founder of [company], and can discuss the design tradeoff or answer follow-up questions at [availability].

The wording answers a concrete question and identifies the speaker’s relationship to the subject. It does not invent a customer anecdote or turn a product design choice into an industry statistic.

If a real example is available, explain what happened and what you observed. If you use a hypothetical example, introduce it as hypothetical. The journalist should not have to infer which kind of evidence they are reading.

Keep promotional language out of the quoted explanation. A useful answer should still make sense if the company name appears only in the attribution.

## Make evidence and availability easy to assess

Link to the most relevant supporting source, not your homepage by default. If a claim depends on a particular document, explain which part establishes the fact.

For a recorded response, give the journalist a short written summary and the relevant timestamp. A long video without a clear answer creates additional work. Confirm whether the recording is intended as background, an attributable statement, or another agreed use.

State your role accurately. If you are discussing an earlier job, make that timing clear. If you have a commercial relationship relevant to the answer, disclose it rather than leaving the reporter to discover it later.

Be realistic about follow-up availability. Do not promise an immediate call if you will be unavailable, and do not assume the reporter can wait for your preferred schedule.

If the request has already passed its deadline, ask whether a response is still useful before investing heavily in a package the journalist can no longer use.

## Handle follow-up without overreaching

A useful first answer may lead to a question beyond your original expertise. Treat that as a new question, not as pressure to maintain the appearance of knowing everything.

You can say, “I can explain the product-design side, but I do not have evidence for that market-wide conclusion.” If another person can answer, offer an introduction only with that person’s agreement.

Keep a note of any factual follow-up you promised. Send the verified answer or explain promptly why you cannot provide it. Do not replace missing evidence with a more confident version of the same assumption.

After the interaction, record which topics you could support well and which required more preparation. That becomes a useful expertise map for future requests, rather than a list of every subject you would like to be associated with.
