For a direct founder message, treat the camera lens as the person you are addressing, not your own preview. For an interview, choose a consistent conversational target near the camera. Map the lens, notes, and other speaker before recording, then test whether your gaze helps the viewer understand who you are speaking to.

## Decide who the viewer is watching you address

A founder can speak directly to customers or let customers observe a conversation. Those are different visual arrangements. In a direct update, the viewer is the addressed person. In a cofounder interview, the viewer may be listening to two people speak to each other.

Choose that relationship before moving your camera. If you begin a direct message while watching a second monitor, the viewer may feel you are talking to someone outside the frame. If you constantly alternate between the interviewer and lens, the intended audience can become unclear.

Write a one-line intention: “I am explaining this change directly to an existing customer,” or “The audience is observing my conversation with our technical cofounder.” That line becomes the reference for your eyeline choices.

This is not a rule against looking away. People pause, think, and glance naturally. The purpose is to remove unintended visual confusion, not to produce unbroken eye contact or an artificial performance.

## Draw a map of the real recording geometry

On a sheet of paper, mark four things: the lens, the preview, the notes, and the interviewer if there is one. Include which device each item lives on. A laptop preview may sit below its camera, while an external camera may be mounted well to one side.

[Wistia recommends an eye-level webcam in its recording setup guidance](https://wistia.com/blog/record-screen-and-webcam). Use a stable arrangement that lets you sit comfortably, then mark the actual lens rather than assuming the middle of the screen is the camera.

Add arrows showing where you currently look during an introduction, a factual sentence, and a pause. If your attention travels between three distant points, you have found a setup problem that confidence practice alone will not solve.

Move notes only after checking readability. Tiny text beside the lens can create squinting and leaning. Use a short cue card rather than trying to fit a complete page into a narrow gap.

The map should describe your real equipment. Do not copy an idealized studio diagram that assumes an operator, separate monitor, or teleprompter you do not have.

## Compare preview watching with direct address

Record the same short message twice. In the first version, use your normal habit. In the second, address the lens for complete thoughts and use the notes only between them. Keep wording and camera position similar.

An original test passage might be: “I want to clarify who this update affects. If you manage the workspace, you will see the new option. Your team does not need to change anything today.” It gives you three thoughts with natural opportunities to pause.

Watch both samples without sound. Note where your attention appears to go, particularly when you begin a sentence. Then listen with sound to check whether a gaze change coincides with losing your place or changing the meaning.

Do not score every blink or glance as a failure. [Descript advises natural lens contact rather than an overly intense stare](https://www.descript.com/blog/article/how-to-read-a-script-like-a-pro-while-recording). A useful review asks whether eye movement repeatedly distracts from the message.

If watching your preview causes constant self-correction, hide or minimize it after checking the setup, provided your recording tool permits that arrangement.

## Place an interviewer with the conversation in mind

For an interview, test the other person's position before the full conversation. If they sit far to one side, you may turn so much that the camera sees mostly your profile. If they sit behind the camera, make sure their actual position does not require you to look noticeably above or below the lens.

For a remote interview, the other person's video window is the conversational target. Place it deliberately and test the resulting gaze. Do not assume a layout that feels comfortable on a large monitor looks natural in the recorded frame.

Use a short question and answer to check this arrangement: “What changed your mind about that decision?” Answer toward the interviewer, then make a separate deliberate closing sentence toward the lens. Watch whether the transition is understandable.

In a two-founder conversation, agree on when direct address is appropriate. One person looking into the lens while the other maintains conversation can work if intentional, but accidental switching may feel disconnected.

Keep the arrangement consistent across pickups. Moving the interviewer to the opposite side changes the apparent relationship even when the words are identical.

## Complete an eyeline worksheet with specific observations

Here is a fictional worksheet for a founder recording from a laptop with an external monitor. The lens is above the laptop, the preview is on the external monitor to the right, and notes are printed flat on the desk.

The baseline observation is: “I start each sentence looking right and finish looking down.” The founder moves the preview to the laptop for setup, reduces the printed page to three cue words, and places those cues close to the camera without covering it.

The second observation is: “The opening now feels addressed to me, but reading the final instruction creates a long downward glance.” The founder scripts that instruction separately, reads it before the take, and records the complete sentence while looking forward.

The final decision is: “Lens for complete thoughts; cue glance during pauses; preview used only before recording.” The worksheet also records a limit: the founder still needs a full text reference for a complicated date. They keep that fact scripted rather than risking an inaccurate improvisation.

This is a local solution to a physical arrangement, not a judgment about personality.

## Review the message rather than your face

Ask a colleague where they felt you were directing the message. Do not first tell them the expected answer. If they say you seemed to be reading an email offscreen, compare the relevant moment with your map.

Choose the take that communicates clearly and preserves accurate wording. A slight glance is usually a smaller editorial concern than an omitted condition. Avoid replacing useful content solely to make the eyes appear perfectly fixed.

Save the map with your recording notes. Include the camera location, notes location, and whether the video is direct address or an interview. Repeat the short test whenever the device arrangement changes.
