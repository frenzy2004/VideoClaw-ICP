Turn a video conversation into an audio-first episode by removing its dependence on slides, gestures, and on-screen examples. Listen without watching, mark every missing piece of context, and repair those moments with concise narration or a different edit. Then check speaker orientation, transitions, audio consistency, and the final listening experience before preparing episode metadata.

## Listen once with the screen out of view

Do not begin by exporting the audio and calling the task complete. Play the conversation without looking at the picture and take notes whenever you cannot understand a reference. Common failures include “this chart,” “the item on the left,” an unspoken reaction, or a product action that occurs silently.

Record the timestamp and the information missing from the listener’s experience. Distinguish essential context from decorative visuals. A speaker’s smile may not need narration; a silent diagram that explains the entire argument probably does.

Use a simple question: could a listener understand the point without opening another device? An audio-first episode should not repeatedly ask people to inspect a screen to follow the main discussion. Supporting materials can add depth, but they should not carry the basic meaning.

[W3C’s transcript guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/transcripts/) distinguishes spoken content from important visual information in descriptive transcripts. Apply that distinction to your listening audit: the spoken track may omit information the original video supplied visually.

Keep the first pass diagnostic. Do not rewrite every sentence before you understand the full pattern of missing context.

## Choose the episode’s listening structure

Decide whether the conversation already has a coherent audio arc. A clear question, several explanations, and a useful conclusion may need only localized repairs. A screen-heavy product session may require a shorter thematic edit or may be unsuitable as an audio episode.

Introduce the speakers and their relevance briefly. Do not assume the listener can read lower-thirds or recognize voices. In a conversation with several participants, retain natural name references or add concise orientation when a long absence makes a speaker’s return confusing.

Remove video housekeeping that has no listening value: instructions to enlarge a slide, commentary about screen sharing, or a silent wait while a window is arranged. Preserve meaningful pauses where they help the conversation feel natural.

Choose a central question for the episode. If the original recording wanders through several unrelated product tasks, select the discussion that can stand alone in sound. Reusing the entire recording is not inherently more valuable than producing a coherent shorter episode.

Avoid manufacturing a conversation that did not occur. Reordering should preserve the meaning of responses and the context of disagreements.

## Build a visual-dependency repair sheet

Consider a fictional conversation between an operations lead and a product analyst about interpreting inventory reports. The original video includes a chart and a brief dashboard demonstration.

| Timestamp note | Missing information | Audio treatment | Meaning check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Speaker says “this line” | Which metric the line represents | Add a short spoken identification | Use the actual metric name |
| Two bars are compared | Direction of the difference | Describe the relationship without invented values | Preserve the source’s qualification |
| Cursor changes a filter | Which location is selected | Add a concise transition explaining the selection | Do not imply automatic selection |
| Guest nods before replying | Agreement is only visual | Keep the complete spoken response | Do not invent a verbal endorsement |
| Slide lists resources | Listener cannot read the list | Mention the most relevant resource in the closing | Put remaining links in episode notes |

Label any newly recorded narration as editorial connective material in the working edit notes. It should explain existing context, not impersonate a guest.

For each repair, decide whether to narrate, cut, or restructure. Adding description everywhere can make an episode cumbersome. Use the smallest change that lets the listener follow the argument accurately.

## Repair context without overnarrating

Write bridging narration in plain language. “The chart compares the two warehouse locations discussed earlier” may be enough. Do not read every axis label if the conversation only needs the direction of a comparison.

Keep newly added claims within the verified source. If the video does not establish a number or causal explanation, do not supply one to make the audio sound complete. A limitation can be stated directly: the speakers are discussing an illustrative example, not a measured customer result.

Place narration where it naturally orients the listener, usually before the reference that needs it. An explanation delivered after a confusing exchange forces the listener to reconstruct the conversation.

Match the tone without pretending the added narrator was present in the original discussion. A short host introduction or transition is a familiar audio convention and can be clearly distinct from the guests’ voices.

If a passage requires extensive description of interface movement, consider omitting it and summarizing the relevant principle. Then direct listeners to the separate video for the visual walkthrough. Keep the audio episode useful even if they never open that companion asset.

## Edit for the ear and verify the export

Listen for abrupt changes in volume, room sound, and speaking rhythm around edits. A visual cut can hide a transition that becomes obvious in audio. Smooth the join enough to preserve natural listening without removing every breath or pause.

Check speaker balance and intelligibility on headphones and an ordinary speaker. Prioritize understandable speech over decorative music. If a sound bed competes with technical terms or names, reduce or remove it.

Export using the requirements of the intended podcast host and distribution route. [Apple’s episode guidance](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/825-how-to-create-an-episode) distinguishes episode creation and delivery through a hosting provider’s RSS feed from other publishing workflows. Verify the actual route before preparing files or promising that a video upload automatically becomes a podcast episode.

Do not invent a technical loudness target from memory if the host provides specific requirements. Have the audio owner check the relevant delivery specification.

Play the exported file from beginning to end. This catches a missing bridge, truncated ending, or accidental silent section that may not have been apparent while reviewing isolated edits.

## Prepare a complete listening handoff

Write an episode description that names the central question, speakers, and practical value. Keep the companion video and supporting links available in the episode notes, but do not make those notes a substitute for missing spoken context.

Add a transcript of the final audio version if that is part of your publishing workflow. The original video transcript may no longer match after cuts and new narration. Verify speaker names and technical terms in the revised text.

Ask a fresh listener to summarize the argument and identify any moment that required a screen. If they cannot explain a comparison, return to the repair sheet rather than simply adding a generic “see the video” instruction.

Keep a record linking the audio episode to the source recording and its final edit. Future corrections should be traceable, especially if a product example changes.

The finished deliverable is an episode designed for listening: understandable references, clear speakers, natural transitions, and useful supporting notes. Extracting an audio file is the technical beginning; removing visual dependence is the editorial work that makes the reuse worthwhile.
