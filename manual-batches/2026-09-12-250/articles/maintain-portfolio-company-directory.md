Keep a portfolio directory current by maintaining one verified company record and tracing its changes to every public listing the platform owns. Separate current facts from historical information, give uncertain fields an explicit state and ask companies to confirm material changes. Updating a logo alone does not make a profile accurate.

## Define what the directory helps people do

A useful directory lets a visitor identify a company, understand its relevance and reach the correct destination. Decide which of those tasks your directory serves before collecting additional fields. A public discovery directory needs different information from a private platform contact list.

For most portfolio media work, start with the approved company name, concise description, current website, relevant category and a contact route. Add stage, location or operating status only when the team has a reliable way to maintain them and they help visitors make a decision.

[Y Combinator's directory announcement](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/the-yc-directory) describes filters for attributes including batch, industry and company status. The useful lesson is that directory fields support discovery tasks. The historical page's company counts are not needed to design your own record.

Avoid adding fields because they appear on another fund's website. Each field creates a maintenance obligation. A sparse confirmed record is more useful than a detailed profile whose most important facts are no longer dependable.

## Separate identity, description and status

Use a stable internal company identifier that survives name changes. The public name can change while the identifier continues linking the company to media, contacts and historical records. Do not use a website domain as the only identifier; domains can also change.

Keep the approved description separate from internal category labels. A company may describe its product in specific customer language while the directory groups it under a broader industry. Those fields serve different readers and should not overwrite one another.

[Canto's explanation of metadata types](https://www.canto.com/glossary/metadata-management/) distinguishes content descriptions from administrative and relationship information. For a company directory, that separation helps keep public copy apart from confirmation dates, source references and links to related assets.

Treat status as a confirmed fact with a source, not a conclusion inferred from a quiet social account or an unavailable website. If the company has not confirmed a change, record the uncertainty privately and decide whether the public field should be omitted.

Preserve historical names where they help people recognize the company, but make the current public name unambiguous.

## Use a field dictionary

Write a short dictionary so different staff members update records consistently. The example below is an original working structure to adapt.

| Field | Meaning | Preferred confirmation | Change behavior |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Company ID | Stable internal identity | Platform record | Does not change with a rebrand |
| Public name | Company's approved display name | Company contact or approved company page | Update current listings |
| Description | Current public explanation | Company-approved wording | Review linked media claims |
| Website | Intended visitor destination | Company confirmation and link check | Replace and test |
| Category | Directory discovery label | Platform taxonomy plus company context | Preserve a category-change note |
| Status | Confirmed operating state, if used | Appropriate company confirmation | Avoid inference from inactivity |
| Last checked | Date of factual review | Review record | Never substitute for source evidence |

For each field, decide whether it is required, optional or conditional. A status field should not force a coordinator to choose an inaccurate label just to save the record. An explicit unknown state can remain internal while the public profile omits the unsupported claim.

Also define which changes need a company review. Correcting a broken link to the company's confirmed new site is different from rewriting its product description. The dictionary should make those differences clear.

## Process a fictional rebrand

Imagine Alder Labs becomes Alder and changes its website. Its old profile says it provides analytics for retail teams, while the company now describes a narrower inventory-planning product. These are three separate changes: name, destination and description.

First, confirm the supplied changes and record their source. Keep the stable company ID. Update the public name and new website, then test the destination. Review the new description as a separate piece of approved copy rather than automatically generating it from the new domain.

Next, search the platform's owned records for the old name and link. The directory, founder biography, image caption, interview page and newsletter archive may each require a different action. A historical newsletter can retain the original wording while a current directory should show the new identity.

Create a change journal with the old value, new value, affected destination and action. Mark items that require annotation rather than replacement. Do not silently rewrite a historical quote to match today's positioning.

Finally, ask the company to check the current profile as a visitor would. A correct database entry is not enough if the public page still shows cached wording or links to the wrong site.

## Use checks that produce decisions

Schedule reviews around meaningful triggers: a company sends an update, a website link breaks, a new public story is being prepared or the platform is assembling a directory feature. A periodic check can supplement these events, but it should have a defined scope.

A link check tells you whether a destination opens; it does not establish that the company is active or that the page contains approved messaging. A changed logo tells you that an asset changed; it does not prove a complete rebrand. Keep observations separate from conclusions.

When a company does not respond, record the request and the unresolved field. Decide whether to retain a clearly dated description, omit a nonessential field or hold a planned feature. Avoid filling the gap with an unsupported status label.

Check for conflicting sources before updating. If the company contact's proposed wording differs from the live website, ask which version is approved for the directory. The coordinator's job is to resolve the discrepancy, not choose whichever wording sounds more impressive.

## Keep maintenance connected to media work

Link directory changes to the assets that depend on the changed facts. A new founder role may affect an interview introduction. A changed product name may affect a caption or resource listing. Not every historical asset needs rewriting, but every affected current claim deserves a decision.

Assign a maintained contact route for correction requests. A company should not need to find the employee who originally created its profile. Include the stable company ID in the request internally so similar names do not create confusion.

Review the change journal for recurring problems. If staff repeatedly discover outdated descriptions while preparing features, the company update route may be unclear. If the same old website appears in several places, the platform may be copying facts without maintaining their relationship to a source record.

The directory should become a dependable starting point for media work, not a competing version of the company's identity. Keep the record small, its sources clear and its change history usable. That makes the next feature, introduction or public correction easier to handle.
