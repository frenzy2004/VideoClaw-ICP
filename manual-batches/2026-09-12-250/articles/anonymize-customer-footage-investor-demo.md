Prepare customer footage for an investor demo by confirming permitted use, removing unnecessary identifying context, and reviewing the entire exported clip for residual exposure. Blurring a name is not a guarantee of anonymity. When the workflow can be shown with fictional data, a fresh capture may be clearer and safer.

## Establish what footage you are allowed to use

Identify the original recording, the people and organizations it concerns, and the purpose for which it was captured. A support-session recording and a customer-approved investor demonstration are not automatically interchangeable. Confirm the intended audience and use before editing.

Keep permission questions separate from image treatment. Removing a logo does not itself establish authorization to share a customer's screen. If the allowed use is unclear, pause sharing and ask the appropriate customer contact or your organization's responsible adviser.

Consider whether this particular customer's footage is necessary. If the reader's job is to inspect a product interaction, a fictional recreation may demonstrate the same behavior without carrying customer context. If the value depends on the real customer's circumstances, be precise about what can be shown.

Do not describe a clip as anonymous simply because you removed obvious names. [NIST's de-identification guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/188/final) distinguishes masking from adequate de-identification and recognizes re-identification risk. This article's editing procedure reduces visible exposure; it does not certify anonymity or legal compliance.

## Map identifying context across picture and sound

Watch the recording once for the product story, then again for identifying details. These are different viewing tasks. A reviewer concentrating on the successful action may miss an email address in a notification or a customer name spoken quietly in the background.

Build an exposure map that includes persistent screen elements, temporary pop-ups, audio, and the assets around the video. The following fictional example concerns a customer scheduling screen:

| Surface | Possible exposure | Editorial treatment to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account header | Company name and logo | Exclude the header or use an authorized replacement capture |
| Job details | Address, contact name and appointment context | Replace the scene with fictional records where feasible |
| Browser chrome | Tenant URL and profile identity | Capture a narrower permitted area |
| Spoken narration | Customer or employee names | Rerecord the affected explanation |
| Face, avatar or camera tile | Recognizable person or customer identity | Retain only for the permitted use, remove the segment, or recapture |
| Original voice | Speaker recognizable without a spoken name | Remove, or clearly label a recreated demonstration with new narration |
| Captions and transcript | Names or contextual clues copied from earlier audio | Review the delivered text separately after audio changes |
| Notification | Message preview and sender | Remove the contaminated segment or recapture |
| Hosting preview | Filename or thumbnail repeats identity | Rename and regenerate the surrounding asset |

Look for combinations, not just single fields. A unique project description, date, and location can provide identifying context even if the person's name is absent.

## Choose removal, masking, or a new capture by scene

Remove information that is not needed to understand the product. Cropping can work when the identifying region stays outside the useful action throughout the scene. Check menus and transitions; a crop that works on the starting frame may fail when a panel moves.

Masking may be appropriate for a stable region within footage you are permitted to use. Inspect its coverage over time, including motion, resizing, and frames immediately before and after a cut. A mask that briefly slips can expose the original detail.

Do not blur the very field that proves the product's result and then expect the investor to trust an unreadable screen. When sensitive evidence and useful evidence occupy the same area, create a new recording with fictional records or obtain a more suitable approved example.

If you recreate the workflow, label it accurately as a demonstration using fictional data. Do not present the recreation as a recording of the customer's actual session. Preserve the real product behavior while changing the props, and avoid implying customer endorsement that has not been given.

## Preserve the product's meaning while removing context

An effective privacy edit leaves the action understandable. Identify which relationships the viewer must still see: the same job before and after assignment, a message linked to its response, or a file attached to the correct record.

If identifiers are replaced in a fresh capture, use consistent fictional values across all scenes. Changing a job name between the input and output can make the demonstration look disconnected. The purpose is not to make the screen vaguely generic; it is to show a coherent task without unnecessary real-world identity.

Check whether your narration still refers to information that is no longer visible. A sentence such as “the customer at this address” becomes confusing when the address is removed. Rewrite it around the task: “the selected service appointment.”

Avoid turning privacy treatment into an unsupported performance claim. A clean screen with simplified records is a demonstration environment, not evidence that the system processed the original customer's full dataset. State the relevant scope naturally when introducing the clip.

## Inspect the rendered file, including transient frames

Export the intended delivery version and review that file, not just the editing timeline. Pause around every masked region's entrance, movement, and exit. Watch at normal speed too; some exposures become noticeable only when a notification animates into view.

Listen without watching. Ask who or what remains identifiable, including a recognizable original voice with no spoken name. Review faces, avatars and camera tiles on a separate picture pass. Then inspect captions and every delivered transcript: text generated before an audio edit can retain removed names. Re-narration is a labeled recreation, not a guarantee of anonymity.

Inspect the thumbnail, title, filename, and description. A carefully edited movie can still expose its subject through a preview image selected from an untreated frame. Keep the source recording out of the folder or link intended for the investor.

Ask a second permitted reviewer to inspect the export with the exposure map. Give them the task of finding residual identifying context rather than approving the visual polish. Record unresolved concerns and choose a new capture when reliable treatment is doubtful.

## Share within the agreed audience and keep limits explicit

Use a delivery method that matches the intended audience. [YouTube explains that unlisted videos can be reshared by anyone with the link](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/157177?hl=en). “Not in search” is not the same as access restricted to named investors.

Test the chosen access arrangement with an intended viewer. Keep the treated export distinct from raw source footage. Do not attach the project package if it includes untreated media unless sharing those materials is separately appropriate.

Access controls and editing solve different problems. Restricted access does not remove sensitive content from a file, and removed identifiers do not prevent recipients from copying a recording. Avoid promising either technique provides complete protection.

The finished work product is a permitted, narrowly scoped clip plus a private exposure map explaining what was removed, retained, or recaptured. If that cannot be achieved confidently, use a fictional demonstration instead of circulating uncertain customer footage.
