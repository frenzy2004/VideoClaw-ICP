Use stock footage for scenes that illustrate a general idea without pretending to document your business. Commission a shoot when identity, product behavior, location, or continuity must be genuine. Make that decision for each planned scene, then price the remaining capture work instead of comparing two vague, all-or-nothing production packages.

## Start with what the scene must prove

A warehouse exterior might establish an industrial setting. A close-up of your packing station might demonstrate how a particular order is checked. Those shots have different evidentiary jobs even if both show logistics.

Write one plain sentence beside every proposed scene: “The viewer should understand that…” Then ask whether a generic image could truthfully carry that sentence. “Orders move through a distribution system” may allow illustration. “Our team scans each item before dispatch” requires something connected to your actual process, not an unrelated worker scanning a box.

This is a purchasing exercise, not a complete storyboard workshop. You are identifying where access to the real business creates value that a library cannot supply. Include the narration and neighboring scenes in the review: an otherwise generic clip can become misleading when placed under a specific claim about your staff.

If a scene merely decorates the video, mark it optional. Removing unnecessary imagery can be a better decision than spending time sourcing a weak substitute.

## Test previews before assuming stock will fit

Search for an actual candidate, not an imaginary category of footage. “Office collaboration” sounds easy to source until the available rooms, uniforms, devices, and working styles conflict with the rest of the video.

Place a watermarked preview in a private evaluation cut if the library permits that use. Check camera movement, duration, lighting, screen direction, framing, and the transition into your real footage. A beautiful clip that cannot connect to the next shot may create more editing work than it saves.

Inspect the clip's usable middle, not just its thumbnail. A hand can obscure the important action; a brand mark can enter near the end; the movement may finish before the narration does. Record the exact usable section and any required crop.

Do not publish a preview or assume that evaluating it grants production rights. [Adobe Stock's license summary](https://stock.adobe.com/license-terms) distinguishes permitted commercial uses from editorial-only assets. That is a purchase constraint, not merely an aesthetic label. Check the specific candidate's designation before treating it as an available replacement.

## Build the shot sourcing map

Use this proposed worksheet to turn a mixed footage plan into an order the production team can estimate. The examples are illustrative, not observations about any business.

| Proposed scene | Must be real? | Stock test | Purchase action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Broad city skyline | No specific office claim | Preview fits the opening pace | Verify asset and intended use |
| Actual product opening | Yes, shows current packaging | Generic substitutes fail identity test | Commission close-up and wider safety |
| Support representative | Yes if presented as your employee | Unrelated person would imply identity | Film consenting staff or revise scene |
| Abstract network movement | No, explicitly illustrative | Candidate clashes with brand colors | Compare another asset with custom graphic |
| Factory sequence | Depends on narration | Machines differ from actual process | Clarify claim before booking anything |

Add columns for asset identifier, preview timecode, license question, continuity concern, and person responsible for the decision. “Stock available” is not a sufficient entry; link the candidate that passed the visual test.

The map should also record negative decisions. A rejected preview with a short reason prevents the next reviewer from restarting the same search. Keep the alternatives small enough to compare thoughtfully rather than building an unmanageable library shortlist.

## Separate visual fit from usage permission

A clip passing the edit test does not establish that your intended use is permitted. Conversely, an appropriately licensed clip can still be a poor or misleading representation of your business.

Ask who will license the asset, for which client, and whether the proposed publication context fits the terms. Save those questions for the licensing check rather than letting a producer answer them from memory during a creative review.

Free libraries also have conditions. The [Pexels license](https://www.pexels.com/license/) prohibits implying endorsement by people or brands depicted. A smiling person in a free clip therefore should not casually become your “satisfied customer” through an adjacent testimonial caption. The underlying issue is the context you create, not whether the file cost money.

For a recognizable location, person, or branded object, ask the vendor what documentation or restrictions attach to the candidate. Escalate unresolved permission questions to the licensor or an appropriately qualified adviser. Do not turn this worksheet into a legal clearance certificate.

## Reduce the shoot without breaking continuity

After choosing credible stock candidates, issue a revised capture order. State what was removed, what remains, and which links between scenes still need to be filmed.

Suppose library footage supplies the broad exterior atmosphere, but the film still needs your product on a desk, a user interaction, and a reaction shot. Those three shots may need matching light, wardrobe, hands, and screen content. Booking each independently could undermine the sequence even though every individual image is authentic.

Ask the camera team to identify shared setups. One desk arrangement might cover several remaining shots efficiently, while a short location move may consume disproportionate preparation. Let the crew explain the consequences rather than assuming fewer requested shots always produce a proportionally smaller quote.

Retain reasonable coverage for the custom scenes: an alternate framing, a clean background, or a longer action may protect the edit. The purpose is a focused shoot, not a fragile minimum in which one missed action defeats the entire plan.

## Compare the finished acquisition options

Request estimates against the same sourcing map. One option might use licensed atmosphere plus a half-day of focused product capture. Another might film all scenes together. A third might simplify the concept enough to remove both an expensive location and several library assets.

Do not compare only the stock license subtotal with the camera invoice. Include search and preview time, editing adjustments, additional graphics, necessary permissions, reshoots, and the consequences of footage that cannot be reused as expected. Populate amounts from actual quotes; there is no universal stock-versus-shoot saving.

Finish with a brief decision note naming the scenes sourced from libraries, the exact custom capture order, unresolved license questions, and the fallback for an unavailable clip. Have the person responsible for the business claim review the sequence alongside the production lead.

The successful purchase is a coherent set of usable images that says something true. A hybrid plan is valuable when it preserves that result while removing capture work that adds little.
