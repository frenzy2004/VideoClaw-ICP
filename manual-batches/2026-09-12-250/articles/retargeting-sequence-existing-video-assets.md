Plan a video retargeting sequence by matching an observed audience condition to a new question and an existing asset that answers it. Do not simply show the same clip again. Verify the platform’s audience rules, exclude people who have already taken the relevant action, and prepare a fallback when the audience cannot serve.

## Treat viewing as a signal, not a diagnosis

A person who watched part of a video has supplied a limited signal. You do not know that they understood the explanation, intend to buy, or hold a particular role in a purchase. Build the next message around a plausible continuation without claiming to know their private motivation.

Start with the source asset’s promise. If it explained a general workflow problem, a follow-up demonstration may be useful. If it already showed the complete workflow, another near-identical introduction adds little. Identify what is genuinely new in the next asset.

[LinkedIn’s Matched Audiences documentation](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a427551/retargeting?lang=en-US) includes audiences based on video-ad viewing and other eligible engagement. Keep the source condition precise. Do not assume an organic clip’s viewers automatically become a usable video-ad audience.

Write the hypothesis in restrained language: “People meeting this viewing condition may benefit from seeing the exception-handling example.” That is a planning statement. It is not evidence that the audience has the same objection or is ready for a sales request.

## Audit the library for the next useful answer

Inventory existing assets by buyer question rather than format alone. A demo, customer conversation, and product explanation can each answer different uncertainties. Note the exact passage that supplies the answer and whether it can stand alone.

Choose a small sequence with distinct jobs. An initial clip might establish the problem. The next could demonstrate a method. A later asset might explain a limitation or help someone decide whether the method fits their situation. The sequence should not become progressively more promotional without adding information.

Check freshness and context. A customer quote from one use case cannot prove that every viewer will receive the same result. Keep qualifications visible in the adapted asset. If a recording only partly answers the next question, repair that gap before assigning it to the sequence.

Avoid assuming everyone sees every stage in order. Delivery is not a classroom syllabus. Each follow-up should contain enough context for someone who remembers little of the original clip. Briefly restate the problem without forcing a long replay of the same opening.

## Build an audience-to-asset routing sheet

Use this fictional planning example for a product that helps teams review requests. It describes possible routing, not an active campaign or a prediction.

| Audience condition | Possible next question | Existing asset | Intended action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Meets selected viewing condition on problem ad | What does a clearer workflow look like? | Focused product demonstration | Watch the worked example |
| Meets selected condition on demonstration ad | What happens when details are missing? | Exception-handling excerpt | Review the clarification method |
| Has taken the relevant request action | What happens after I ask for help? | Existing response explanation | Receive the appropriate follow-up, not the same acquisition prompt |

Add fields for the platform audience name, lookback setting, exclusions, and minimum readiness. Keep those operational fields separate from the editorial hypothesis.

The third row matters: someone who has already completed the request should not keep receiving an invitation to do exactly that. Confirm which exclusions the actual campaign setup can support.

If the available signals cannot distinguish these groups reliably, simplify the plan. One useful follow-up with a clear exclusion can be more coherent than a complex sequence built on assumptions the platform cannot implement.

## Check audience feasibility before editing every variant

[LinkedIn’s video-audience setup guide](https://www.linkedin.com/help/lms/answer/a427086/) documents viewing thresholds, lookback choices, and an audience minimum of 300 matched member accounts. It also distinguishes video-view counts from matched accounts. Check current readiness in the actual account before treating a source view total as a usable audience.

A threshold is a configuration choice, not a universal definition of interest. The same viewed percentage can represent very different amounts of content across two recordings. Review what the viewer could have seen by that point.

Ask the paid-media owner to verify eligibility, overlap, exclusions, and the intended delivery setup. Keep budget and activation decisions with that owner. A content routing sheet should not silently become authorization to launch advertising.

Prepare a fallback if the audience is too small or unavailable. You can keep the assets ready for direct follow-up, combine genuinely compatible conditions where appropriate, or wait for sufficient eligible activity. Do not broaden the audience so far that the next message loses its rationale just to make a campaign serve.

## Adapt each reused asset for its new context

A follow-up clip needs an opening that reconnects the topic without presuming surveillance-like knowledge. “Here is how the workflow handles missing details” is useful. “We saw you watch our last video” adds unnecessary personal framing and may make the message uncomfortable.

Keep the central proof intact. Remove event housekeeping, internal references, and unrelated sections, but preserve the condition that makes the example accurate. If the clip uses sample data, say so where it matters.

Align the accompanying text, video ending, and destination. A follow-up about exceptions should not send viewers to a generic brand page that never addresses them. If the next action is another video, identify what new answer it provides.

Use consistent visual cues so the assets feel related, but do not make them indistinguishable. Each one should signal a different question. Review the sequence as a set: can a colleague explain the added value of every stage without relying on campaign names?

That review often reveals a missing middle asset or an unnecessary repeated introduction before any media budget is involved.

## Evaluate the sequence without inventing a journey

Review delivery and outcomes at the level your tools support. Separate eligible audience size, actual exposure, video engagement, destination visits, and the intended action. Do not add stage totals together and label them unique people who completed a journey.

Check whether the exclusions work and whether people receive relevant next content. A campaign that serves can still be logically wrong. An outdated destination, repeated acquisition prompt, or mismatched follow-up may matter more than a small change in a headline metric.

If comparing sequences, define the difference in advance and avoid changing audience conditions, creative, and destination simultaneously. Otherwise the result cannot tell you which change mattered. Treat limited evidence as limited evidence rather than declaring a winning sequence from a few actions.

Update the routing sheet with observed problems and next decisions. Keep unverified explanations labeled as hypotheses. The work product is a feasible content sequence with distinct answers and clear conditions—not a claim that every viewer follows a predictable path from first exposure to purchase.
