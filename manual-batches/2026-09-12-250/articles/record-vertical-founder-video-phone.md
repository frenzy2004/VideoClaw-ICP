Record a vertical founder video by composing the original shot in portrait orientation, supporting the phone securely, and checking the active lens before speaking. Save a short test of your normal voice and movement, then review it with sound. Record the full message only after the picture, audio, and framing work together in the intended orientation.

## Start with a message that fits a direct phone recording

Choose one customer question or founder observation that can stand on its own. A phone recording works best when you know what the viewer should understand before deciding how to hold the device.

Write three cues: the point, the concrete explanation, and the next step. This is a delivery aid for an original recording, not a plan to cut a longer video into social clips.

For a fictional founder, the point might be that an upcoming customer office hour will focus on approval roles. The explanation is why that distinction caused questions. The next step is where existing customers can find the confirmed details. Verify those facts before recording.

Avoid choosing a location merely because it looks spontaneous. A noisy corridor can make a simple message hard to understand. The phone's portability is useful only if the chosen place still supports clear communication.

Decide whether this is a personal direct address or a more formal statement. That choice should influence the delivery and setting without changing the facts.

## Set portrait orientation and stabilize the phone

Place the phone vertically before beginning the test. Check the recording preview and saved result rather than assuming the interface orientation guarantees the file you want.

Use a secure support on a stable surface. If you improvise with existing objects, make sure the phone cannot slide, fall, or cover its camera and microphone openings. Do not balance it above people, liquids, or valuable equipment.

Compare the supported position with a hand-held baseline. Holding the phone may be appropriate for some messages, but it can also change distance and angle as your arm tires. Choose deliberately after seeing the saved sample.

[Apple documents camera grid and level controls for composing a shot](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/set-up-your-shot-iph3dc593597/26/ios/26). If your camera app provides similar guides in the mode you are using, they can help you check alignment. They do not replace watching your normal movement.

Leave room for the gestures the message needs. A portrait frame can feel roomy above the head while cutting off hands that carry part of the explanation.

## Confirm the camera and recording mode

Identify which lens is active. With a front-facing camera, your image may be easy to monitor, but the lens is not the center of the preview. With a rear camera, ask someone to help frame the shot or record a test you can inspect.

[Apple's video guide explains Video mode and notes that formats vary by iPhone model](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/record-a-video-iph61f49e4bb/ios). On other phones or camera apps, consult the equivalent current guidance. Do not assume every control or recording option appears on every device.

Choose a supported setting that works with your intended delivery process. Avoid changing several technical settings simply because the highest number sounds more professional. A stable, understandable recording is the first requirement.

Check battery and available storage before the full take. Close or manage interruptions using options appropriate to your device and organizational requirements. Keep emergency or necessary contact arrangements in mind rather than disabling everything indiscriminately.

Make sure recording actually starts. A prepared performance in front of an idle preview produces no usable file.

## Run a short focus-and-sound test

Record the first few sentences with your normal speaking voice. Include a small head turn and the hand movement you expect to use. Stop, open the saved clip, and listen as well as watch.

Check whether your face remains clear, whether light changes distract, and whether the microphone captures speech without rubbing, wind, or a hand covering an opening. Review at an ordinary playback level. Do not judge sound while concentrating only on the picture.

Use a small test card with four fields: orientation, face visibility, speech clarity, and movement. Record an observation for each rather than a vague pass based on the overall impression.

If focus or brightness changes during the sample, simplify the scene or use supported controls you understand, then test again. Do not borrow photo-mode instructions blindly for a different recording app or mode.

The goal is a sample that resembles the actual message. A silent still frame cannot validate audio, and a brief count cannot show how your posture changes during explanation.

## Improve a fictional hand-held setup

Imagine a founder, Ana, making a customer thank-you from her office. Her first vertical sample is hand-held at a low angle. The frame moves when she gestures, her sleeve brushes the phone, and she repeatedly watches her own face.

Her test card records three distinct problems: unstable composition, handling noise, and an unintended eyeline. She does not solve them by speaking more enthusiastically.

Ana places the phone securely on a shelf at a comfortable height, steps into a marked position, and records the same passage. The picture is steadier and the handling noise disappears, but the voice sounds more distant. She moves to a quieter room and chooses a closer supported position without crowding the frame.

In the final test, she uses the lens as the main address point and glances at three cue words between complete thoughts. She saves the accepted arrangement as a note with a reference still.

These are fictional observations, not evidence that a particular shelf, distance, or phone will work for everyone. The example shows how to change one cause at a time.

## Record, review, and keep the accepted original

Once the test works, record the full message in complete thoughts. Leave a clear start and ending rather than reaching for the phone while still saying the final instruction.

If you make a factual mistake, restart the affected thought with enough context to understand it. Do not rely on a later edit to repair an ambiguous correction. Preserve exact names, conditions, and dates.

Watch the entire saved file before sharing. Confirm the orientation, sound, opening, ending, and practical meaning. Check that a notification, background person, or private document did not appear midway through the take.

Keep the accepted original and a note identifying the intended message. Do not confuse the first test with the final version when handing the file to someone else.
