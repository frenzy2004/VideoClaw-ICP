Run a paid editor test on a small, representative task with the same inputs and evaluation rules for each candidate. Agree compensation, permitted use, delivery, and one bounded follow-up change. Judge the edit and the reasoning behind it before awarding recurring work; a polished sample alone cannot establish fit.

## Choose one decision the audition must reveal

Begin with the production problem you expect to encounter repeatedly. Perhaps interviews contain several versions of the same answer. Perhaps the speaker explains a workflow in the wrong order. Perhaps screen footage needs careful selection to preserve context. Choose one of those problems and build the test around it.

Avoid combining a full brand film, animation treatment, caption system, thumbnail, and campaign strategy into one audition. Such a task makes it difficult to see why candidates differ and asks them to absorb too much unpaid preparation around the paid work. You can investigate additional specialties after establishing the core fit.

Upwork's hiring guidance identifies editing skill, portfolio fit, and collaboration as relevant considerations. That supports evaluating more than visual polish, without supplying a universal candidate score. [Upwork's video editor hiring guide](https://www.upwork.com/hire/video-editors/how-to-hire/).

Write a single hiring question: “Can this editor select a clear explanation from imperfect interview takes?” Keep it visible while preparing the exercise. Every requested deliverable should help answer it. Remove anything that merely makes the audition resemble a large real project.

## Assemble a small but revealing source pack

Use material that you may share for evaluation. A staged recording with invented company details can expose editing decisions without involving an actual customer. Supply enough context to make the work fair: intended audience, central message, essential factual statement, and desired output.

For a fictional example, imagine a scheduling tool called Juniper Desk. Record three versions of an explanation. One is concise but omits a dependency; another is complete but hesitant; the third repeats the benefit without explaining the action. Add a screen recording that shows the dependency. The editor must decide how to combine clarity with completeness.

Include a deliberate but ordinary ambiguity, such as two plausible opening shots. Do not conceal a rule and later penalize candidates for failing to guess it. Tell them that they may ask questions and may document assumptions.

Send a file inventory and check that every candidate can open the media before the work clock begins. Missing downloads reveal little about editorial judgment. Keep source duration, delivery format, and optional supporting assets identical across the comparison.

## Put the paid boundary in writing

Agree the fee directly with each candidate. If different candidates quote different amounts, keep the evaluation rubric constant and record the commercial difference separately. A cheaper test is not automatically a stronger result, and a higher fee is not proof of better craft.

Specify what payment covers: reviewing the source pack, creating the sample, explaining key choices, and completing the agreed follow-up change. State whether the work is evaluation-only. If you later want to publish it, discuss the necessary permissions and compensation rather than treating the audition as a free production asset.

A platform can have its own funding and acceptance procedure. Upwork, for example, describes fixed-price milestones with defined work, payment, and approval mechanics. Those mechanics apply to that platform; a direct engagement needs its own clear arrangement. [Upwork's milestone guidance](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/44564821903763-Understanding-milestones-on-fixed-price-contracts).

Give candidates a real deadline with a time zone and an agreed start. If you change the brief, pause the comparison and communicate the amendment consistently. Record delivery problems separately from editing defects so an administrative misunderstanding does not silently become a craft judgment.

## Review the sample with observable anchors

Watch the edits without candidate names when practical. Ask a second reviewer to watch independently before discussing impressions. The aim is to identify concrete differences, not manufacture a precise score from subjective taste.

Use this audition sheet as a starting point:

| Dimension | Evidence to look for | Note to record |
|---|---|---|
| Selection | Necessary explanation survives the cut | Which essential idea was preserved or lost? |
| Continuity | The viewer can follow the action | Where does context disappear? |
| Intelligibility | Speech and on-screen evidence remain understandable | What required a replay? |
| Restraint | Graphics or music serve the explanation | Which addition helps or distracts? |
| Judgment | Choices have a coherent reason | What tradeoff did the candidate explain? |

For each row, choose “ready,” “needs a bounded change,” or “does not meet this task.” Add a timestamp and a reason. Avoid arbitrary weights unless your team can explain why one dimension should dominate.

Read the candidate's decision notes after watching. A thoughtful explanation may reveal a limitation in your source material, but it should not erase an obvious viewing problem. Equally, an unfamiliar stylistic choice should not be treated as a failure merely because it differs from your preferred reference.

## Request one realistic change

The follow-up should test adaptability, not endurance. Use a change that could occur in normal work: replace the opening claim, shorten an explanation while retaining a dependency, or remove a visual that a stakeholder cannot approve.

Give the same change to each candidate if it makes sense for both edits. When the edits differ substantially, choose changes of comparable scope and explain that distinction in the decision record. Keep the task inside the paid boundary you agreed.

Observe the response before judging the new file. Did the editor notice a conflict? Did they ask a useful question? Did they explain that the requested change would weaken continuity? Collaboration includes identifying a problem with the instruction, not just executing it quickly.

For the Juniper Desk example, remove the screen that displays a prerequisite. A strong response could request a replacement, rearrange the explanation, or flag that the result would otherwise become misleading. Document the action and its effect. Do not turn every audition into a hidden test of whether the candidate agrees with you.

## Make the hiring decision and close the loop

Compare the finished sample, change response, stated availability, and actual commercial proposal. Keep those categories separate. Someone may be the best editor for the sample and still lack capacity for the recurring schedule you need.

Write a short decision note covering the strongest evidence, remaining uncertainty, and next assignment. If the result is mixed, identify one specific issue that a small first commission can resolve. Avoid expanding the audition indefinitely in search of certainty.

Tell unsuccessful candidates that the evaluation is complete and arrange payment according to the agreed terms. Retain only the materials your arrangement allows. Do not circulate their work internally as an approved creative direction without considering the permitted use.

For the selected editor, transfer what the test taught you into the first real brief: useful source context, recurring ambiguity, preferred decision notes, and the appropriate review window. The test has done its job when it reduces uncertainty about the working relationship and produces a concrete, bounded next commission.
