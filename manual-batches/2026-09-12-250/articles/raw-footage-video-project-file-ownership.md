Agree separately on copyright, delivery of raw footage, editable project files, and third-party components before commissioning a video. Receiving a final export does not answer every ownership question. Use a material-by-material schedule to specify the proposed rights, files, dependencies, delivery timing, and unresolved exceptions, with qualified advice where the agreement is unclear.

## Split the ownership question into practical parts

When a buyer asks “Do we own the video?” they may mean several things: permission to publish it, freedom to edit it later, receipt of all camera originals, access to the editing project, or exclusive rights in newly created material.

Ask which outcomes the business actually needs. A company planning to change product details later may need a usable project and supporting assets. Another may only need specified publication rights and an archive of approved masters.

The [Copyright Office's presentation of Section 202](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html) distinguishes ownership of a material object from copyright in the work embodied in it. That is the central reason to discuss file delivery and rights separately.

Do not turn this distinction into a verdict about a particular vendor agreement. It is a prompt to identify missing terms and obtain appropriate review before signing or relying on an assumed entitlement.

## Inventory what the production will create and use

List final exports, camera originals, recorded audio, project files, graphics source files, and any custom music or narration. Then distinguish newly created material from pre-existing templates, licensed stock, fonts, plugins, and other third-party components.

Identify who supplies each category. A production company may hire a camera operator, motion designer, composer, or editor, while your company supplies logos and product screenshots. Ask how the proposed agreement addresses those contributions.

“Raw footage” should be defined in the conversation. Does the buyer mean all camera-card contents, selected usable takes, synchronized media, proxies, or only clips appearing in the final cut? Those are different deliverables with different preparation and storage needs.

Ask for a sample inventory from a comparable handoff, with confidential information removed. It can reveal what the vendor normally means by project delivery without requiring you to accept that default.

## Build the deliverable-and-rights schedule

Use this original schedule as a briefing aid for the supplier and adviser. Leave legal conclusions to the actual agreement and applicable rules.

| Material | Business need | Question about rights | Question about delivery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Approved master | Publish in named placements | What use is granted and when? | Which export formats arrive? |
| Camera originals | Re-edit selected material later | What permissions and restrictions apply? | All takes or selected files, and how organized? |
| Editing project | Change text and timing | May another authorized editor modify it? | Application version and linked dependencies? |
| Custom graphics | Update branding | What rights apply to newly created work? | Source files and editable controls? |
| Library components | Keep the finished production usable | Which third-party terms remain relevant? | Evidence, replacements or permitted assets? |
| Archive copy | Recover work after a loss | What may each party retain? | Retention period and retrieval procedure? |

Add the responsible supplier, agreed delivery date, any preparation fee, and unresolved exception. Avoid a single “ownership included” checkbox that conceals the differences between rows.

Have the editor identify which promised files are necessary for the future change you actually anticipate. A large archive is not automatically useful if it lacks the project structure or dependencies needed to reopen the work.

## Treat work-for-hire language as a review question

The [Copyright Office's Circular 30](https://copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf) explains that work-for-hire status depends on qualifying circumstances and requirements, including an express signed agreement for qualifying commissioned work. It is not a rule that paying a production invoice automatically makes the buyer the author.

Ask the vendor what legal arrangement it proposes for newly created material and how contributions from its personnel and subcontractors are addressed. If it relies on work-for-hire language, request qualified review of whether that arrangement fits the actual facts.

Also ask about any separate transfer or license language and when the intended rights take effect. Do not assume a clause concerning final payment answers what may be done with drafts, raw recordings, or pre-existing components.

The goal is to make the transaction explicit, not to force every production into the same contract form. Complex ownership, cross-border, performer, or third-party questions deserve advice tied to the actual commission.

## Specify a usable handoff and its limits

Ask the vendor to name the application version, file formats, plugins, fonts, linked media, and any materials it cannot transfer. A project file alone may be small because it points to assets elsewhere.

Define what substitutes are acceptable when a dependency cannot be supplied. That might mean a rendered graphic plus an editable alternative, or documentation identifying an asset the business must license separately. Agree those limits before treating source delivery as a complete promise of future editability.

Include a short acceptance exercise: an authorized editor opens the package and performs one agreed change. This tests usability, not legal ownership, and should occur separately from the rights review.

Record any costs for organizing, transferring, or retrieving source material. Use the vendor's actual quote; there is no universal rule that every file is automatically included or always costs extra.

## Close the agreement with an exception list

Before signing, review every unresolved row with the supplier. State what is included, excluded, dependent on another license, or subject to further advice. If an exception prevents the business's intended future use, resolve it or reconsider the purchase.

Agree the retention arrangement too. Ask how long the vendor keeps project materials, how retrieval works, and what notice or confirmation accompanies the end of retention. Do not rely on the assumption that a production company keeps everything indefinitely.

At final delivery, reconcile the package against the schedule and record missing items without equating possession with clearance. Keep rights evidence and technical handoff evidence together but distinguish their purposes.

A sound purchase leaves the business able to explain what it may do, what it will receive, and what remains dependent on someone else. That is substantially clearer than a broad promise to “own everything” whose practical and legal meaning has never been discussed.
