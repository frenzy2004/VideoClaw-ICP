Support competing portfolio companies by separating assignments, source material and decision context before shared media work begins. A restricted folder alone cannot prevent an editor from carrying one company's unpublished plans into another brief. Use an assignment-boundary sheet and a conflict-escalation exercise to decide who can handle each task and what must remain separate.

## Identify the overlap that matters

Two companies can share an industry without competing for the same audience or opportunity. Conversely, companies in different categories may be preparing similar announcements or working with the same customer. Ask what overlap affects this particular media assignment rather than labeling an entire company relationship as safe or unsafe.

The platform does not need a dossier of confidential competitive strategy. It needs enough context to identify relevant boundaries: the target audience, unreleased subject matter, involved customers, planned timing and people already exposed to sensitive material. Keep the record limited to what the coordination decision requires.

Distinguish three situations. A routine public biography may need ordinary company separation. Two unpublished launch narratives may require separate staff and reviewers. A shared event involving competitors may need an agreed neutral brief and a clear way to raise concerns.

This is an operational method for media work, not legal advice about confidentiality, competition or investment duties. Where an assignment raises those questions, route them to the organization's designated advisers and follow the resulting instructions.

## Separate files, people and reusable knowledge

File access is one boundary. [Google's shared-drive guidance](https://support.google.com/a/users/answer/12380484?hl=en) shows that access depends on roles and sharing paths. Use the controls appropriate to your system and test them; do not assume a company folder name prevents cross-company visibility.

Staff assignment is another boundary. An editor who has read an unreleased customer story may not be the right person to shape a competitor's positioning that week. Removing the editor's file access does not erase what they already learned. Record whether the assignment requires reassignment, narrower scope or a decision by the platform lead.

Reusable process knowledge is different from company-specific content. A generic interview checklist can be shared without importing an unpublished customer quote. A lesson about asking clearer questions may be reusable; a distinctive launch sequence or private customer objection may not be appropriate to transfer.

Non-attribution also has limits. The [Chatham House Rule](https://www.chathamhouse.org/about-us/chatham-house-rule) addresses direct and indirect identification. Removing a company name is not a sufficient test for whether a private example can be reused in another company's brief. Check the actual sharing instructions and the identifying context.

## Populate an assignment-boundary sheet

Imagine two fictional companies, Alder and Beacon, preparing media about a similar workflow. The platform has one shared coordinator and two editors. It needs to decide assignments without revealing either brief to the other company.

| Work item | Material involved | Proposed staffing | Boundary | Escalation trigger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alder launch interview | Unreleased customer example | Editor Maya plus Alder reviewer | Alder-only source packet and review thread | Maya is later asked to shape Beacon's competing story |
| Beacon product introduction | Unreleased positioning | Editor Joel plus Beacon reviewer | Beacon-only brief and references | Request to compare against Alder's private draft |
| Shared event introduction | Public company descriptions | Coordinator plus both company contacts separately | Neutral public facts only | One company requests preferential framing |
| General interview checklist | Platform-created method | Shared resource owner | No private examples or copied company wording | A proposed example can identify a private participant |

Add the assignment owner, confirmed access, permitted source set and decision reference. The sheet should point to restricted briefs rather than reproduce their contents in a widely shared register.

The coordinator can see that a conflict exists without giving every contributor access to both narratives. Where even the assignment title is sensitive, use a neutral reference and keep the explanatory note within the appropriate audience.

## Rehearse a concrete escalation

Suppose Maya has reviewed Alder's unpublished interview. A partner then asks her to improve Beacon's introduction because she now “knows the category.” Maya should not need to explain Alder's private content to justify pausing the assignment.

Use a short escalation message: “I have recent exposure to restricted material that may overlap this brief. Please confirm a separate editor or a narrower task before I review it.” The platform lead can inspect the relevant boundary record privately and choose a next step.

In this fictional case, Joel retains Beacon's narrative assignment. Maya may still help with a neutral formatting check using only a supplied final text, if the responsible lead determines that this narrower task fits the agreed boundary. That is a documented scope decision, not a universal rule that formatting work is always safe.

Record the outcome and affected deadlines. If reassignment creates a delay, tell the company about the practical change without revealing the competitor's plans. “We are changing the reviewer to maintain assignment separation” can explain the action without disclosing confidential context.

## Review shared outputs for hidden comparisons

A shared newsletter, event introduction or portfolio learning resource can mix company contexts even when production folders are separate. Review the combined output for implied comparisons, unequal descriptions and clues drawn from private briefings.

Use the same public-source standard for each company in a neutral introduction. If one company has confirmed a current description and another has not, do not fill the missing information with knowledge from a private meeting. Ask for approved wording or use a narrower factual introduction.

Watch for examples that reveal a company through a combination of details. An unnamed company with a distinctive customer, launch month and founder background may still be recognizable. If the example is unnecessary, remove it. If it is essential, seek the appropriate authorization and review rather than claiming that a pseudonym solves the problem.

Keep review responsibilities explicit. Each company checks its own facts; the platform editor checks the combined framing. Neither role automatically grants access to the other's confidential materials. The final shared output should be reviewable without reopening private production histories.

## Maintain a usable boundary record

Revisit assignments when staff change, a brief expands, an announcement becomes public or a new company enters the same project. Boundaries are tied to current work and information, so an old “cleared” label should not carry forward indefinitely.

Record what changed and who accepted the revised assignment. Preserve unresolved questions as holds. Do not use a blanket checkbox to imply that a complicated company relationship has been permanently resolved.

After the project, review whether the escalation route worked. Could contributors raise a concern without exposing sensitive details? Did the platform make a timely staffing decision? Did a shared output require information that should never have entered its brief?

The useful result is a small, maintained assignment sheet and a practiced way to pause questionable work. It helps a multi-company media team make concrete staffing and source-use decisions while keeping broader legal and governance questions with the people responsible for them.
