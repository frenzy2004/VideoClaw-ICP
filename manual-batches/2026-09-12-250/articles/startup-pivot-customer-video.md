Explain a startup pivot to customers by showing what continues, what changes, and what remains undecided. Record the founder's reasoning alongside a simple commitment comparison, then give each affected customer group a clear next step. Customers should finish the video knowing how their work is affected, not merely understanding why the company wants a new direction.

## Start with the customer's existing relationship

A pivot may feel like a strategic breakthrough inside the company and an unwelcome uncertainty outside it. Customers have already made decisions based on what you offered. Begin with those decisions rather than the story you want to tell about the future.

List the commitments customers may rely on: access, support, data handling, integrations, pricing arrangements, and any announced transition dates. Ask the responsible owners to verify what actually changes. Do not infer continuity from the fact that the product still opens.

[PostHog has described changes of direction in its own early history](https://newsletter.posthog.com/p/read-this-before-you-pivot). That first-party context shows that a pivot can be part of a startup's development; it does not establish that any particular pivot is good for existing customers.

Your recording has a narrower task than justifying the whole strategy. It must explain the consequences accurately and make it possible for customers to act.

Write the opening from their perspective: “Here is what our change in focus means for your current workspace.”

## Build a visible commitment comparison

Create three areas on a plain slide or document: continuing, changing, and unresolved. Use customer-facing statements rather than internal team names or product bets.

For a fictional startup called Draftline, imagine the company is shifting from broad project planning toward review and approval workflows. The continuing area says existing project records remain available under the current agreement. The changing area says new development will focus on review steps rather than additional planning views. The unresolved area says the team has not decided whether a proposed calendar feature will proceed.

Every statement in this example is invented. For your own comparison, verify each condition and avoid copying its reassurance.

Add an owner and a checked date to your private version. Keep the public visual readable, but preserve the internal evidence behind it. If a commitment is conditional, put the condition next to the statement.

Do not place a future aspiration in the continuing column. “We remain committed to excellent service” is not a substitute for explaining actual support availability.

## Explain the reason without rewriting the past

Once customers understand the immediate consequences, explain what led to the decision. Use concrete observations your team can support. Distinguish those observations from the judgment you made.

A useful structure is: “We observed this pattern; we considered these options; we chose this direction; here is the trade-off.” You do not need to disclose private customer information or every internal debate to provide a meaningful explanation.

Avoid pretending the previous product was never important. Customers may still depend on it. Calling it a distraction can make their work sound disposable. You can acknowledge that the earlier direction helped you learn while taking responsibility for the change.

In the fictional Draftline case, the founder might say: “Our strongest fit appears to be the review stage, so we are concentrating new work there. That means we will not pursue every planning feature we previously considered.” This is clearer than claiming the company is simply becoming more focused with no downside.

If you do not yet know the full transition plan, say which questions remain open and when customers can expect the next communication.

## Record two scenes with different jobs

Use a direct-address opening for accountability, followed by the commitment comparison for explanation. [Wistia's presentation guidance describes combining the speaker and supporting visuals](https://wistia.com/blog/presentation-videos). The two-scene structure here is an original application to customer pivot communication.

Scene one establishes the change and acknowledges the customer's position. The founder speaks directly: “We are narrowing our product focus. I want to explain what that means for the work you already do with us.”

Scene two displays the comparison. Narrate one row at a time, keeping the relevant condition visible. Do not read every word mechanically; explain why the row matters and where the customer can find the written detail.

Return to direct address for the next step if that feels clearer. Keep the recording free from celebratory montage that competes with difficult information. A customer facing disruption does not need to be persuaded that the announcement is exciting.

Record dates, eligibility, and commitments carefully. Use a script for sensitive wording even if the surrounding explanation is conversational.

## Give different customer groups different next steps

One generic call to action may not serve everyone. Separate customers whose current work continues, those who need to discuss a transition, and those waiting for a feature that may no longer proceed.

For the fictional Draftline video, a continuing customer might need no immediate action. A customer whose planned workflow depends on an uncommitted feature should contact a named team before making further plans. A customer already discussing a transition needs the agreed support route, not a public form with no context.

Put these paths in the accompanying written note. The video should explain the distinctions, while the note carries the exact destinations and dates.

Do not ask customers to celebrate, share, or buy something before giving them the practical information they need. A founder's communication should reduce ambiguity, even if it cannot remove disappointment.

Check that the people receiving replies know the same boundaries as the video. A clear recording followed by contradictory support answers creates a new problem.

## Test the video for customer comprehension

Ask a reviewer to answer three questions after watching: What changes for my current work? What should I do now? What has not been decided? Compare their answers with the approved commitment sheet.

If they remember the strategic rationale but cannot answer those questions, revise the sequence. Put consequences earlier, simplify the comparison, or separate a complex customer path into its own written explanation.

Check the visual and narration together. A row that says “under review” should not be accompanied by confident language about when it will ship. Keep the uncertainty consistent across both.

Save the fact sheet, final recording, and written note together. When the plan changes, update the customer-facing explanation and identify what has changed since this version.
