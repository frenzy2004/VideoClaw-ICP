Hire a drone videographer by checking both the named pilot and the feasibility of the proposed flight. Request credential evidence, location-specific planning, suitable work samples, and a clear fallback if aerial capture cannot proceed. Treat certification as one part of qualification, and leave flight safety and permission decisions with the qualified operator.

## Explain why the footage needs to be aerial

Start with the view you need to communicate. An aerial shot might establish a site's scale, show a relationship between buildings, or reveal a process that is difficult to understand from the ground. Describe that purpose before asking for a particular maneuver.

Separate essential information from visual preference. If a ground-level or elevated fixed-camera shot can communicate the same fact, keep it as a possible substitute. This gives you a useful fallback without making the aerial commission meaningless.

Provide the exact proposed location, intended date window, approximate recording needs, and relevant site contacts. Mention people, vehicles, nearby activity, and any access limitations you know about. Do not assume the supplier can determine all of those conditions from a street address.

Ask for complete relevant samples. A dramatic landscape reel does not necessarily demonstrate the ability to frame a constrained business site or follow a carefully bounded production brief. Identify what the operator personally handled in the sample and what the proposed commission includes beyond flight.

## Verify the named operator's evidence

For work proposed under Part 107, ask who will serve as the remote pilot and request evidence that matches that person's identity. The FAA describes a Remote Pilot Certificate requirement and recurrent training needed to maintain aeronautical knowledge recency. [FAA remote-pilot guidance](https://www.faa.gov/uas/commercial_operators/become_a_drone_pilot).

Record the documents reviewed and any questions the supplier needs to resolve. A company logo, drone ownership, or an attractive portfolio is not the same evidence as the named person's qualification. Ask who substitutes if that person becomes unavailable.

Keep the scope of your check clear. You are gathering facts for supplier qualification, not conducting your own aviation licensing assessment. If a document is unclear, ask the operator to explain it or direct you to the appropriate official verification route.

Confirm who provides the aircraft, supporting equipment, and any additional crew the operator considers necessary. Ask how the production contact will communicate creative requests without interfering with flight responsibilities. That conversation should establish a workable relationship between your desired footage and the operator's authority over the operation.

## Ask for location-specific feasibility

A qualified pilot still needs to evaluate the particular flight. Request a written feasibility response that identifies the location, intended operating area, date assumptions, and any constraints that could affect the requested footage.

The FAA states that flights near airports in controlled airspace require authorization and may be subject to altitude and other operating limitations. Its guidance describes authorization routes including LAANC and FAA DroneZone. [FAA guidance on flying near airports](https://www.faa.gov/uas/getting_started/where_can_i_fly/airspace_restrictions/flying_near_airports).

Do not translate a general map screenshot into a conclusion that filming is permitted. Ask the operator which checks and permissions apply to the proposed activity, what evidence is already available, and what remains pending. Site access and other applicable restrictions may need separate confirmation.

Set a date for resolving feasibility before you commit related production resources. If an authorization, property agreement, or operational condition is uncertain, make the booking status reflect that uncertainty. A conditional proposal is more useful than a confident promise that conceals unresolved requirements.

## Build the operator evidence packet

Use a compact packet that connects the supplier, the location, and the footage. Keep sensitive credential information accessible only to the people who need it.

| Packet item | What to request | What it helps establish |
|---|---|---|
| Named pilot | Identity and relevant credential evidence | Who is responsible for the operation |
| Knowledge recency | Applicable evidence and explanation | Whether the operator has addressed current qualification requirements |
| Proposed location | Map, address and intended area | The actual site under review |
| Flight feasibility | Required checks, permissions and pending items | Whether the requested footage is realistically available |
| Creative sample | Comparable complete work and role attribution | Whether the supplier can capture the required view |
| Production interface | Contact, communication method and decision authority | How creative requests reach the operator |
| Fallback | Alternate date or substitute footage | What the purchase delivers if plans change |

Mark each item supplied, pending, or not applicable with a reason. Avoid a numerical score that makes several minor strengths outweigh a critical unresolved requirement.

Ask the supplier to correct your summary. The packet should document a shared understanding of the proposed work, not suggest that your procurement form overrides the operator's assessment or official requirements.

## Agree the commercial response to a no-fly decision

Discuss adverse weather, changing site activity, unavailable access, and other conditions the operator identifies. Ask who decides whether aerial work can proceed and how that decision is communicated to the rest of the production team.

The operator should be able to decline an unsafe or impermissible operation without pressure to satisfy a shot list. Your commercial agreement should explain what follows: a replacement date, a changed scope, a ground-footage alternative, or another negotiated outcome.

For a fictional example, a company wants an overhead view of a facility. The operator identifies unresolved conditions for the proposed date. The buyer can make that shot optional, reserve a later approved session, or commission ground footage that explains the same layout. The right choice depends on how essential the aerial view is.

Record actual cancellation, postponement, and additional-work terms. Do not assume they match a location booking or the wider production contract. Ask the relevant advisers to review unclear commercial language rather than importing a supposed standard fee from an online example.

## Confirm the capture and delivery boundaries

Before the session, confirm the approved creative priorities and the operator's current feasibility position. Ask what changes would require another review, such as a different takeoff location, new timing, or additional activity around the site.

Specify the footage deliverables in terms the editor can use: expected files, shot descriptions, recording settings where relevant, and the delivery route. Agree whether the commission includes raw material, a selected set of clips, an edited sequence, or a combination. Handle rights and reuse through the actual agreement.

On the day, route requests through the named production contact. If the plan changes, let the operator assess the implications before the team assumes the original arrangement still applies. Capture useful ground alternatives when those are part of the agreed scope.

After delivery, evaluate whether the footage communicates the intended information and whether the supplier met the agreed process. Keep the evidence packet with the commission record. A sound purchase connects creative fit, a qualified operator, and a feasible specific operation without confusing any one of those with the others.
