Make a smaller investor demo by protecting the few details that prove the product works, then comparing exports against those details. File size alone is a poor finishing target. A compact recording that destroys an input value, result label or spoken qualification has lost something more important than resolution.

## Set a delivery constraint and a proof floor

Ask where the recording must go and what actually limits delivery. A form may impose a file-size ceiling; an investor may need a downloadable copy; an event operator may request a particular file. Record the real requirement instead of inventing a universal “small enough” target.

Separate size from duration. Removing an unnecessary introduction can reduce the amount of material without degrading every frame. But cutting evidence, qualifications or genuine waiting time can change the meaning of the demonstration. Treat editorial shortening and compression as different decisions.

Define a proof floor: the minimum information that must survive. For a fictional inventory product, it might include the selected item identifier, an entered quantity, the confirmation message and the corrected stock count. Name those details before touching export settings.

Keep the approved master unchanged. Make delivery copies with distinct filenames and write down which master they came from. Repeatedly exporting an already reduced copy makes it harder to know whether a defect came from the source, an earlier export or your latest setting.

## Select representative stress scenes

Choose scenes that expose different weaknesses. A static screen with small text tests fine detail. Scrolling tests motion. A gradient or dark panel can reveal visible degradation. A short spoken qualification tests whether the audio remains intelligible.

Do not evaluate only the attractive opening frame. The decisive result might appear for a short interval near the end, where compressed text becomes difficult to read during motion. Mark its timestamp in the source master.

Create this original export comparison card before making candidates:

| Proof scene | What must survive | Candidate A | Candidate B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Item selection | Exact fictional identifier | Observed reading | Observed reading |
| Quantity entry | Entered value and field context | Observed reading | Observed reading |
| Result update | Changed count and status | Observed reading | Observed reading |
| Spoken limitation | Complete qualification | Listener repeat-back | Listener repeat-back |
| Fast interaction | Action-to-result continuity | Observed behavior | Observed behavior |

Use actual observations such as “reviewer read 18 as 16,” not just “looks soft.” A concrete reading error tells you why that candidate is unsuitable. Record the viewing size and player so the comparison remains interpretable.

## Make controlled candidates from the master

Change one main export variable at a time where your editor permits it. Compare a lower resolution against the same framing, or another quality setting against the same dimensions. Avoid changing crop, frame rate, audio and compression simultaneously, then guessing which change caused the defect.

[Apple’s QuickTime export guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/quicktime-player/export-movies-qtp20e395859/10.5/mac/26) distinguishes H.264 and HEVC options and notes that QuickTime exports MOV, not MP4. Read the actual exporter’s choices; a filename extension and a quality label do not describe the same property.

When the recording sits inside a Windows PowerPoint deck, [Microsoft’s media-compression guidance](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/powerpoint/compress-your-media-files) warns that compression removes embedded subtitles and alternate audio tracks. That operation is not available in PowerPoint for Mac or the web. Do not shrink the deck without checking what the process discards.

Name candidates by an understandable revision, such as inventory-review-A and inventory-review-B. Keep a private note of their settings, byte size and source master. The recipient needs one approved file, not a folder of unexplained experiments.

## Compare what the investor receives

Open each candidate in the intended delivery player at a realistic picture size. Put the same proof scene on screen and ask a reviewer to read the decisive details. Do not supply the correct values first; that turns the exercise into recognition rather than observation.

Listen to the qualification and result narration at a normal playback level. A smaller file is not acceptable if the viewer must increase the volume dramatically or guess a clipped word. Compare the beginning and end of spoken phrases as well as their middle.

Inspect transitions into the result. A still frame can look fine while the label becomes unreadable during movement. If the claim depends on seeing a value change, test the change itself rather than a paused final screenshot.

When every small candidate fails, revisit framing in the source project. Enlarging the necessary interface region before export can be more useful than trying another aggressive setting. Preserve enough surrounding context to show that the selected input and displayed result belong to the same task.

## Use transfer arithmetic as planning, not a promise

You can estimate an ideal transfer time from file size and an assumed connection rate. For example, a fictional 24 MB file contains about 192 megabits using decimal units. At an assumed sustained 8 megabits per second, the arithmetic gives 24 seconds before overhead.

That is an illustration, not an observed delivery result or a claim about investor bandwidth. Real transfers include conditions the simple calculation omits. Use the estimate to compare candidates, then test the actual delivery path where you can.

Record actual upload and opening behavior separately from the estimate. Did the upload complete? Was processing required before playback? Did the recipient view the intended file? A successful local export says nothing about whether the hosted copy is ready.

Do not adopt a smaller candidate simply because the estimated saving looks attractive. If it saves little practical friction while making the product result harder to inspect, retain the clearer version or agree on another delivery route.

## Select one copy and preserve its evidence

Approve the smallest tested candidate that meets both the delivery constraint and the proof floor. Your decision note should name the file, the source master, the test environment and any known limitation. “Candidate B: all four required values readable in the intended player” is a useful observation.

Recheck subtitles, audio tracks and timing after any further processing. If the hosting service creates its own playback version, inspect that received version too. Do not assume the file you uploaded and the image the investor watches are identical in quality.

Keep the rejected candidates out of the sharing folder to avoid an accidental send. Preserve the master privately so you can make a different delivery copy later without starting from the reduced export.
