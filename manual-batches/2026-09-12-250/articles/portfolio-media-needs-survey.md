Ask portfolio companies about media-support needs by connecting every survey question to a decision the platform can actually make. Distinguish current tasks, missing help and reasons for non-participation. Use a question-to-decision map and an honest response-coverage summary so the loudest respondents do not automatically define the next program.

## Start with decisions the survey can inform

A needs survey should help choose a bounded action, such as which office-hour topics to offer, which submission instruction to clarify or which existing resource needs a better entry point. It should not become a general request for every service a company might someday want.

Write the decisions before the questions. If the platform cannot provide a requested service or change a relevant policy, be clear about that boundary. Asking about an unavailable service without context can create an expectation that it will be offered.

The [UK Analysis Function's questionnaire guidance](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/questionnaire-design-guidance/) emphasizes testing comprehension and related questions in context. For a small portfolio survey, use that principle to check whether people interpret “media help” and the response options as intended.

Keep needs assessment separate from program outcome evaluation. This exercise asks what companies are trying to do and where support is missing. It does not establish whether the existing program caused growth, improved fundraising or produced a financial return.

## Define the eligible companies and response unit

Decide who should respond and what one response represents. A company-level needs view may require one nominated contact who can consult colleagues. Individual perspectives can also be useful, but they should not be silently counted as separate companies.

Record the eligible population from the program's current company list. Include companies that have not used media support, not only people who attended recent workshops. Non-participation may reflect lack of need, poor awareness, unsuitable timing or an inaccessible process.

Explain the purpose, intended use and visibility of responses. Do not promise anonymity if company identifiers or follow-up details make respondents identifiable. Collect the minimum information needed for the decision and offer a separate route for sensitive issues.

[The Analysis Function's user-centred survey guidance](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/a-user-centred-design-approach-to-surveys/) considers respondent needs, question flow and pre-testing. Apply that by making the request short, understandable and usable in the format companies will actually receive.

## Build a question-to-decision map

The following is an original draft instrument for a portfolio media program. It is a starting point to test, not a validated questionnaire or evidence of actual company demand.

| Draft question | Response approach | Decision it informs | Interpretation limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Which media task are you actively trying to complete in the next planning period? | Choose relevant tasks, with none and a short write-in | Select support topics tied to current work | Interest is not a commitment to attend |
| What is the main obstacle to completing that task? | One primary obstacle plus optional explanation | Distinguish missing expertise, inputs, approval or capacity | The company may face several obstacles |
| Which existing support route, if any, have you tried? | Named routes plus none and not aware of them | Identify awareness and access gaps | Use alone does not prove usefulness |
| If you did not use support, what best explains that? | Not needed, unaware, timing, fit, access or write-in | Choose a targeted participation repair | Non-users are not a single group |
| What would be a useful next step from the platform? | Bounded options the program can consider | Prioritize a feasible intervention | A preference does not guarantee delivery |

Add a brief invitation for a follow-up conversation separately from the substantive answers. A company should not need to volunteer for an interview merely to submit its needs.

Avoid asking several questions in one item. “Do you need faster and better video support?” combines timing and quality while suggesting the desired answer. Ask about the actual task and obstacle instead.

## Test wording with realistic respondents

Before sending the survey broadly, ask a few people from the intended audience to explain how they understand each question and how they choose an answer. Include someone who has not used the program so familiar internal terms do not pass unchallenged.

Test the full flow, including instructions, branching and the final screen. A question about obstacles may be irrelevant to someone who selected no current task. Provide a sensible route for that response rather than forcing them to invent a problem.

Observe where response options overlap. A company may interpret “capacity” as a lack of internal time and “production support” as the same thing. Clarify the distinction only if it matters to the platform's decision; otherwise simplify the options.

Check that the instrument works on the devices and access routes respondents use. Keep any expected completion-time statement grounded in the tested instrument rather than an optimistic guess. If the survey feels burdensome, remove questions that do not change a decision.

## Interpret responses with coverage visible

Create a response-coverage summary before choosing an intervention. Record eligible companies, responding companies, duplicate responses resolved and groups not yet represented. Do not convert a convenience response set into a precise portfolio-wide demand estimate.

Consider a fictional example: twelve eligible companies receive the survey, seven provide company-level responses and five do not. Four respondents mention uncertainty about preparing media review requests. That supports investigating the review-request problem among those respondents; it does not establish that four-twelfths or a majority of the full portfolio needs the same intervention.

Read write-ins alongside selected options. A request for editing may reveal that the real obstacle is an unavailable approver. A request for a new workshop may reflect difficulty finding an existing recording. Keep these distinctions visible instead of counting keywords as service demand.

Look for missing perspectives and conduct a proportionate follow-up where appropriate. If non-users did not respond, state that limitation and avoid claiming the survey explains their behavior. A short conversation can clarify a gap without turning the exercise into endless research.

## Choose one action and close the loop

Connect the findings back to the original decisions. For the fictional responses, the platform might test a clearer review-request example and a short advisory session with interested companies. Label this as a proposed response to observed needs, not a promised improvement in business performance.

Document the evidence, uncertainty, selected action, owner and review point. Also record requests the platform will not act on now and why. A survey that produces no visible decision can make future participation harder to justify.

Tell companies what was learned at an appropriate level of aggregation and what will happen next. Avoid sharing identifiable complaints or ranking companies by engagement. Invite corrections if the interpretation missed an important constraint.

The finished work product is a tested question-to-decision map, a transparent account of response coverage and a bounded support action. It helps a portfolio media program listen before allocating effort while preserving the distinction between expressed needs, feasible services and demonstrated outcomes.
