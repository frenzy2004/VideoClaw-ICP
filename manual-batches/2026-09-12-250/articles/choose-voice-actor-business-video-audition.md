Choose a voice actor by testing the parts of your script that are hardest to perform, not by selecting the most impressive demo reel. Use a short, agreed audition to compare pronunciation, meaning, pace, and response to direction. Then book the recording arrangement and pickups that the actual script requires.

## Decide what the narration must accomplish

Before searching voices, state the listening job. A process explanation needs the audience to understand a sequence. A product introduction may need confidence without hype. A sensitive service message may need calm clarity rather than sales energy.

Describe the intended audience and delivery context without reducing the brief to stereotypes about age, gender, or accent. If a language variety or pronunciation convention matters, explain the audience requirement and how it will be evaluated.

Identify the script's real difficulties. These might include an invented product name, a technical abbreviation, a contrast between two similar terms, or a closing sentence that must land within a fixed picture duration.

Use existing demos to shortlist actors with a plausible fit. A reel is useful evidence of range, but it may not show how the actor handles your terminology, recording conditions, or direction. The audition should answer those remaining questions rather than repeat the reel's strongest material.

## Build a small, purposeful audition script

Select a short passage that combines a difficult name, an important contrast, and a complete ending. Remove confidential details that are not needed for evaluation. Tell the talent whether the text is final or a constructed test.

Here is an illustrative audition specimen for a fictional product, not a script to publish:

“LumaDock keeps the original file intact. You choose the version to share, not a replacement for your archive. Open the project, check the preview, and send the approved copy.”

Provide the intended pronunciation of the fictional name in writing and, when helpful, an authorized reference recording. Mark the meaning that matters: the contrast is between sharing a version and replacing an archive.

Ask for one principal read and, if agreed, one clearly different direction. Do not request a complete finished commercial disguised as an audition. Establish the scope, compensation, and permitted evaluation use with the talent or platform before the work starts.

## Use a listening ballot with observable evidence

Listen to auditions with the same script visible and at comparable playback levels. Keep background music out of the first comparison so production polish does not obscure performance.

This proposed ballot helps reviewers explain a preference rather than simply vote for a voice.

| Test | What to listen for | Record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product pronunciation | Name matches the supplied guide | Correct, unclear, or specific difference |
| Intelligibility | Important words are easy to distinguish | Word and timecode causing uncertainty |
| Meaning | Contrast falls on the intended idea | Meaning heard by the reviewer |
| Pace | Explanation has room to register | Rushed phrase or unnecessary pause |
| Ending | Final line resolves within the need | Timing and whether the delivery feels forced |
| Direction response | Alternate read changes the requested quality | What changed and what did not |

Have reviewers record their first understanding before discussing the performance as a group. If everyone hears a different meaning, examine the script as well as the actor.

Do not pretend the ballot produces an objective universal ranking. A candidate can be excellent but wrong for this particular explanation. The value is a defensible reason for choosing the person and a clear direction for the session.

## Decide whether live direction is worth buying

A self-directed recording may suit an approved script with straightforward pronunciation and a well-matched actor. A live session can help when emphasis is delicate, names are unfamiliar, or the decision-maker needs to hear alternatives immediately.

[Voices describes live directed sessions](https://www.voices.com/help/knowledge/faq/live-directed-sessions) as remote, real-time collaboration, with the quote covering narration, applicable licensing, and the session. Its guidance supports asking for those components separately; it does not establish a standard duration or fee.

Before booking, confirm the connection method, who records the final-quality audio, who attends, and who gives direction. A buyer listening through a call should not assume the call recording is the production master.

Choose one director and let other stakeholders send notes through that person. Read the script aloud beforehand, settle factual wording, and bring a pronunciation list. Paying for live collaboration will not resolve an internal disagreement about what the business wants to say.

## Define pickups and permitted use before recording

Ask the talent to distinguish performance corrections, pronunciation corrections against the supplied guide, and changes to an already approved script. Those categories may be treated differently in the actual quote.

List likely pickup needs without assuming unlimited availability. If a legal reviewer may change a sentence after recording, say so before hiring. Ask how additional sessions are scheduled, how matching recording conditions are handled, and how new work is priced.

Keep audition material separate from licensed final work. The [Voices client policy](https://www.voices.com/company/terms-and-policies/client-on-platform-policy) restricts using talent audition audio without payment or express approval and prohibits use beyond the job's scope. Follow the actual platform and talent agreement; do not infer publication rights from receiving a file.

Describe the intended placements and duration of use, and ask about any limitations. If synthetic reuse or voice-model creation is contemplated, raise it as a separate permission discussion rather than hiding it inside a generic narration order.

## Confirm the hire with a recording-ready brief

Send the selected actor one consolidated package: final script version, pronunciation guide, intended tone, audience context, required timing, session arrangements, delivery format, usage description, and agreed pickup terms.

Include the audition observations that influenced the decision. “Keep the restrained opening; make the archive contrast slightly clearer” is actionable. “Be more engaging” leaves the actor guessing about what to change.

On receipt, check the entire narration against the approved script. Listen for omitted words, inconsistent names, unwanted noises, and alternate takes that have not been clearly labeled. Test the selected recording under the actual picture and music before closing the job.

If an issue appears, identify whether it is a performance, script, recording, or edit problem. That classification helps the right person resolve it without unnecessary rerecording.

The final hiring decision should explain why this actor fits the script, what direction helps them succeed, and what the buyer has actually ordered. A purposeful audition makes those answers available before the full recording commitment.
