Capture a hardware investor demo with a wide view that preserves the physical setup and a close view that makes the decisive result inspectable. Record the actual input, action, and outcome from the same run. Keep scale, operator involvement, and test conditions visible enough that editing does not exaggerate the prototype.

## Define the physical event the camera must prove

Choose one operation, not an entire product tour. A fictional sorting device might move a marked block from an input tray to the intended output bin. That event is specific enough to plan a shot around and to verify afterward.

Write down what would make the event ambiguous. A hand might block the transfer. The destination bin might sit outside the frame. The operator might load a prepared result before the take. Each ambiguity is a camera-planning problem before it becomes an editing problem.

Identify the safe operating area with the person responsible for the hardware. Keep cameras, cables, lights, and observers outside that area. Do not alter protective equipment or approved operating procedures for a more dramatic angle.

Decide which conditions matter to interpretation: prototype version, object type, surface, operator setup, or environmental constraint. The clip does not need a laboratory report, but it should not imply an unrestricted real-world capability from a carefully prepared demonstration.

## Establish the setup and scale in a wide shot

Frame the whole operation so the viewer can understand where the input starts and where the output ends. Include the operator's relevant actions without making the founder's face the largest object in the picture.

Give the viewer an honest sense of scale. A familiar object or a labeled dimension can help when it is appropriate and accurate. Do not use a low camera angle that makes a tabletop prototype look like a larger installation.

Keep important connections visible when they affect the apparent capability. If the device receives power or control from an external system, avoid framing that suggests it is untethered or autonomous. Explain what is outside the frame when that context matters.

Lock down the camera position with a stable support. Walk through the complete motion before recording to check for occlusion. The operator should not discover during the decisive moment that their shoulder blocks the only view of the mechanism.

Use the wide view as the continuity record. Close-ups can add detail, but they should not replace the evidence connecting the physical input to its result.

## Add a close view for the decisive detail

Choose the close-up based on the viewer's inspection job. It may need to show a sensor reading, a connector engaging, a small indicator, or the exact object entering the correct bin. A close-up of the product logo adds little evidence.

Test focus and exposure through the full motion. A reflective surface or a bright display can make the important detail disappear even while the overall shot looks attractive. Review the actual recording rather than only the live camera preview.

[Apple's iPhone recording guide](https://support.apple.com/en-nz/guide/iphone/iph61f49e4bb/ios) notes that available formats and frame rates vary by model. Choose from the settings your capture device actually offers, and test them with the hardware's movement.

Do not assume the highest available setting is necessary. Prioritize a clear view, stable focus, and enough detail to inspect the result. If the device's behavior includes a brief light or display change, verify that the recording preserves it instead of relying on a still photograph taken afterward.

## Build a shot plan around one genuine run

Use a compact evidence shot list before recording. The following fictional plan is for the marked-block sorter:

| Shot | View | Required evidence | Continuity constraint |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Setup | Wide | Device, input tray, output bins and operator | Show actual preparation |
| Input | Close | Marked block before insertion | Same block used in the run |
| Action | Wide continuous | Block enters and device acts | No hidden handoff |
| Result | Close | Marked block in the intended bin | Capture the same run's outcome |
| Condition | Readable label or spoken note | Prototype and relevant test condition | Do not imply broader validation |

If you use two cameras, start both before the operation and establish a common visible or audible reference outside the active mechanism's path. Keep both running through completion.

[Apple documents synchronizing clips from the same take](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/guide/final-cut-pro/verc1fabc30/mac). Whatever editor you use, confirm synchronization against actual motion rather than assuming an automatic match is correct.

Label every source with a run identifier. Do not combine the wide view from one attempt with a successful close-up from another without disclosing the reconstruction.

## Preserve sound and operator work that explain the outcome

Decide whether hardware sound is evidence or merely background. A latch click may help establish engagement; a loud workshop fan may obscure the founder's explanation. Record a short test to discover the difference.

When sound matters, capture it close enough to be intelligible without placing equipment in an unsafe location. Do not add a stock click or motor sound to imply an event the recording did not capture. If narration replaces noisy location audio, retain the meaningful product sound where feasible and explain the operation accurately.

Show the operator's necessary intervention. Resetting a mechanism, positioning an object, or confirming a control can be part of the real workflow. Cutting all those actions may falsely imply a hands-off system.

If a run fails, keep the source record and decide what the final example can honestly establish. One successful demonstration can illustrate a capability under stated conditions; it is not a reliability test. Avoid percentages or generalized performance claims that the recording does not support.

## Edit for inspection and retain the wide proof take

Begin with enough setup to orient the viewer, then show the action and result. Use close-ups to clarify details while preserving the relationship to the continuous wide take. If a cut changes runs, say so rather than relying on visual similarity.

Hold the result long enough for the viewer to identify it. A fast montage can make hardware seem active while preventing anyone from inspecting the outcome. Remove decorative shots before shortening the decisive evidence.

Watch the final export with someone who did not attend the recording. Ask what the machine did, what the operator did, and which conditions they understood. If they infer a capability the prototype does not have, repair the framing or explanation.

Keep the uncut wide recording with the matching detail sources. That gives you a useful answer when an investor asks to see the whole operation.
