The most damaging Demo Day video mistakes are not expensive-camera problems. They are failures of clarity, evidence, privacy, readability, approval, and recovery: the viewer cannot explain what the company does, a claim has no support, the product screen cannot be read, sensitive data appears, captions change the meaning, the CTA points nowhere useful, or the only playback path fails. Audit those risks before polishing transitions.

This list is designed for a US accelerator founder reviewing an actual draft. It separates failures the team can detect directly from claims that require an authorized owner or professional review.

## Mistakes 1–3: the story does not create a clear decision

### 1. Opening with company history instead of the viewer's problem

A chronological origin story may matter to the founder, but it makes the viewer work to find the customer, problem, and change. Start with a concrete situation the intended viewer can recognize. Then identify what the company enables and why the proof matters now.

**Audit test:** After one viewing, ask someone outside the product team to complete: “This company helps ___ do ___ when ___.” Do not coach them. If they cannot answer, rewrite the opening rather than adding more footage.

### 2. Trying to include every pitch point

A video becomes a compressed data room when every market fact, feature, credential, customer segment, and future plan competes for attention. The [Y Combinator Demo Day pitch guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/) argues for clarity, truthful presentation, and a small number of memorable ideas. A recorded asset still needs that discipline.

Choose three or four points that move the viewer toward one next step. Put supporting detail in a linked deck, product note, or follow-up response. Editing faster does not solve an overloaded argument.

### 3. Using a CTA that does not match the audience

“Learn more” can hide an undecided next step. A Demo Day investor version might invite a follow-up conversation or direct the viewer to approved material. A customer version might request a product discussion. A public recap may simply explain where to follow the company.

**Audit test:** Name the person who owns the destination, the expected response, and the fallback if the link changes. Confirm that the spoken CTA, on-screen CTA, description, and destination all agree.

## Mistakes 4–6: product proof is weak or unsafe

### 4. Showing an interface tour instead of one proof path

Rapidly visiting many screens proves navigation, not value. Choose one user situation, show the action, and pause on the observable result. Narration should tell the viewer what changed and why that change matters.

Use a simple sequence:

1. Identify the clean starting state.
2. Name the user's intended action.
3. Show the action at a readable pace.
4. Hold on the result.
5. Connect the result to the pitch claim.

If the real workflow is too long or risky for an event recording, use an approved excerpt or stills and explain the boundary. Do not imply a continuous live workflow when the edit combines separate states.

### 5. Recording unreadable screens

The editor's monitor is not the viewing environment. Fine text, low-contrast controls, tiny cursors, and wide dashboards may disappear when projected, compressed, embedded, or watched on a phone.

**Audit test:** Export the draft, watch it at normal size on a phone, then project or cast it through the expected event path. Ask a reviewer to read every necessary label without pausing. Crop to the relevant region, increase deliberate pauses, and remove nonessential interface detail.

### 6. Exposing personal, customer, or confidential information

Notifications, browser tabs, account names, email addresses, tokens, customer records, internal URLs, chat windows, test credentials, or unreleased features can appear in a few frames. Blurring after capture is not a complete control because the source files still contain the data and a later edit can restore the wrong shot.

Prepare a dedicated recording environment:

- use synthetic or explicitly approved sample data;
- disable notifications and unrelated apps;
- close hidden tabs and windows;
- remove credentials and personal identifiers;
- confirm which product version may be shown;
- review the full frame, not only the cursor path;
- restrict access to raw captures that contain anything sensitive.

If the team is unsure whether information may be shown, stop and route the question to the responsible owner. This article cannot decide privacy, contractual, security, or disclosure obligations.

## Mistakes 7–8: evidence and language drift during editing

### 7. Publishing a claim without a source and approver

Numbers, comparisons, customer statements, market claims, product capabilities, security descriptions, and fundraising language need an internal source and responsible approver. The [FTC advertising FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business) explains the general principle that objective advertising claims need a reasonable basis. What substantiation is appropriate depends on the claim and context.

Create a claim ledger with exact wording, source, owner, approval state, and expiry. Editors should not strengthen a statement for rhythm. “Can,” “typically,” “up to,” “currently,” and “will” can carry materially different meanings.

**Audit test:** Read only the spoken and on-screen factual statements. For each one, point to the dated source and approval. If the source supports a narrower statement, narrow the script.

### 8. Letting the cut imply a result the source footage does not prove

Editing creates meaning through sequence. A reaction shot placed after a product action may imply the person reacted to that action. A result screen captured in a different environment may imply continuity. A chart shown under a customer story may imply attribution.

Keep scene IDs tied to source context. When a sequence is illustrative, label it accurately or narrate the boundary. Avoid fabricated dashboards, scores, testimonials, or “example results” that could be mistaken for real evidence.

## Mistakes 9–10: accessibility and approval are treated as export chores

### 9. Trusting automatic captions without review

The [YouTube Help page on automatic captions](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554?hl=en) warns that automatic captions can misrepresent speech and should be reviewed and edited. Startup names, technical terms, numbers, accents, and noisy product audio are especially vulnerable.

Review captions against the approved master. Check:

- company, founder, customer, and product names;
- every number and unit;
- negatives such as “not” and “without”;
- speaker changes when they affect understanding;
- line breaks over interfaces and lower-thirds;
- relevant non-speech audio;
- the CTA and URL.

Also watch the video muted. If a viewer needs an unlabeled visual cue or audio-only explanation to understand the proof, fix the content rather than relying only on transcript availability.

### 10. Collecting comments without one final approver

Five reviewers can produce five sets of valid notes and no release decision. Define review scopes: the product owner checks what is shown, claim owners check factual language, the founder checks story and voice, the accessibility reviewer checks captions and meaning, and the release owner confirms the package.

Use one version-specific approval record. “Looks good” in an old message is not approval of a later export. If a change touches approved language, footage, captions, CTA, or rights, send the affected area back through the relevant gate.

## Mistake 11: relying on one playback path

A browser, embedded player, cloud account, conferencing platform, venue network, adapter, or codec can fail even when the media file is sound. Do not turn a strong pitch into a live troubleshooting session.

Prepare three levels:

| Level | Asset | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Required event or presentation playback path | Run from the actual account, device, display, and audio route. |
| Backup | Approved local copy independent of the network | Open offline on the event device and verify picture, sound, and aspect ratio. |
| Recovery | Approved still sequence or short proof frame | Rehearse a concise narration that keeps the pitch moving. |

Label the approved master clearly and remove superseded cuts from the event folder. Confirm that the backup has the same captions, claim language, and CTA as the primary version.

## A practical risk heatmap for review

Score each mistake using two editorial questions, not invented incident statistics:

- **Consequence:** If this failure reaches the audience, could it confuse the decision, expose information, misstate evidence, block access, or stop playback?
- **Detectability:** Can a reviewer reliably find it by watching the exported package in the real delivery context?

| Review priority | Typical risks | Required action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stop release | Unsupported claims, sensitive data, unapproved rights, wrong master | Remove, correct, or obtain authorized approval. |
| Fix before event | Unreadable proof, incorrect captions, broken CTA, untested backup | Rework and repeat the real-context test. |
| Editorial improvement | Slow opening, excess detail, weak transition | Fix if it improves the one intended decision without destabilizing approved evidence. |

The categories are a VideoClaw editorial framework. They are not measured likelihoods, legal classifications, or evidence that these failures occur at a particular rate.

## The 20-minute release audit

Use this sequence on the exact export:

- [ ] Say the intended viewer and next step aloud.
- [ ] Restate the company and product change after one viewing.
- [ ] Match every factual statement to its source and approver.
- [ ] Inspect every product frame for private or confidential information.
- [ ] Read the product proof on a phone and the intended display.
- [ ] Watch muted and review captions word for word.
- [ ] Open the CTA from the published location.
- [ ] Confirm people, customer references, music, and third-party assets have recorded rights or permission.
- [ ] Play the local backup offline on the event device.
- [ ] Rehearse the still-frame recovery path.
- [ ] Record final approval against the exact version and package.
