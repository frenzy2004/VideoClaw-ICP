To make a founder pitch video without losing your voice, lock the facts but not every connective word. Start from an approved one-sentence company description, choose three or four memorable points, record short bullet-led founder takes, show one clean product proof sequence, and have the founder approve the final claims, captions, CTA, and edit. Natural delivery and evidence control can coexist.

The workflow below is designed for a US accelerator founder preparing an application, Demo Day, launch, or fundraising follow-up. Always apply the current format and distribution rules of the actual program or recipient.

## 1. Define the viewer, job, and next step

Before writing, complete one sentence:

> For **[specific viewer]**, this video explains **[company change]**, proves **[one fact or workflow]**, and asks them to **[one next step]**.

“Investors” is sometimes still too broad. A Demo Day attendee seeing the company for the first time, an accelerator reviewer, a sector specialist, and a person already in diligence have different context. Name the viewer situation without pretending to know an individual's private preferences.

Select one primary job:

- introduce the founders in a requested application format;
- create first-pass understanding before a meeting;
- support a Demo Day pitch with one product proof;
- answer a known follow-up question;
- prepare an approved explanation for launch or partner review.

The CTA should match that job. Test the destination and name the person responsible for the response. A video is not finished when the export completes; it is finished when the intended next step works.

## 2. Lock the factual spine, not a memorized monologue

Write an approved factual spine with four layers:

1. **Company description:** one sentence naming the customer, situation, and change.
2. **Memorable points:** three or four ideas the viewer should retain.
3. **Proof:** one product sequence or factual statement that supports a point.
4. **Next step:** one accurate invitation and destination.

The [Y Combinator Demo Day pitch guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/) emphasizes clarity, truthfulness, and memorable points. Its precise event format is not a universal rule for every video, but the communication discipline transfers well.

Now classify the language:

| Layer | Control level | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Locked wording | Exact approval required | Company description, metrics, customer names, product claims, availability, fundraising language, CTA |
| Bullet-led idea | Meaning stays fixed; phrasing can vary | Founder insight, customer situation, transition into proof |
| Natural connector | Founder chooses comfortable words | Short transitions, emphasis, personal but approved context |

This lets the founder speak instead of recite while preventing a spontaneous exaggeration from becoming part of the cut.

If the asset is a program application, follow the current instructions. The [Y Combinator application-video guidance](https://www.ycombinator.com/video/) is an example of a program distinguishing the founder application video from a separate product demo. Do not add production elements that conflict with the requested format.

## 3. Build a six-frame pitch-video outline

Use six conceptual frames. A frame can be a founder take, product capture, graphic, or combination; it is not a prescribed shot length.

### Frame 1: the customer situation

Name the specific person and costly or important moment. Avoid beginning with the company founding date, a broad market label, or a string of technical nouns.

**Prompt:** “When **[customer]** tries to **[job]**, they currently face **[specific friction or risk]**.”

### Frame 2: the company change

Explain what becomes different. Use approved wording that a non-specialist can repeat.

**Prompt:** “We help them **[new action or outcome]** by **[plain-language mechanism]**.”

### Frame 3: the founder insight

Show why the team sees the problem clearly. Use relevant experience or observed behavior without turning biography into the whole pitch.

**Prompt:** “We learned **[specific insight]** while **[relevant context]**, which changed how we approached the problem.”

### Frame 4: one product proof

Show a clean starting state, one action, and an observable result. Narrate what the viewer should notice. Label a controlled example accurately and never imply live customer usage from synthetic data.

### Frame 5: selected evidence

Use one or two approved facts appropriate to the viewer and stage. Every number, customer reference, comparison, or market statement needs a source, owner, and approval.

### Frame 6: the next step

State the CTA in language that matches the destination. Hold the URL or action long enough to understand, then test it in the released location.

The six frames are a VideoClaw editorial method, not an accelerator-mandated template. Remove or combine a frame when the actual instructions require a different format.

## 4. Tie factual scenes to source IDs

Create a source sheet before recording:

| Source ID | Scene or claim | Source | Owner | Approval state |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S-01 | One-sentence company description | Current company brief | Founder | Approved or revise |
| S-02 | Product workflow | Recorded build or release reference | Product owner | Approved or revise |
| S-03 | Operating fact or metric | Dated internal record with definition | Metric owner | Approved or omit |
| S-04 | Customer reference | Permission record and source | Authorized owner | Approved or omit |
| S-05 | CTA | Live destination and response plan | GTM owner | Approved or revise |

The [FTC advertising FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business) describes the general requirement for a reasonable basis for objective advertising claims. The right substantiation depends on the claim and context. This workflow does not determine legal sufficiency; it makes the internal source and decision visible so questions can reach the correct owner.

Put the source ID in the shot list and edit notes. If an editor changes wording, combines scenes, or replaces a product capture, recheck the source. Editing can change the meaning even when every original clip was accurate.

## 5. Record short takes that preserve founder delivery

Set up a quiet, stable recording environment with a clean background appropriate to the requested format. Prioritize intelligible audio, a steady frame, and comfortable eye line over decorative complexity. If the program specifies a simple founder recording, keep it simple.

For the founder take:

- place bullet prompts near the camera rather than reading a dense script;
- record one thought at a time;
- leave a short pause before and after each take;
- repeat the thought with different natural phrasing when wording is not locked;
- say locked claims exactly as approved;
- label the strongest approved take after review;
- keep the founder's actual cadence instead of removing every breath.

Do not use editing to manufacture a continuous performance if the sequence changes meaning. Combining clean takes is normal; implying a chronology, reaction, or certainty that did not exist is not.

For multiple founders, decide why each person speaks. A forced round-robin can fragment the story. Assign sections based on direct knowledge while keeping the company description and CTA consistent.

## 6. Capture one product proof safely

Prepare a dedicated capture state:

- synthetic or explicitly approved sample data;
- notifications disabled;
- unrelated tabs and apps closed;
- credentials, personal information, and internal URLs removed;
- current product state confirmed by the product owner;
- cursor path rehearsed;
- readable zoom and crop;
- local recording tested for sound and frame quality.

Record the proof in three parts: starting state, user action, result. Capture extra hold time on each state so the editor does not need to race. If the workflow is long, choose a truthful excerpt and explain the transition. Do not hide cuts in a way that overstates continuity.

Add a still-frame fallback. It can help the founder narrate the proof when motion playback is unavailable and can serve as an accessible reference in follow-up material.

## 7. Edit for clarity and evidence continuity

Build the first cut around meaning:

1. Place the best approved take for each of the six frames.
2. Remove repetition that does not add context.
3. Insert product proof only where the narration prepares the viewer for it.
4. Keep necessary interface labels readable.
5. Add text only when it clarifies, not when it adds new unsupported claims.
6. Check every transition for accidental implication.
7. End on the exact approved CTA.

Create a scene-level review table:

| Scene | Viewer takeaway | Source IDs | Reviewer | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 01 | Customer and situation | S-01 | Founder | Open or approved |
| 02 | Company change | S-01 | Founder | Open or approved |
| 03 | Founder insight | Approved take | Founder | Open or approved |
| 04 | Product proof | S-02 | Product owner | Open or approved |
| 05 | Selected evidence | S-03 or S-04 | Claim owner | Open or approved |
| 06 | Next step | S-05 | GTM owner | Open or approved |

Version cuts clearly. Notes must refer to a specific version and scene. After a factual or structural revision, do not inherit approval from an older cut.

## 8. Review captions, context, rights, and playback

Review the exact export in the conditions where it will be watched:

- compare captions with every spoken word, proper noun, number, and CTA;
- watch muted to find missing context;
- watch on a phone and the intended display path;
- inspect every product frame for private or confidential information;
- confirm permission for each person, customer reference, music track, logo, screenshot, and third-party asset;
- open the CTA from the published or submitted location;
- test a local backup independently from the primary playback path;
- confirm the final title, description, thumbnail, and transcript match the approved cut.

The founder should approve the final delivery, but approval may also be needed from product, claims, legal, privacy, security, customer, or rights owners. Define scopes rather than asking everyone to approve everything.

## Example six-frame outline

This fictional outline demonstrates structure only; it is not a customer result or VideoClaw performance claim.

| Frame | Example editorial direction | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer situation | “Small accelerator teams collect founder media across disconnected folders before showcase week.” | Present as the fictional scenario, not measured prevalence. |
| Company change | “The product organizes approved source material into a reviewable workflow.” | Use only if this matches current approved product language. |
| Founder insight | Founder explains why evidence ownership matters during a deadline. | Use an authentic approved take. |
| Product proof | Show one synthetic source item moving to an approved state. | Label the environment and avoid fake customer data. |
| Selected evidence | Show a sourced workflow property, not a fabricated KPI. | Tie the scene to its source ID. |
| Next step | Invite the defined viewer to the correct page or conversation. | Confirm availability and destination before release. |

The example is deliberately modest. The goal is to show how to structure evidence, not to supply claims for another company to copy.

## Founder pitch video production checklist

- [ ] One viewer, one job, and one next step are written down.
- [ ] The company description and factual language are approved.
- [ ] Three or four memorable points fit the requested format.
- [ ] Six conceptual frames have a purpose and source.
- [ ] The founder uses bullets for ideas and exact wording for locked claims.
- [ ] One product proof path uses clean, permitted data.
- [ ] Every factual scene has a source ID and owner.
- [ ] The edit preserves chronology, attribution, and certainty.
- [ ] Captions, muted viewing, mobile readability, and CTA are reviewed.
- [ ] People, customer references, music, and third-party assets have recorded permission or rights.
- [ ] Primary and backup playback paths are tested.
- [ ] The exact release package receives final approval.
