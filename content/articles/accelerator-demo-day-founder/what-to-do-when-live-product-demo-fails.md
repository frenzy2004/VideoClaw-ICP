When a live product demo fails, stop troubleshooting at a threshold your team chose before the event. State the switch without drama, play a verified backup that demonstrates the same product action and result, and continue the pitch. The founder's job is not to diagnose production infrastructure on stage; it is to preserve the audience's understanding and avoid replacing a failed live state with misleading evidence.

That response needs preparation. A generic screen recording is not automatically a valid fallback. The backup must represent the same product state, use the same approved claims, avoid exposed customer or test data, and work on the equipment available in the room. If it does not, the safer response may be a still-image walkthrough or a concise verbal explanation rather than a polished but inaccurate video.

## Treat the failure as a controlled handoff

A live-demo recovery plan has five states: detect, decide, disclose, switch, and continue. Each state has one owner and one visible outcome.

| State | On-stage action | Off-stage owner | Completion condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detect | Pause the product narration and identify that the expected state did not appear | Demo operator checks only the agreed quick signals | The team can name the failure state without guessing at a cause |
| Decide | Apply the preassigned stop threshold | Demo lead calls live, retry once, switch, or skip | One option is selected without debate |
| Disclose | Use one plain transition sentence | Founder speaks; operator prepares the fallback | The audience knows the format changed |
| Switch | Play or show the verified equivalent evidence | Operator launches the tested local asset | The same user action and result are visible |
| Continue | Return to the pitch's meaning and next step | Founder resumes the narrative | The audience can explain why the product action matters |

The sequence prevents three common problems. First, the founder does not spend the remaining pitch narrating an uncertain diagnosis. Second, the audience is not left wondering whether a recording was always intended to look live. Third, the fallback does not quietly expand the claim beyond what the failed demonstration was supposed to show.

The exact stop threshold is an editorial operating choice. A team might allow one reload, one reconnect, a fixed number of seconds, or no retry at all. Choose it during rehearsal based on the event format and technical setup. Do not invent a threshold on stage.

## Define what counts as a failure before Demo Day

“The demo broke” is too broad for a useful response plan. List the specific failure states that can interrupt the product story.

- **Access failure:** sign-in, permissions, expired session, or account state prevents entry.
- **Network failure:** the product or an external dependency does not respond.
- **Data failure:** the expected record, result, or test fixture is missing or altered.
- **Display failure:** the operator can see the product but the audience cannot.
- **Input failure:** camera, microphone, controller, browser, or demo device does not accept the expected action.
- **Product-state mismatch:** the visible build differs from the approved rehearsal or backup.
- **Privacy or rights failure:** personal data, confidential information, a third-party asset, or an unapproved name appears.

The last two states deserve a mandatory stop. A founder may be able to talk through a slow network, but should not continue showing sensitive information or describe one product state while displaying another. The recovery plan should state which conditions permit a retry and which require an immediate switch or skip.

Keep the technical tree shallow. The on-stage operator needs a short decision card, not an incident-management runbook. Detailed diagnosis can happen after the session. Demo-time notes should answer only: What is visible? Is the approved action recoverable within the threshold? Which fallback is equivalent?

## Prepare an evidence-equivalent backup

The fallback should support the same sentence as the live demonstration. Build a small equivalence table during rehearsal.

| Live-demo element | Backup requirement | Evidence check |
|---|---|---|
| Starting state | Same user role, data context, and product state | Product owner confirms the setup |
| User action | Same meaningful input or decision | Demo owner can reproduce it |
| Product response | Same result, without hidden edits that change meaning | Reviewer compares live and recorded flows |
| Spoken claim | Identical approved wording or a narrower replacement | Claim owner signs off |
| Disclosure | Clear label such as “recorded walkthrough” where needed | Event and company reviewers approve language |
| Sensitive data | Fictional, cleared, or removed | Privacy owner reviews every frame |

An older recording can become inaccurate after a UI change. A newer recording can also be invalid if it includes an unreleased capability. Store the capture date, build or product-state label, source file, owner, and approved claim beside the fallback. Recheck those fields after any material product change.

If the live flow uses a customer account, do not assume the backup may show it. Customer permission, confidentiality, and publicity rights need their own review. A fictional or controlled data set is often easier to reuse, but it still needs to be clearly presented so the audience does not infer a real customer result.

The FTC's [small-business advertising guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business) explains that advertising claims, including implied claims, need appropriate support. This article does not decide the legal status of a Demo Day pitch or a particular statement. It applies the practical principle that switching formats must not change the meaning or support behind the claim.

## Write the switch line before the event

The best transition is brief, factual, and rehearsed. It should identify the change in format without speculating about the cause.

Useful patterns include:

- “The live environment is not returning the approved state, so I am switching to the recorded walkthrough of this same flow.”
- “I am going to show the verified backup of the action we just attempted, then continue with what the result means.”
- “We are skipping the live interaction and using the labeled fallback so you can see the intended product sequence.”

Avoid lines that create new claims:

- Do not say the product “never does this” unless the company can support that statement.
- Do not blame venue Wi-Fi, an integration, a provider, or an operator without verified information.
- Do not promise that the issue is trivial, fixed, or unrelated to production.
- Do not describe the fallback as live if it is recorded or simulated.

Write one primary switch line and one shorter version. Put both on the presenter's confidence monitor or printed cue card. The founder should not need to search a shared document while the product is unavailable.

## Assign decisions before rehearsing the pitch

Live-demo recovery becomes slow when authority is unclear. Assign the following roles even if one person holds several of them:

| Role | Decision |
|---|---|
| Founder or presenter | Delivers the transition and returns to the audience problem |
| Demo operator | Executes only the approved retry and fallback actions |
| Demo lead | Calls the stop threshold and selects the fallback |
| Product owner | Confirms the backup matches the approved product state |
| Evidence reviewer | Confirms spoken and visible claims remain supportable |
| Privacy or rights reviewer | Clears screens, names, assets, and data for the event context |

For a small accelerator team, the founder may also be demo lead and product owner. That does not remove the need for explicit decisions. Write each decision next to the person's name. When pressure rises, roles reduce the temptation to improvise a technically clever but editorially unsafe workaround.

Techstars' [pitch toolkit](https://toolkit.techstars.com/master-your-pitch) and Y Combinator's [Demo Day pitch guidance](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/) support disciplined preparation, clarity, and concise communication. Neither source establishes the five-state protocol in this article. The protocol is an editorial method for translating those presentation principles into a recoverable product demonstration.

## Rehearse failures, not only the happy path

Run at least one rehearsal in which the operator deliberately creates each high-priority failure state. The goal is to test the decision and handoff, not to embarrass the presenter.

Use this rehearsal card:

1. Name the injected failure without telling the founder in advance.
2. Start a visible timer for the team's chosen threshold.
3. Record whether the operator used only approved recovery actions.
4. Check whether the founder used the disclosure line accurately.
5. Confirm the fallback launched locally and at the correct point.
6. Compare the live and fallback product states and claims.
7. Ask a reviewer to summarize what the audience would believe.
8. Log one change to the runbook, asset, or wording.

Test the actual presentation device, adapter, display mode, clicker, audio path, and offline asset. A backup stored only in a cloud drive does not cover a network failure. A video that plays on a laptop but has no audio in the venue does not cover a display-path problem. Keep a locally stored copy and verify that it opens without the demo account.

Also rehearse the decision to skip the fallback. If the backup is stale, unapproved, or cannot be displayed, the founder should be ready to say: “I do not have an accurate fallback for this state, so I will describe the user goal and follow up with the verified walkthrough.” That preserves trust better than using evidence the team knows is wrong.

## Continue with meaning, not an apology loop

Once the fallback ends, return to the customer problem and the demonstrated change. A simple bridge works:

> “That sequence shows how the user moves from [starting state] to [verified result]. The reason it matters is [approved customer or operating consequence].”

Do not replay the failure, diagnose it aloud, or repeatedly apologize. The audience needs to know what the demonstrated action means and what happens next. If the failure itself is material to the product claim, acknowledge that the team will follow up with verified context rather than minimizing it.

After the event, preserve the incident facts separately from the public explanation. Record the observed state, time, environment, selected fallback, wording used, audience questions, and owner for technical follow-up. Do not convert an early guess into a public postmortem.

## Final live-demo contingency checklist

Before entering the room, verify:

- Failure states and mandatory-stop conditions are written.
- The stop threshold and retry limit are assigned.
- The founder has a primary and short switch line.
- The fallback represents the same approved product state and claim.
- The backup is local, opens offline, and works on the presentation path.
- Every visible name, logo, screen, and data field is cleared.
- The presenter, operator, and decision owner know their roles.
- The team has rehearsed a failure and a skip-fallback outcome.
- The pitch can continue if no demonstration is available.
- A post-event incident owner is ready to capture facts.
