After Demo Day, publish only material that helps a real viewer continue the conversation. Start with a concise recap, then add one product-proof explanation, one milestone or learning update, and one clear next-step page or post. Build all four from the approved pitch story and source pack; do not treat the event as permission to invent new traction claims, reveal confidential details, or publish every piece of footage.

This is a public-content plan, not a substitute for direct investor follow-up. The most valuable next action may be answering a requested question privately, updating a data room, arranging a product conversation, or sending a reference. Public assets should make those conversations easier, not compete with them.

## Start with the conversations the event created

Before scheduling posts, collect what actually happened. Use a simple request log for the first pass:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Viewer | Investor, prospective customer, partner, recruit, press, or general audience |
| Exact question | The wording the person used, without interpretation |
| Requested item | Deck, product explanation, metric context, meeting, reference, or another asset |
| Public or private | Whether the answer can be shared broadly |
| Evidence owner | Person who can confirm the facts and scope |
| Next action | Specific response, owner, and date |

Do not convert a private diligence question into a public post merely because it sounds interesting. A question about customer concentration, security, product roadmap, fundraising terms, or an unreleased feature may need a controlled response. The content plan begins only after each item has an audience and disclosure decision.

Y Combinator's [seed fundraising guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-raise-a-seed-round/) treats fundraising as a focused process of conversations and investor decisions rather than a one-day media event. The editorial implication is straightforward: Demo Day creates inputs. Your team still has to route each input to the right answer and format.

## Asset one: publish a concise, factual recap

The recap establishes the public record of the event. It should answer four questions:

1. What event or accelerator context occurred?
2. What problem and product action did the company present?
3. What approved, current company fact can be repeated?
4. Where should a relevant viewer go next?

A useful recap is often shorter than the pitch. It does not need applause footage, a “we crushed it” claim, or a list of everyone who attended. It needs the correct company name, event wording, product-state label, approved visual, and one destination.

Use this recap outline:

> At [verified program or event], we showed how [specific user] can [observable product-supported change]. The demonstration used [accurate product-state or recording context]. If you work on [qualifying problem], [specific next step].

Verify the event name and any permitted accelerator reference against official program guidance. Participation does not automatically imply endorsement, investment, partnership, or customer validation. Avoid adding accelerator logos or investor likenesses unless the company has explicit rights and approval for that use.

If the pitch included a time-sensitive number, check it again before repeating it. A dated metric should carry the date or period that makes it meaningful. If the current source differs from the on-stage version, update the public statement or omit it rather than preserving a stale number for narrative consistency.

## Asset two: explain one product proof sequence

The second asset answers the most common “how does it work?” question with one visible product sequence. It may be a short video, annotated image sequence, or text walkthrough. Choose the format that can represent the action accurately.

Use a proof card:

| Component | Required decision |
|---|---|
| Audience question | One question the event actually produced |
| Starting state | What the viewer needs to understand before the action |
| Product action | One reproducible input or user decision |
| Result | The visible state created by the action |
| Product-state label | Live, recorded, prototype, simulated, or another accurate term |
| Claim source | Record supporting each spoken or on-screen factual statement |
| Permission check | Names, logos, screens, data, music, footage, and quotes |
| CTA | The next conversation the proof supports |

Do not turn the proof asset into a full product tour. A tightly framed explanation is easier to review and update. It also gives the viewer a stable reference to forward with the original question intact.

If the Demo Day recording contains the needed action but also includes unapproved audience footage or confidential screens, recapture the product sequence in a controlled environment. Reuse is efficient only when the source can pass the current rights, privacy, evidence, and product-state checks.

## Asset three: publish a milestone or learning update

The third asset adds progress without manufacturing momentum. Choose either a verifiable milestone or a useful operating lesson that follows from the event.

Examples of milestone classes, subject to company approval, include:

- a product state that has actually changed;
- an announced release with a stable public source;
- a team or operating milestone the company is authorized to disclose;
- a dated participation milestone described without implying endorsement;
- a customer-discovery learning stated without exposing a person or confidential business detail.

A learning update should distinguish observation from interpretation. For example:

- **Observed:** “Several follow-up conversations asked how review ownership works.”
- **Interpretation:** “We think approval handoffs deserve a clearer product explanation.”
- **Action:** “We published a source-linked walkthrough of the current review flow.”

That structure is stronger than “the market validated our vision.” The latter may imply more than a handful of conversations can establish. Name the sample or context only when it is appropriate to disclose, and avoid converting informal feedback into a performance or demand claim.

Techstars' [pitch guidance](https://toolkit.techstars.com/master-your-pitch) supports clear, audience-aware communication. The observation–interpretation–action format is VideoClaw editorial guidance, not a Techstars rule or proof that a particular post will improve fundraising.

## Asset four: create one stable next-step destination

The final asset is the destination that the other three can reference. It may be an approved product page, a contact path, a meeting-request page, a founder update, or a focused explanation of the use case shown. The key is stability.

Check five elements:

- **Audience:** Who is the page for?
- **Promise:** What can they accurately learn or do there?
- **Proof:** Which approved sources support the page?
- **Owner:** Who receives and routes the response?
- **Refresh trigger:** What product, evidence, or company change requires an update?

Do not send every audience to the same generic homepage if their next actions differ. Investors may need a controlled follow-up path. Prospective customers may need a problem-specific conversation. Recruits may need a current role page. A general public recap can still use one broad learning destination, but it should not claim to meet every diligence need.

Test the link outside the company account and on a mobile connection. Remove references to unavailable downloads, invite-only materials, closed forms, or unapproved features. Record the destination owner and date beside the source pack so future edits do not leave the content pointing at an abandoned page.

## Map the four assets to distinct viewers

One asset can serve more than one audience, but each should start with a primary viewer and question.

| Asset | Primary viewer question | Best evidence source | Appropriate next action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Factual recap | “What did this company present?” | Approved pitch story and verified event context | Learn the core problem and company direction |
| Product-proof explanation | “Can I see the relevant action?” | Current, reproducible product sequence | Discuss the demonstrated workflow |
| Milestone or learning update | “What changed or what did the team learn?” | Dated milestone record or approved observation log | Follow the specific development |
| Stable next-step destination | “How do I continue?” | Current offer, availability, and routing owner | Take one audience-matched action |

This map reduces duplication. If the recap already contains the full product sequence, the proof asset has no separate job. If the learning update merely restates the recap, delay it until the company has a distinct observation worth sharing.

## Use a source-controlled publishing workflow

For each asset, keep the content draft beside its inputs rather than copying facts into a disconnected social scheduler.

Use these fields:

1. Asset owner and primary viewer.
2. Viewer question and desired next action.
3. Approved claim wording and source links.
4. Product-state label and capture date.
5. Event-name and affiliation review.
6. Privacy, confidentiality, copyright, and permission decisions.
7. Caption, alt text, and transcript review where applicable.
8. Final approver and approval date.
9. Distribution destinations.
10. Refresh or removal trigger.

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's [private-company resources](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/private-companies-sec) provide federal securities-law context for private companies raising capital. They do not turn this article into legal advice. Company-specific fundraising communications, public offering questions, and material disclosures should be reviewed by qualified counsel and the company's authorized decision-makers.

## Decide what not to publish

The public content plan should have an explicit no-release outcome. Keep material private when:

- an investor, customer, employee, or partner expected confidentiality;
- the company cannot support a material statement;
- a product recording exposes sensitive data or an unreleased state;
- the company lacks rights to footage, music, marks, quotes, or likenesses;
- the event reference could imply an affiliation or endorsement the company cannot substantiate;
- the public asset would interfere with a requested private response;
- nobody owns the destination or can answer the resulting inquiries.

Not every Demo Day needs a highlight reel. A clean recap and direct, well-routed follow-up may be the right output. Quality here means accuracy and usefulness to the intended viewer, not the number of posts extracted from an event.

## A practical four-asset review board

Before scheduling, review the portfolio as a set:

| Review question | Recap | Proof | Update | Next-step destination |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Has one primary audience? | Yes or revise | Yes or revise | Yes or revise | Yes or revise |
| Answers a distinct question? | Yes or merge | Yes or merge | Yes or delay | Yes or replace |
| Claims have sources and owners? | Required | Required | Required | Required |
| Media rights and privacy cleared? | Required | Required | Required | Required if media appears |
| CTA works and is owned? | Required | Required | Required | This is the destination |
| Refresh trigger recorded? | Required | Required | Required | Required |

Publish the smallest complete set. If only the recap and next-step destination pass review, use those two. Add the proof and learning assets when they answer real questions with cleared evidence.
