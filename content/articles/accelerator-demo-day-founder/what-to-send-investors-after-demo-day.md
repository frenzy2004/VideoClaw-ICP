After Demo Day, send an investor the specific item they requested, a concise company recap with current approved proof, and one clear next step. Do not send a generic folder of every deck, clip, memo, and screenshot. Choose the smallest asset that answers the question accurately, can be forwarded in the intended context, and does not reveal information the recipient was not meant to receive.

A short video is useful only when the investor needs to see a product action or founder explanation that text cannot communicate as clearly. A deck is better for a structured company overview. A memo is better for a question that needs reasoning and context. A data-room item is better for controlled diligence. A live meeting is better when the answer is sensitive, interactive, or likely to create follow-up questions.

## Use the request, not the channel, to choose the asset

Start by writing the investor's question exactly as asked. Then route it through this table.

| Investor question | Best starting format | Why it fits | Main release check |
|---|---|---|---|
| “Can you send the latest overview?” | Current deck or concise approved recap | Preserves company structure and context | Version, date, recipients, and fundraising wording |
| “Can I see how the product handles this workflow?” | Narrow product-proof video or live demo | Makes one action and result visible | Product state, data, claims, permissions, and fallback |
| “Why did you make this strategic choice?” | Short memo or meeting | Allows reasoning, alternatives, and assumptions | Confidentiality and approved forwardability |
| “Can you support this metric?” | Controlled data-room item or sourced written answer | Keeps definition, period, and evidence together | Access control, metric scope, and owner |
| “Can I speak with a customer or expert?” | Permissioned introduction or reference process | Respects the third party's context and consent | Explicit permission and coordinated timing |
| “What should we discuss next?” | Scheduled conversation with a short agenda | Lets both sides resolve an open decision | Owner, agenda, and materials needed in advance |

This routing model prevents format from driving the answer. A founder who has just produced a polished video may want to send it everywhere. That is a production bias. The investor's question, disclosure context, and evidence should decide.

Y Combinator's [seed fundraising guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-raise-a-seed-round/) emphasizes focused investor conversations and a deliberate fundraising process. The request-to-asset table is VideoClaw editorial guidance for handling the materials around those conversations; it is not a YC-prescribed document list.

## Send a concise recap that can stand on its own

Even when responding with a requested attachment, include a short written recap so the item is not detached from context. The recap can contain:

- the company name and one-sentence problem definition;
- the current product action most relevant to the investor's question;
- one approved, dated proof point or a clear statement that no additional proof is being asserted;
- the purpose and version of the attached or linked item;
- one next step with an owner.

Avoid copying the full pitch into the message. The recap should help a recipient understand why the asset was sent and what decision or question comes next. It also reduces the risk that a forwarded link appears without its product-state or evidence boundary.

A safe drafting pattern is:

> You asked about [exact question]. The attached [asset type and version] shows [narrow content]. It reflects [product state or evidence date] and is intended for [audience or purpose]. The next useful step is [one action].

The bracketed fields force specificity. If the team cannot fill in the evidence date, intended audience, or next action, the asset is not ready to send.

## Use a deck for structured context

A deck is appropriate when the investor needs a coherent overview: problem, product, market framing, business model, team, evidence, and fundraising context. It is not automatically the right answer to a detailed product or diligence question.

Before sending, check:

1. The file is the current approved version.
2. The date and confidentiality or distribution label are accurate.
3. Numbers use the same definitions and periods as their source records.
4. Product screenshots reflect the stated product state.
5. Customer names, quotes, and logos have the necessary permission.
6. The deck does not contain notes, hidden slides, comments, or metadata meant for internal review.
7. The link's access settings match the intended recipient and forwarding expectation.

Do not attach both an old event deck and a newer fundraising deck without explaining the difference. If the Demo Day version has become stale, preserve it internally as an event record and send the current approved item.

## Use a memo when the question needs reasoning

A memo is useful for a decision that cannot be understood from a slide or product clip. Examples include product positioning, a market assumption, a go-to-market choice, a hiring plan, or an explanation of what the team has learned.

Structure a concise memo around:

- the investor's question;
- the current answer;
- evidence and sources;
- important assumptions or unknowns;
- alternatives the team considered;
- the decision or next conversation requested.

Keep facts and founder interpretation visibly separate. “Three interviewees asked about approval ownership” is an observation if the company has the notes and permission to summarize them. “This proves the whole market needs an approval platform” is a much broader interpretation. Label the latter as a hypothesis if it is included at all.

A memo may be more forwardable than a call, which makes its disclosure boundary important. Mark the intended audience, avoid unnecessary personal or customer data, and do not assume that “confidential” text by itself creates or enforces a legal obligation. Use the company's approved information-sharing process.

## Use video only for a visible product or founder question

A product-proof video earns its place when seeing the action materially improves the answer. Keep it narrow: one starting state, one meaningful action, one observable result, and a clear product-state label.

Use this video release card:

| Field | Required answer |
|---|---|
| Investor question | The exact question this video answers |
| Product sequence | Start, action, and result |
| Product state | Current live state, recorded environment, prototype, simulation, or another accurate label |
| Evidence | Source for each spoken or on-screen claim |
| Data | Confirmation that visible information is fictional, approved, or appropriately controlled |
| Rights | Permission for every third-party mark, quote, image, voice, or recording |
| Forwarding boundary | Who may receive the link and what context must travel with it |
| Expiry | Product or evidence change that requires replacement |

Do not use a highlight reel to answer a workflow question. Do not use stage applause as evidence of demand. Do not use an edited product result that removes a meaningful limitation. If the product state cannot be shown accurately in a short recording, offer a live walkthrough or a narrower written response.

Techstars' [pitch toolkit](https://toolkit.techstars.com/master-your-pitch) supports clear, prepared communication. The start–action–result model and video release card are editorial controls, not Techstars requirements or a claim that video improves fundraising outcomes.

## Use the data room for controlled diligence

Some answers belong in a controlled diligence environment rather than an email attachment or public link. Examples may include detailed financial information, contracts, capitalization records, security materials, customer data, or internal analyses, subject to the company's process and counsel.

When routing an item to the data room:

- confirm the requester and the appropriate access level;
- identify the document owner and current version;
- use a stable file name and date;
- define any metric or term needed to interpret the item;
- remove unrelated information that the request does not require;
- record access and replacement decisions through the approved system;
- answer the question in the message instead of forcing the investor to guess which file matters.

The principle is minimization: provide the information needed for the legitimate diligence question through the correct controlled path. A folder full of documents is not a substitute for a routed response.

## Arrange a reference only with explicit permission

A customer, adviser, employee, or domain expert is not an asset the founder can attach. A reference introduction needs the person's permission, context, and timing.

Before offering one:

1. Ask the person whether they are willing to participate.
2. Explain who the investor is and what topic is likely to be discussed.
3. Confirm what relationship or experience may be described.
4. Avoid coaching the person toward unsupported claims.
5. Coordinate the introduction and respect a refusal or delay.
6. Do not publish the reference's identity or comments without separate permission.

If permission is not available, say so and offer another evidence source. Do not replace a real reference with a composite testimonial, invented quote, or implication that unnamed customers support the company.

## Keep fundraising communications inside the approved boundary

Private-company fundraising communications can raise company-specific securities-law and disclosure questions. The SEC's [private-company guidance](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/private-companies-sec) provides federal context but does not answer every question for every offering, company, state, or recipient.

Use an internal escalation rule for:

- public or broadly forwarded fundraising language;
- offering terms, returns, forecasts, or material risk statements;
- investor eligibility or solicitation questions;
- selective disclosure of sensitive company information;
- statements that may differ from formal offering or diligence materials;
- uncertainty about state, federal, or cross-border requirements.

This article is operational guidance, not legal advice. Route those questions to qualified counsel and the company's authorized fundraising decision-makers before sending the asset.

## Build one response packet, not a media bundle

For each investor, create a small response record:

| Field | Example of a complete entry |
|---|---|
| Question | Exact wording from the conversation |
| Selected asset | Current deck, narrow demo, memo, data-room item, reference, or meeting |
| Why this format | One sentence connecting format to the question |
| Evidence owner | Named internal owner |
| Version and date | Current source-control identifiers |
| Disclosure state | Public, permissioned, controlled, or counsel review |
| Message owner | Person sending and answering follow-up |
| Next step | One requested action and date |
| Refresh trigger | Product, metric, permission, or fundraising change |

The packet can point to several items if the investor asked several distinct questions. It should not include materials merely because they exist. Before sending, ask whether every link has a job and whether the recipient can understand that job without the founder narrating it live.

## Final send checklist

- The response begins with the investor's exact question.
- The chosen format is the smallest one that answers it.
- The recap states product and evidence context.
- All documents, recordings, and links are current versions.
- Numbers, product states, customer references, and claims have owners and sources.
- Access settings and forwarding expectations are deliberate.
- Sensitive, legal, and offering questions received the required review.
- Every third-party reference or media element has permission.
- The message asks for one next step.
- A named person owns the reply and asset refresh.
