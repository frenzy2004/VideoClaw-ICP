A startup pitch video is a recorded founder explanation made for a defined viewer and next step. Its job is usually to create enough clarity and interest for a follow-up conversation while preserving approved facts. It is not automatically a substitute for a live pitch, product demo, pitch deck, application video, or data room; each asset answers a different question.

The useful first decision is therefore not “Which camera or AI editor should we use?” It is “What decision should this recording help one audience make?” Once that is clear, duration, evidence, scenes, CTA, and production choices become easier to defend.

## What a startup pitch video is—and is not

A pitch video combines the founder's explanation, selected business context, and enough product or market evidence to move a specific viewer forward. It can be self-recorded, captured during an accelerator activity, produced as a standalone asset, or edited from approved source material. Production method does not define the category; the viewer's job does.

A useful pitch video should let the intended viewer answer:

- Who has the problem?
- What costly or important situation are they in?
- What change does the startup enable?
- What evidence should I believe at this stage?
- Why is this team pursuing the problem?
- What should I do next?

It is not a complete diligence package. It should not make the founder compress every market assumption, financial model, technical detail, customer record, and legal disclosure into one linear file. The [Y Combinator guide to Demo Day pitches](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/) treats a pitch as a way to generate interest and meetings, not as a container for every available fact. A recording needs the same restraint.

It is also not defined by a universal runtime. An accelerator, application, event, or recipient may specify a limit. The right response is to follow that requirement and build the story around it, not to present an unrelated duration as an industry law.

## Choose one of four practical jobs

Most accelerator-stage pitch videos can be assigned one primary job. Mixing jobs is possible, but it increases the chance of a vague CTA and mismatched evidence.

### Job 1: create a first-pass understanding

The viewer has little context and needs the customer, problem, change, and team in plain language. Use a clean company description, one memorable insight, and one proof point. The next step might be opening the deck or agreeing to a conversation.

### Job 2: make the founder legible before a meeting

The viewer has seen basic company information but has not heard the founder explain it. The video should reveal how the founder thinks and communicates without turning into a performance reel. Use the founder's natural language, bullet-led takes, and direct explanation.

### Job 3: prove one product claim

The viewer understands the story but needs to see a workflow, output, or technical behavior. The founder provides context, then the video shows one clean proof path. This is closer to a narrated product proof than to a general company introduction.

### Job 4: answer a known follow-up question

The viewer has already engaged and asks something specific: how onboarding works, what the product replaces, how a result is produced, or what the current workflow looks like. The video should answer that question directly instead of replaying the full company pitch.

Write the selected job at the top of the brief. If a proposed scene does not help that job, move it to a different asset.

## How the pitch video differs from adjacent fundraising assets

| Asset | Primary viewer question | Best evidence form | Typical next step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Live pitch | Is this company worth a conversation now? | Founder delivery, memorable facts, concise narrative | Meeting or follow-up request |
| Recorded pitch video | Can I understand and assess the founder's explanation asynchronously? | Approved founder take plus selected proof | Watch, share internally, or continue the conversation |
| Product demo | Does a particular workflow or capability work as described? | Clean product sequence with contextual narration | Technical or product follow-up |
| Pitch deck | Can I scan the company, market, model, traction, team, and ask? | Structured slides and sourced facts | Deeper review or meeting |
| Data room | Can I evaluate the detailed evidence and risks? | Controlled documents, records, and permissions | Diligence |

These are editorial distinctions, not formal legal categories. A real accelerator or investor may name and combine assets differently. Use the requested terminology and delivery constraints while preserving the underlying job.

The [Techstars pitch toolkit](https://toolkit.techstars.com/master-your-pitch) focuses founders on audience, structure, and the outcome of a pitch. The practical implication for video is that two files with identical footage may need different openings, evidence, and CTAs when their viewers differ.

## Build the video around a viewer-to-next-step chain

Use this sentence:

> For **[specific viewer]**, this video explains **[company change]**, proves **[one approved fact or workflow]**, and asks them to **[one next step]**.

Now test each element.

**Specific viewer:** “Investors” may still be too broad. Is this for accelerator-selected Demo Day attendees, a warm introduction, a sector specialist, or someone already in follow-up? Do not imply personalization you do not have; simply be clear about context.

**Company change:** Describe what becomes possible or different for the customer. Avoid a list of implementation details before the viewer understands why they matter.

**Approved proof:** Choose evidence appropriate to the stage. A product workflow, dated operating fact, or permitted customer statement can be useful when it has a source and owner. Do not manufacture a result or imply production use from a controlled example.

**One next step:** The CTA should be available, owned, and suited to the viewer. Test the link and assign someone to respond.

If the sentence requires several viewers, three proofs, and multiple CTAs, create separate versions or choose a narrower job.

## Decide what evidence belongs in the recording

Create a small evidence table before scripting:

| Evidence candidate | Viewer question | Source and date | Permission or approver | Include? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Product sequence | Does the workflow exist in this form? | Recorded build or release reference | Product owner | Yes, if current and readable |
| Company metric | Is there evidence of activity or progress? | Internal record with definition | Metric owner | Only with approved wording |
| Customer reference | Who uses or values this? | Permission record and source | Authorized owner | Only within granted scope |
| Market statement | Why is the problem important? | Named primary or reliable source | Claim owner | Only if the source supports the wording |
| Future statement | What comes next? | Approved plan | Founder and required reviewer | Clearly distinguish plan from current fact |

Fundraising communications can carry securities-law context. The [SEC's private-company resources](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/capital-raising-building-blocks/private-companies-sec) provide federal background for private companies raising capital. This article does not decide whether a specific recording, distribution method, statement, or audience is legally appropriate. Route those questions to qualified counsel and the company's authorized decision-makers.

The operational rule is narrower: every factual statement in the cut should have an identifiable source, exact approved wording, and responsible owner. That makes review possible; it does not replace professional advice.

## Keep founder voice without giving up control

Authenticity does not mean improvising every factual statement. Control the information and leave room in the delivery.

Use a three-layer recording plan:

1. **Locked facts:** company description, numbers, customer references, product claims, legal or availability language, and CTA.
2. **Bullet-led ideas:** the problem, founder insight, transition into proof, and why the moment matters.
3. **Natural phrasing:** connective language and delivery that the founder can say comfortably without changing meaning.

Record several short takes rather than one memorized monologue. Label the approved take for each section. During editing, check that removing pauses or combining takes has not changed chronology, attribution, or certainty.

A polished video that sounds unlike the founder can weaken the asset's job. A natural take that introduces unsupported claims creates a different risk. The recording process should protect both voice and evidence.

## A minimum viable pitch-video brief

Complete this before production:

- **Audience:** one defined viewer context.
- **Primary job:** first-pass understanding, founder legibility, product proof, or follow-up answer.
- **One-sentence company description:** approved wording.
- **Three memorable points:** no more than the viewer needs for this step.
- **Proof sequence:** one source-controlled fact or workflow.
- **Founder role:** what the founder says that a deck cannot.
- **Excluded material:** details intentionally left to deck, demo, data room, or follow-up.
- **CTA:** one destination, owner, and response expectation.
- **Distribution:** where the file may appear and who may receive it.
- **Approval:** named owners for story, product, claims, captions, rights, and release.
- **Backup:** local master and a still-based recovery option where relevant.

If the brief is complete, the team can choose a production method. If it is not, new software will only make the ambiguity faster to render.

## When not to make one video

Do not force a single startup pitch video to serve every accelerator stage. Separate assets are usually clearer when:

- an application asks founders to introduce themselves but not demonstrate the product;
- the product proof contains details inappropriate for broad distribution;
- Demo Day requires a short pitch with a specific delivery format;
- an investor follow-up asks a technical question that the general pitch cannot answer;
- the public launch uses different claims, permissions, or CTA;
- a customer or partner reference is approved only for a limited audience.

Reusing approved source material can still be efficient. Reusing one finished file without checking its job and context is not the same thing.
