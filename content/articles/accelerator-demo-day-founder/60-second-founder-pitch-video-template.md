A useful 60-second founder pitch video script is not a miniature pitch deck. It is a five-block explanation: name the person with the problem, describe the change they need, show how the product creates that change, state one approved piece of evidence, and ask for one next step. Write each block as bullet prompts, attach every factual claim to a source, and remove any sentence that the team cannot support or show clearly.

This structure is especially useful for a US accelerator founder who has several audiences arriving at once. An investor may discover the company after Demo Day, a prospective customer may receive the link from an adviser, and a recruit may see the same clip on a founder profile. The video cannot answer every question for every viewer. Its job is to make one accurate idea easy to understand and give the right viewer a reason to continue.

## Use five blocks instead of compressing the deck

The worksheet below is a drafting model, not a promise that every founder speaks at the same pace. The time bands are editorial planning ranges. Record naturally, then shorten language or remove a block detail if the finished take runs long.

| Planning range | Block | The question it answers | Required source before recording |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–10 seconds | Person and problem | Who experiences a specific problem? | Interview notes, operating observation, or another approved problem source |
| 10–20 seconds | Desired change | What should become easier, safer, faster, or possible? | Approved product positioning and a clear audience definition |
| 20–38 seconds | Product action | What does the product actually do? | A current product-state note and a reproducible demo sequence |
| 38–50 seconds | Proof | What can the company support today? | Dated metric, approved customer evidence, or a plainly labeled product fact |
| 50–60 seconds | Next step | What should this viewer do now? | One owner, one working destination, and an audience-appropriate request |

The order matters more than ornate wording. A viewer needs the problem before the interface has meaning. The product action needs to be visible or easy to picture before the evidence feels grounded. The next step needs to follow from the pitch rather than appear as a generic request for attention.

Y Combinator's [application-video guidance](https://www.ycombinator.com/video/) asks founders to communicate directly and naturally in a short video. That is a useful constraint for delivery, but an application video and a public or investor-facing Demo Day asset are not automatically interchangeable. The audience, approved facts, confidentiality rules, and desired next action may differ.

## Block one: identify a person and a concrete problem

Start with a person in a situation, not a category slogan. “Operations teams lose the final approval state across email and spreadsheets” is more useful than “work is broken.” The first sentence should help a qualified viewer recognize the job without forcing everyone else to decode internal startup language.

Use this fill-in prompt:

> For [specific person or team], [observable situation] creates [specific consequence].

Before keeping the sentence, ask four questions:

- Is the person narrow enough that the viewer can picture them?
- Is the situation observable rather than speculative?
- Does the consequence follow from an approved source or direct operational fact?
- Does the wording avoid implying that every member of a market has the same experience?

A founder may know the customer problem intimately but still overstate its frequency. “Every team wastes weeks” needs evidence. “In the workflow we designed for, an approval can become separated from its source” is narrower and easier to support. This is not timid copy. It is a clearer claim with a visible boundary.

If there are two different customers, choose one for this video. A marketplace that serves buyers and sellers may eventually need two versions. Trying to cover both sides in one minute often produces a category description rather than a coherent founder explanation.

## Block two: describe the change before naming features

The second block defines the useful change. It should connect the problem to a better operating state without jumping directly into a feature list.

Use this prompt:

> We help [the same person] move from [current state] to [desired state] by [plain-language mechanism].

“Move from scattered approvals to a source-linked review queue” explains a change. “AI-powered end-to-end platform” does not. The latter may contain product language, but it leaves the viewer to infer the user, action, and result.

For a Demo Day or accelerator audience, keep the mechanism close to what can be shown. If the product is in private alpha, say so when that state matters. If the demonstration uses prepared data, disclose that context rather than allowing the viewer to assume a live customer environment. If a capability is planned rather than available, do not present it in the current-state block.

The [YC guide to Demo Day pitches](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/) emphasizes clarity and concise explanation. The editorial adaptation here is to make “what changes” its own checkable block. That separation prevents a polished feature sequence from replacing the customer job.

## Block three: show one product action from start to finish

Choose one action that proves the product mechanism. A one-minute pitch is not the place for a full interface tour. The strongest sequence often has three visible states:

1. A clean, understandable input.
2. The product action that matters.
3. The resulting state the user can inspect or continue using.

Write the product block as stage directions as well as speech:

| Field | Draft |
|---|---|
| Spoken line | One sentence explaining the action |
| Starting frame | The minimum context the viewer needs |
| Product action | One cursor path or visible transformation |
| Result frame | The state that supports the spoken line |
| Product-state label | Live, recorded, prototype, simulated, or other accurate description |
| Sensitive-data check | What must be replaced, blurred, removed, or kept off screen |

Do not make the founder narrate every click. Let the image carry the observable action while the voice explains why it matters. If the product action cannot be seen at this scale, use a labeled diagram or a closer crop rather than making a broad verbal claim.

Record a clean backup of the same sequence. The fallback should show the same product state and support the same spoken claim. A backup that comes from a newer prototype, a different data set, or an edited result can create an evidence mismatch even if it looks smoother.

## Block four: choose one proof point and show its boundary

Proof is not decoration at the end of a story. It is a statement that a viewer may repeat, forward, or use in a decision. The proof block should therefore include an owner, date, source, and approved wording before it enters the script.

Possible evidence classes include:

- a current, reproducible product fact;
- a dated operating metric with a defined population;
- approved customer evidence with the necessary permission;
- a founder or team credential stated narrowly and accurately;
- a milestone whose date and scope can be verified.

The FTC's [advertising substantiation policy](https://search.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation) provides the factual boundary: objective claims need a reasonable basis. This article does not determine what evidence is legally sufficient for a particular company or fundraising communication. It recommends a workflow: attach the source and reviewer to the sentence before recording, and route sensitive or material claims to qualified company counsel or another authorized reviewer.

Use a claim card for the selected proof:

| Field | Required entry |
|---|---|
| Exact spoken wording | The sentence that will appear in the take |
| Evidence source | Stable internal record or approved public source |
| Scope | Product state, date range, audience, and exclusions |
| Permission | Who may be named or shown, and under what terms |
| Owner | Person accountable for accuracy |
| Remove if | The source expires, the wording changes, or approval is withdrawn |

If no evidence passes this check, omit the proof block rather than inventing a softer-sounding claim. A precise product demonstration may be more credible than an ungrounded superlative.

## Block five: ask for one next step

The final line should tell the intended viewer what to do, not list every company goal. Choose one action that matches the distribution context.

- For an investor follow-up version: ask for the next conversation or invite a specific diligence question.
- For a customer-discovery version: ask qualified teams to discuss the workflow shown.
- For a recruiting version: point to the relevant role or team page.
- For a general Demo Day recap: provide one stable place to learn more.

Check the destination immediately before publishing. Make sure it works without internal access, matches the spoken request, and has an owner who can respond. Avoid putting an email address, calendar, download, and social follow on the same closing frame. Multiple choices dilute the only action the video can clearly support.

## Turn the template into bullet prompts

Read-aloud prose often becomes stiff because it was written for the page. Convert the five blocks into prompts that preserve the factual spine while allowing the founder to speak naturally.

Example with a fictional workflow company:

- **Problem:** “GTM teams lose the approved source when a campaign moves from interview to clips.”
- **Change:** “We keep each edit connected to the source, decision, and next review.”
- **Product action:** “Here, a reviewer opens one claim and sees the exact transcript segment behind it.”
- **Proof boundary:** “This is a recorded walkthrough of our current private-alpha workflow using fictional data.”
- **Next step:** “If your team manages the same handoff, compare your current process with this one.”

This example is editorial and fictional. It is not a VideoClaw performance claim, customer result, integration statement, or promise about the product. Replace every line with approved company-specific language.

During rehearsal, mark only the words that cannot drift: product-state labels, numbers, customer names, legal names, and the CTA. Let connective language remain conversational. If a founder repeatedly misses a sentence, simplify the sentence instead of demanding a more polished performance.

## Run the evidence and recording preflight

Before recording, the founder and reviewer should be able to answer yes to each item:

- The video has one primary audience and one next step.
- Each of the five blocks fits on one worksheet row.
- Every objective claim has a source, scope, owner, and approved wording.
- The product sequence can be reproduced in a clean environment.
- The product state is labeled accurately.
- No customer name, logo, quote, screen, or data appears without permission.
- The backup take supports the same claims as the preferred take.
- Captions and on-screen text match the final approved wording.
- The CTA destination works and has a response owner.
- A reviewer can remove a claim without rebuilding the entire story.

Finish by recording two or three natural takes from the same approved prompts, not by creating new claims between takes. Preserve the source footage and the final prompt sheet together so a future update can identify what changed.
