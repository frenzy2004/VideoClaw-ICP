A useful Demo Day video plan starts before recording. Lock the audience and next step, approve every factual claim, choose one product proof sequence, assign an owner to each production gate, prepare captions and playback backups, and decide what can be reused after the event. Treat the video as a controlled evidence asset in the accelerator workflow, not as a last-minute decoration for a finished pitch.

The checklist below covers the full preparation cycle. It is intentionally different from a final 72-hour preflight: planning determines what should exist and who owns it; preflight only confirms that the approved assets still work.

## 1. Write the decision brief before the script

Begin with a one-page brief that answers five questions. If the team cannot answer them in plain language, recording will amplify the ambiguity.

| Decision | Write this down | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Viewer | The specific audience for this version | An accelerator reviewer, a Demo Day investor, a prospective design partner, and a public launch audience do not need identical context. |
| Job | The decision the video should help the viewer make | “Understand us” is vague. “Request a product follow-up” or “open the technical appendix” is testable. |
| Core idea | One sentence the viewer should remember | This becomes the standard against which every scene is kept or cut. |
| Proof | One visible workflow or approved fact | Product proof should resolve a real viewer question, not merely show that an interface exists. |
| Next step | One destination and one owner | The CTA should lead somewhere the team can monitor and service. |

The [Y Combinator guide to Demo Day pitches](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/) emphasizes clarity, truthfulness, and memorable points. The [Techstars pitch toolkit](https://toolkit.techstars.com/master-your-pitch) similarly frames pitch preparation around the audience and the outcome. Those sources discuss pitching; the decision brief above is VideoClaw's editorial method for translating that discipline into a recorded asset.

Do not set a universal duration at this stage. The accelerator may impose a format, and the job of the asset may require a different length. Write the required constraint into the brief rather than importing a number from an unrelated program or example.

## 2. Lock the story and create a claim ledger

“Story lock” does not mean every sentence is frozen. It means the team agrees on the sequence of ideas and on the facts that may appear. A practical sequence for an accelerator founder is:

1. Name the customer and costly situation.
2. Explain the specific change the company enables.
3. Show one product action or evidence point.
4. State the current company status using approved language.
5. Ask for one next step.

Now build a claim ledger. Give every factual statement a source, owner, approval state, and expiry condition. A claim can be a number, customer reference, performance comparison, market statement, partnership, security description, product capability, or fundraising status. Store the exact approved wording rather than a loose note such as “mention traction.”

| Claim ID | Proposed wording | Source | Approver | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C-01 | One-sentence company description | Current company brief | Founder | Approved or revise |
| C-02 | Product capability shown on screen | Current release notes or product owner | Product lead | Approved or revise |
| C-03 | Customer or usage fact | Dated internal record and permission | Responsible owner | Approved or omit |
| C-04 | Fundraising or availability statement | Counsel-approved or founder-approved source | Named approver | Approved or omit |

The ledger is not legal advice and does not decide what a company may communicate. It makes the internal decision visible. When a claim has no source or authorized approver, the production action is simple: remove it, qualify it accurately, or escalate it before recording.

## 3. Plan product proof that survives a real room

A Demo Day audience often sees a projected screen, a livestream, a compressed recording, or a phone replay rather than the founder's editing monitor. Choose proof that remains understandable after those transformations.

Use one “proof path” with a beginning, action, and observable result:

- Start from a clean, non-sensitive state the viewer can identify.
- Show one meaningful user action without racing the cursor.
- Pause long enough for the result to register.
- Narrate what changed and why it matters.
- Record a clean local take without notifications, personal data, hidden tabs, or customer information.
- Capture a still frame that can replace the motion sequence if playback fails.

Avoid a grand tour of every screen. A broad tour asks the audience to decide what matters. A proof path makes that editorial choice before the event.

Create a shot list with source IDs. A factual title card should point back to the claim ledger. A product scene should identify the build or environment recorded. A founder quote should have an approved take. This lets the editor improve pacing without silently changing the evidence.

## 4. Assign owners and gates across the production cycle

Small startup teams often assume everyone owns the video. In practice, that means no one owns the final decision. Assign one accountable person to each gate even if the same founder holds several roles.

| Phase | Accountable owner | Gate to pass | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Story lock | Founder | Audience, job, core idea, and CTA approved | Decision brief |
| Evidence lock | Claim owners | Every factual statement sourced or removed | Claim ledger |
| Capture readiness | Product or operations lead | Clean environment and proof path rehearsed | Shot list and capture state |
| Recording | Founder or producer | Usable founder take and product take captured | Labeled source files |
| Edit review | Editor | Structure, readability, and evidence continuity preserved | Review cut |
| Accessibility review | Named reviewer | Captions accurate and essential visual information understandable | Captioned cut |
| Release approval | Founder plus required approvers | Correct version, CTA, permissions, and playback paths confirmed | Approved master and backup |

Use explicit version names such as `review-01`, `review-02`, and `approved-master`. Never rely on “final-final” naming. Put review notes against a specific version and scene. If a reviewer changes a factual statement, route it back through the claim owner instead of treating it as copyediting.

## 5. Design for captions, muted viewing, and readable screens

Captions are part of the content, not an export option. The [W3C caption guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) explains that captions provide speech and important non-speech audio information needed to understand media. For a founder video, that means names, product terms, numbers, and calls to action need deliberate review.

Plan accessibility during script and shot selection:

- Put important meaning in narration or text, not color alone.
- Keep interface text large enough to read in the intended player.
- Avoid covering product controls with captions or lower-thirds.
- Review proper nouns and technical terms in the caption file.
- Include relevant non-speech information when it changes meaning.
- Watch the finished video muted and without the original project context.
- Watch it on a phone and on the actual event display path when possible.

Automatic captions can accelerate a draft, but a named person should compare them with the approved audio. The approval is for the captioned master, not only for the edit before captions were added.

## 6. Build two playback paths and one recovery script

Your primary playback path might be the accelerator's presentation system, a browser, a conferencing platform, or an embedded player. The backup should not depend on the same connection or account.

Prepare:

1. The approved master in the required delivery format.
2. A local backup tested on the presentation device.
3. A static proof frame or short sequence the founder can narrate if video playback is unavailable.
4. A clearly named folder containing only approved event assets.
5. A one-sentence recovery line the founder has rehearsed.

The recovery line should preserve momentum rather than apologize at length: “I’ll show the approved still and walk you through the action.” The exact language is editorial and should fit the founder's voice. The operational point is to decide it before stress narrows the team's options.

Run the primary and backup paths from the room, account, and device that will be used. Confirm audio routing, aspect ratio, fullscreen behavior, login state, and offline access. A backup file that has never been opened on the event laptop is only an assumption.

## 7. Approve the release package, not just the video

The final package should include more than one media file:

- approved master and local backup;
- captions or transcript;
- claim ledger and source references;
- thumbnail or poster frame;
- CTA URL and owner;
- permissions record for people, customer references, music, and third-party assets;
- approved title and description;
- source project or edit decision list if revisions will continue;
- short list of scenes cleared for post-event reuse.

Ask every required reviewer to approve the same package. A product lead may approve the interface capture but not a fundraising statement. A founder may approve the narrative but not have authority over a customer logo. Make those boundaries explicit.

Use this release question: “If this exact package became public today, which statement, face, screen, asset, or destination would surprise its owner?” Investigate every answer before release.

## 8. Plan post-Demo-Day reuse before the event

Demo Day is one distribution moment. The evidence gathered for it can support follow-up only when rights, context, and version control survive the event.

Mark each scene with one of three reuse states:

- **Reusable as approved:** the claim, permission, and CTA remain current for the intended audience.
- **Reusable after review:** the scene is valuable but requires a new introduction, updated data, or different CTA.
- **Event-only:** the context, rights, or statement should not travel beyond the event.

Create a simple follow-up map: investor reply, product proof link, founder update, recruiting explanation, or launch education. Do not publish all variants automatically. Each new audience changes the context, so the owner should confirm that the approved evidence still supports the new cut.

## Final checklist for the accountable owner

Before moving from planning into production, confirm:

- [ ] One viewer and one next step are written down.
- [ ] The company description and memorable points are approved.
- [ ] Every factual claim has a source and owner.
- [ ] One product proof path has been chosen and rehearsed.
- [ ] Recording accounts, screens, and sample data are clean.
- [ ] Every production gate has one accountable owner.
- [ ] Captions and muted/mobile viewing are included in review.
- [ ] Primary and independent backup playback paths are defined.
- [ ] Permissions and third-party asset rights are recorded.
- [ ] The exact release package and CTA will receive final approval.
- [ ] Reusable scenes are labeled before the event.
- [ ] Follow-up versions will receive their own context and approval.
