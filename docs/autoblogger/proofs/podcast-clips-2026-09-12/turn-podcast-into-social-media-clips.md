To turn a B2B podcast into GTM clips, choose the campaign objective first, select three to five sharp moments, convert audio or video into captioned visual assets, approve the message before distribution, and adapt each clip for the channel owner who will publish or promote it.

## Campaign clip decision worksheet

We recommend filling this worksheet before opening an editor, because the same podcast can become social clips, email material, or a wider clip library depending on the campaign job.

- Campaign objective: ____
- Buyer moment: ____
- Clip source: audio only, recorded video, or both: ____
- Moment type: customer pain, market shift, contrarian opinion, practical tip, or founder point of view: ____
- Primary channel: LinkedIn, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, newsletter, or sales email: ____
- Asset shape: short clip, audiogram, quote graphic, or longer segment: ____
- Approval owner: ____
- Lock condition: message, guest context, and call to action approved: ____
- Distribution owner: ____
- Follow up signal to review: saves, comments, replies, meetings influenced, or sales team reuse: ____

Use a narrow rule for the first pass: if the episode supports one campaign idea, produce three clips; if it has several strong, distinct moments, produce up to five clips; keep social cuts roughly 30 to 90 seconds unless the channel owner asks for a longer segment.

Do not clip every interesting quote, because a GTM campaign needs moments that connect to a buyer belief, objection, or action.

## Recommended workflow from episode to campaign clips

Goldcast frames podcast repurposing as a pre recording strategy: define formats against marketing goals, then pull three to five moments into 30 to 90 second clips for social channels.

Y Combinator's Conveo job post treats repeatable formats, briefs, shot lists, edit templates, and channel native cutdowns as the machinery that lets each shoot keep producing assets.

1. Check prerequisites before editing: gather the final episode file, any recorded video, the transcript or notes, the campaign brief, the brand kit, and the channels where the clip will appear.

2. Write one campaign thesis in plain language, such as "mid market finance teams are outgrowing spreadsheet forecasting," then reject any moment that does not make that thesis clearer.

3. Mark six candidate timestamps, then score each one from one to three for relevance, clarity, and standalone value.

4. Choose the visual route: if video was recorded, start from the camera cut; if the episode is audio only, upload the podcast file or use an RSS based workflow to create a visual asset with captions, waveforms, speaker treatment, or supporting visuals.

5. Cut the ranked moments into three to five draft clips, and make each draft understandable without the full episode introduction.

6. Package each draft for its channel by adding a direct opening line, readable captions, a concise end card, and a call to action that fits the campaign stage.

7. Run the approval gate before anything ships: the campaign owner approves message fit, the guest owner checks context, and the distribution owner confirms the publishing destination.

8. Set the clip status to Locked only after all three owners approve, then let channel owners publish, promote, or place the clips according to the campaign plan.

9. After launch, review which clips earned useful engagement or reuse, then save the best opening lines, formats, and edit notes as the template for the next episode.

## Hypothetical worked example for a founder led campaign

In this hypothetical example, a founder of a payroll software company records a 42 minute B2B podcast with a customer about mistakes during international contractor onboarding.

The worksheet objective is pipeline education, the buyer moment is "operations leaders know expansion is coming but fear compliance errors," and the primary channels are LinkedIn, the newsletter, and a sales follow up email.

The founder marks six timestamps and keeps three because each one can stand alone: a painful manual process, a surprising compliance misconception, and a practical question buyers should ask vendors.

The editor creates three 45 to 70 second drafts with captions and a short visual treatment, while the campaign owner writes one call to action that points viewers toward the same expansion checklist.

Before the clips are locked, the campaign owner approves message fit, the customer marketing owner checks the guest context, and the demand generation owner confirms where each clip will run.

Clip one goes to LinkedIn as the pain narrative, clip two goes into the newsletter as the insight teaser, and clip three goes to sales as a conversation starter for expansion accounts.

If the guest later asks for a wording change after lock, the founder reopens only that clip, updates the edit, repeats the approval gate, and leaves the other approved clips untouched.

## Troubleshooting choices when the clip plan stalls

Problem: the episode is audio only.

Remedy: create a visual version using captions, waveforms, speaker overlays, or supporting visuals, then treat the result as a campaign clip rather than pretending it was filmed.

Problem: no moment feels strong enough for social.

Remedy: return to the campaign thesis and look for a clearer buyer question, or use the episode as newsletter material instead of forcing a weak video cut.

Problem: stakeholders keep requesting changes after approval.

Remedy: use the Locked status from the workflow, reopen only the affected clip, record the requested change, and require the same owners to approve the new version.

Problem: every channel owner wants a different version.

Remedy: keep one approved master message, then make channel native edits for placement, pacing, caption density, and call to action instead of changing the argument.

Problem: the team lacks editing capacity.

Remedy: document the brief, timestamp list, caption style, and end card once, then use templates, AI assisted workflows, freelancers, or an editor bench to repeat the system.

Problem: the clips get published but do not help the GTM motion.

Remedy: compare the follow up signals against the worksheet objective, then adjust the next episode brief before recording rather than only changing the final edit.
